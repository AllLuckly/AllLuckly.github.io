When drawing a portrait, it is easy to finish one eye in great detail before discovering that its position does not fit the face. This exercise uses a monochrome portrait turned slightly from the front. Establish the relative positions of the head and features first, then build volume with three value levels. The goal is a coherent study, without pursuing photographic effects through hair, eyelashes, or skin texture.

Choose an easy-to-observe reference

Prepare a portrait photo you have permission to use, preferably with light from one side, a calm expression, and no extreme upward or downward head tilt. You can view the reference on another device or beside the canvas; no particular split-screen entry point is required. An imagined simplified portrait is possible too, but proportions are harder to judge. First observe the widest points at the forehead, cheekbones, and jaw instead of immediately tracing the eyes.

Create a 1200×1500-pixel canvas with a light warm-gray background. Limit the colors to dark brown-gray, mid-gray, and light gray, reserving a few light areas close to the background. Use separate layers for sketch, values, and details. Make the head about 950 pixels tall, leaving some neck and shoulders below to support it instead of trapping the chin against the canvas edge.

Check the head with a centerline and horizontal landmarks

Sketch an approximately egg-shaped head outline, then mark the facial centerline. When the head turns slightly right, the centerline curves right too, and the two sides of the face differ in width. Lightly mark the brow line, nose base, and mouth, comparing actual distances in the reference instead of treating a fixed proportion as universal. Forehead height, nose length, and chin shape all contribute to individual features.

Represent the eyes initially with two short lines or small shapes, the nose with a simple wedge, and the mouth with a slightly bending line. Do not draw pupils, nostrils, or lip texture yet. Zoom out and check whether the features as a whole lean too far to one side, whether the chin is too pointed, and whether the neck joins at the correct place. Repositioning is cheapest at this stage.

Paint one connected shadow mass first

Assume light comes from the upper left. Organize the right side of the face, the area below the eye sockets, and the side of the nose into larger mid-gray regions. Treat the shadow as a connected shape rather than outlining each eye and the nose in black. The neck beneath the chin usually has a deeper occlusion shadow, but do not turn it into a complete black collar. Two gray shapes can be enough to indicate shoulder direction.

Reserve light areas on the left forehead, the illuminated cheekbone, and part of the nose bridge; they need not all connect. Let the light-shadow boundary follow the face's turns: clearer at the cheekbone and softer on the rounded cheek. Hide the sketch afterward and confirm that the head direction and light source remain readable without detailed features.

Set the eyes, nose, and mouth into the face through values

Start the eyes with the sockets and short dark upper-eyelid lines. Do not fill the whites with pure white: within the socket's shadow, they are usually only slightly lighter than their surroundings. Small dark points are sufficient for the pupils, placed consistently with the gaze. The eyes can have different detail levels, with the farther eye simplified rather than forming a perfectly symmetrical pair of symbols.

Describe the nose mainly through its side shadow, a short shadow underneath, and the light on the bridge, without drawing complete black outlines on both sides. For the mouth, establish the line between the lips first, then suggest thickness with a darker upper lip and lighter lower lip. Use small dark marks at the corners rather than extending them into conspicuous smile lines. After each feature, return to the full view and check that it still belongs to the same face.

Treat the hair as one large shape first

Block in the overall hair silhouette with dark gray, distinguishing the directions of the crown, sides, and fringe. Do not fill it strand by strand. First reserve two or three lighter masses of hair. Make parts of the hairline clear and others loose, with only a few short lines near the ears. A uniformly jagged hairline will pull attention away from the face.

Use the brow line and the area near the nose base as references for ear placement, while following the actual reference. The near ear can have one inner shadow shape; the far ear needs only an outline. Do not outline both sides of the neck equally dark. One side can merge into the background. For choosing edges, see Three-stone edge exercise; a portrait also needs areas of emphasis and restraint.

Make a final check with three value levels

Zoom out and confirm clear groupings of light, midtone, and deep shadow. Concentrate the darkest marks in the upper eyelids, nose base, mouth line, and a little of the hair rather than making every feature equally black. Reserve only a few brightest areas too, so the forehead, nose tip, lips, and chin do not all look dotted with white. If too many midtones obscure the volume, combine similar gray shapes before adding more tonal steps.

Keep the sketch layer after finishing, but hide it for export. You can use Layer opacity Temporarily reduce the detail layer's opacity and compare what the large shapes and details each contribute. If the portrait looks more convincing with the details faded, some fine facial lines need to be removed or repositioned.

Common problems and the next exercise

Why do the features look correct individually but not together? Common causes are inaccurate relative distances or a mismatch between the head outline and the direction of the features. Compare the eye spacing, nose-base-to-mouth distance, and mouth-to-chin distance before adding eyelashes. Overall placement usually matters more than local detail.

Is the portrait incomplete without skin texture? This exercise is complete when its structure and values work. Skin texture is not required and can hide problems if added too early. For the next portrait, keep the dimensions and three grays but move the light to the opposite side. Observe how the shadow regions reconnect instead of copying the previous portrait's shadow positions.
