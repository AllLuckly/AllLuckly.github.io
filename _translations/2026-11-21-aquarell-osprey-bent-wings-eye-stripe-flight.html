Build a flying osprey from an observed silhouette, bent wings and light and dark markings.

Choose a source and viewing angle

The US National Park Service’s osprey material shows a dark eye stripe, light head and underside, and wings bent at the wrists in flight. This exercise depicts one bird viewed from slightly below and to the side; it does not claim a hunt, migration or location. Find a clear reference you may use for study, decide which wing is closer to the viewer, and record its source. Do not approach nests or disturb wild birds.

Set the canvas and silhouette

Using the new-document entry actually visible in Aquarell, create an approximately 1800 × 1400 pixel landscape canvas and save an editable original. Separate sketch, sky, body, wings and detail using layers available in the installed version. If interface labels differ, use identifiable functions on the device. Draw a light body axis tilted slightly upward, place a slender torso at its center, and reserve space for head and tail. Canvas proportions are for this exercise, not real dimensions.

Locate the wrists with wing guides

Extend a wing guide from each side of the torso, linking shoulder, wrist and tip with a gently bent line. Perspective can shorten the far wing, and the near wing need not mirror it. First make sure both attach to the body instead of floating like leaves. View both guides and the torso at small size. If the wrist bends disappear unless details are visible, revise the main contour before continuing.

Complete wing edges and tail

Draw wing surfaces around the guides. First distinguish leading and trailing edges with continuous contours, then use the reference to separate a few long feathers at the outer ends. Avoid equal-length spikes for every feather or disconnected tips. Extend a slightly spread fan of tail feathers from the rear of the body. Where tail and a wing overlap, decide which is in front and remove only the hidden contour.

Place the light head and dark eye stripe

Block the head in a warm light value, extending a bill from the front and shaping its downward curve from the reference. Place a dark area through the eye toward the back of the head while keeping the eye readable. The stripe follows the head’s volume; it is not a horizontal decoration crossing the sky. At small scale, check that head and torso connect, then use a little dark value to separate bill, eye and stripe. Do not invent the hidden side.

Separate underside and wing values

Keep the underside predominantly light and place darker wing areas according to the reference’s actual orientation. A surface visible from below is not automatically the back pattern. Block one wing as a whole, then use a restrained value difference near the wrist; compare the other wing before repeating. If pale belly and sky merge, tint the sky slightly blue-gray instead of outlining the whole belly in black. Ensure the far wing remains visible.

Check flight without inventing behavior

If texture and background have been drawn on separate layers, temporarily hide those layers; otherwise use a sketch without them and inspect the silhouette: do head and tail follow the body axis, do both wings emerge from the shoulders, can each wrist be traced, and are the tips inside the canvas? If a wing appears reversed, compare its leading and trailing edges with the original reference instead of hiding the problem under feathers. If feet are obscured in the chosen view, omit them. The picture needs no fish, nest or invented hunting detail.

Compare two spatial thumbnails

On a separate sketch, try level and slightly tilted wing silhouettes while keeping the body and head unchanged. Compare negative space and the readability of the wrists. Choose the clearer arrangement and adjust the main draft. Do not treat the other thumbnail as a real pose at the same instant. If the far wing disappears behind the head, alter the composition or leave a gap, and record the reference and artistic choice.

Finish background and restrained details

Use a low-contrast sky and keep hard cloud edges away from the eye stripe and wrists so the silhouette remains readable. Add a few lines following the wing tips and tail, without letting their density overwhelm light belly and dark wing areas. When viewing mirrored or small, check that the direction of flight remains clear. If not, adjust the large shapes instead of darkening every outline.

Save a separate black-and-white silhouette and try wider and narrower wings without moving the body. Compare which version still shows the shoulder, wrist and tip connections and whether the surrounding space feels balanced. After choosing the clearer draft, compare it with the reference; composition experiments are not fixed anatomical proportions. Keep the original reference and first draft so later adjustments remain traceable.

Save and inspect the output

Save the editable original, export a separate image through the share or export entry actually visible, and reopen it to inspect the eye stripe, both wing tips and four corners. Return to the original and identify sky, body and wing layers. If a detail is absent, check visibility, crop and file location before overwriting anything. Include the bird source and reference with the artwork. Mark unperformed device, export and reopening steps as pending; do not claim a device test.
