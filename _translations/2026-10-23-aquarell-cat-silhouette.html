Without facial features or coat colors, a silhouette must communicate the pose through its outer contour. Ears, the chest curve, and gaps between hind legs and tail often convey “sitting and looking back” better than eyes. Establish the large shape first, then add fur edges in a few places instead of hiding the body structure beneath countless short lines.

Choose a pose with a readable contour

Create a 1200×1400 pixel canvas with a pale warm-gray background and a dark blue-gray cat. Practice a seated side view with the head turned slightly left: head about 260 pixels wide, chest about 300, haunches about 430, and total height about 850. Curve the tail from the lower right toward the front left, leaving a small gap through which the background remains visible.

On the sketch layer, draw overlapping head, chest, and haunch masses, followed by the tail's direction. Do not start with ear tips or whiskers. First check whether the body's weight falls between haunches and front paws. Place the head slightly forward and let the back descend gently from the neck to the haunches. Put the final silhouette, background, and ground shadow on separate layers for easier shape changes.

Join the body into one complete dark shape

Use a coverage brush around 80 to 120 pixels to fill the head, chest, and haunches, then connect the neck. Use one dark color without adding internal shading yet. Form the front legs with two similar vertical shapes, one slightly behind and only partly visible. Keep the paw bottoms fairly flat rather than making both legs pointed where they meet the ground.

After filling, hide the sketch and zoom out. If it is not recognizable as a cat, first adjust the back, chest, and head-to-body ratio. Ears and tail help recognition but cannot replace body structure. An overly thin neck can look birdlike, while very round haunches and a tiny chest can resemble a ball. Correct these large relationships before adding detail.

Use only a few turns for ears and muzzle

Add two triangular ears of slightly different sizes, with the near ear larger and only part of the far ear showing. Connect the bases naturally to the head instead of attaching two separate triangles. Let the muzzle project slightly left, with a small notch between nose and chin. A few strokes suffice; exaggeration makes it resemble a long-snouted animal.

If the head turns farther back, shorten the muzzle and bring the ears closer together. A side-view exercise does not need a pair of large frontal eyes. The silhouette can have no internal features at all. If you add one eye, use a short mark only slightly lighter than the body, not the brightest focal point.

Negative space separates legs and tail

Check for a small indentation between chest and front leg and a curved gap between tail and body. These background shapes explain how the body parts relate. Carving a gap with a small eraser on the silhouette layer is cleaner than drawing a pale line across the body. Confirm the active layer before erasing to avoid damaging the background.

Make the tail thicker at its base and gradually narrower toward its tip, with a rhythm of longer and shorter curves. Avoid an equal-width ring, and do not let its tip just touch a paw tip and create an ambiguous join. Leave a gap of around 30 to 50 pixels. For observing negative shapes, see Winter-branch negative-space exercise; here, replace the sky with the background around the cat.

Concentrate fur edges on the chest, cheeks, and tail

Switch to a small brush around 8 to 15 pixels and add a few short, pointed groups of fur below the chest, directed downward. Fur beside the face can fan slightly outward, while tail fur follows its curve. Use only three to five strokes per group with smooth contours between them. Keep most of the back line intact so the body's weight stays clear.

Do not make every fur stroke equally long or space them evenly around the whole outline. If the edge looks sawtoothed, reconnect most strokes with the subject color and retain only key tufts. Long-haired cats can have broader chest and tail variations; short-haired cats need fewer. Express the difference first in the main contour, not merely the number of hair strokes.

Support the pose with background and shadow

Keep the background lighter behind the head and add a pale-gray ground shadow below the body, extending down and right. Reserve the darkest parts for the haunches and paws touching the ground. If the background feels too flat, add a warm area with a very light large brush, but do not draw a halo around the cat. The silhouette is the subject; the background should make it easier to read.

At completion, shrink the canvas to roughly a small phone list card. Confirm that ears, muzzle, back, and tail remain clear, then enlarge it to inspect overly dense fur and any accidentally broken tail tip. For sticker use, see Exporting a transparent PNG, but keep the background here while assessing the composition.

Two common failures and further practice

What if it looks like a rabbit or fox? First check whether the ears are too long or the muzzle too pointed, then the head-to-chest ratio. Do not force the species to read correctly by adding eyes and whiskers. The value of silhouette practice is recognition from shape alone; changing the large forms is usually most effective.

The fur edges keep getting messier. Will a finer brush fix them? A finer brush adds detail but does not automatically restore the contour's rhythm. Erase back to a smooth section of the back first, retaining fur only in a few groups at the chest and tail. For the next drawing, try a curled-up pose, again starting with the large head, chest, and haunch masses, and compare how the gap between tail and body changes.

Another check is to examine only the three contacts with the ground: haunches, front paws, and tail. The haunches carry most of the weight, the paws less, and the tail simply rests nearby. Equal darkness and width at all three contacts can stiffen the pose. Shortening the shadow under the tail often makes the body's center of gravity clearer.
