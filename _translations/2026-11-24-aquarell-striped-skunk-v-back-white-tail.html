Use a permitted reference to observe white markings, short-leg connections and a bushy tail.

Choose a reference showing the back

Choose a permitted side-rear or oblique photograph of a standing striped skunk, ideally showing shoulders, white back markings and tail. The National Park Service describes a white marking that may divide into a V at the shoulders, with length and width varying between individuals; another guide notes the black-and-white tail and thin facial stripe. Draw only what the chosen photograph shows, not one individual’s pattern as a universal standard.

Set up canvas and margins

Create an approximately 1700 by 1200 landscape canvas in Aquarell, adjusting dimensions for the intended use. Separate sketch, body, white markings, tail and background using layer controls visible in the installed version; save an editable original. Place the body centrally, leaving space in front of the head and extra room behind for a raised or extended tail. Use a light guide box to ensure no part touches the canvas edge.

Block body and head

Use a low long oval for the torso and a smaller shape in front for the head, comparing the height of shoulders, back and rump. Follow the reference for muzzle direction instead of elongating the head into a red fox shape; leave individual hairs for later. Mark a light body axis and zoom out to compare head, body and tail-root proportions, correcting connections that look like three separate balls.

Place short legs and feet

Mark visible legs beneath the shoulder and rump and connect the body’s underside to the ground. Draw only visible contours of obscured far legs rather than forcing four parallel columns. Mark contact points and support direction before refining feet. Compare the heights of the visible contacts: on sloped ground they need not lie on one horizontal line. Observe whether the near leg overlaps the far leg and use different values to show depth; if grass hides toes, preserve that occlusion. Hide the background briefly to check that the body does not float and that near and far legs do not disconnect.

Draw tail root and silhouette

Start the tail at the rump and block its overall silhouette from the photograph, comparing curve, width and direction. A bushy edge may be irregular, but establish a continuous large form rather than radiating identical straight lines from the body. The tail may be raised or extended depending on the reference; ensure its root joins the rump so it does not resemble a separate cloud. Hide and restore the tail sketch to inspect its junction with the rump; close any background gap at the root before adding edge detail.

Organize white shoulder markings

On the marking layer, first locate the visible white areas extending from neck and shoulder along the back. If the chosen reference clearly shows a V, locate the fork and follow each branch toward the rear, comparing width and direction; do not invent a branch hidden by the side view. Avoid drawing detached parallel ribbons or filling the entire back white unless the photograph actually shows that pattern.

Separate facial and tail white

Observe whether the fine facial stripe is visible and how black and white fur is distributed on the tail. The facial line, back stripes and pale tail fur are distinct regions. Establish boundaries and overlaps before softening edges with a few strokes. Do not infer an unseen facial marking from the back or paint the whole tail white by default; individuals vary. Temporarily use three guide colors for back markings, pale tail fur and the light background to inspect their areas and overlaps, then restore the intended black-and-white palette.

Model dark fur

Lay dark gray or near-black on the body, using the reference lighting to distinguish shoulder, flank and legs. Black fur is not one structureless pure-black patch, but highlights should not turn the body light gray. Hide the white marking layer temporarily to check the separation of head, torso, legs and tail root. Restore it and refine boundaries without letting a pale edge cover an entire leg.

Build ground and occlusion

If the animal stands on ground in the reference, add a small contact shadow beneath the feet and let a few blades of grass overlap toe tips; establish light before shadow direction. If the background is rock or snow, use corresponding color areas from the photo rather than combining incompatible surfaces. Reduce background detail so white markings and tail edges read; adjust local background value first if pale fur disappears.

Troubleshoot and export

If back markings look pasted outside the body, revisit shoulder and rump volume and adjust how the white areas follow the body’s curve. If the tail looks like a spiky ball, reduce the uniformly radiating lines. Save the editable original and export a separate PNG or JPEG using a control visible in the installed version. Reopen it to inspect head, feet, tail tip and margins; record the reference without claiming unperformed device or print tests.

Compare two backgrounds

Duplicate the original, using a lighter ground in one and a medium-value background in the other while keeping the skunk’s structure and markings unchanged. Compare both at the same size to ensure dark legs, white back and tail edge remain readable. If an area disappears, adjust its neighboring background before changing animal markings arbitrarily. Keep editable copies and source notes, including the photograph’s view and any areas hidden by grass, for later reference.
