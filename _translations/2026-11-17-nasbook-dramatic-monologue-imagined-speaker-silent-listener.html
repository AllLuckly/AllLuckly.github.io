A poem’s I need not be its author, and an addressed you needs textual identification. Read the original Night caretaker in Book Isle, checking fictional voice, address, discouragement and self-report. Use the poetic sense of dramatic monologue without treating four lines as factual testimony or deciding a character’s guilt from implication.

Preserve title, four lines and the author boundary

Save D1 with title Night caretaker and the four original lines below. The title frames a caretaker character; connect I to the utterances rather than the real writer’s actions. Retain authorial lines, import a clear PDF/EPUB and check inspector, door, key anchors. D1 is a card label; the first line starts Keep. This is invented, not a real case or another poet’s work. For a later published poem retain author, edition and context instead of importing this fictional setup.

Night caretaker

Keep your gloves on, inspector.
I swept the ash before you came.
You need not look behind that door.
The key? I set it in the river.

Start with fictional voice and an addressed listener

Poetry Foundation describes an imagined speaker addressing a listener whose speech is not presented, usually not the reader. Use that working definition without classifying every first-person poem alike. D1’s inspector and second-person address ground an internal listener. Keep author, speaker, reader separate. Dramatic does not require stage solitude, and a short poem is not automatically lyrical autobiography. Establish formal evidence before interpreting self-revelation.

Trace address and deictic words to the listener

Inspector names the addressee; your gloves refers to that person’s gloves; before you came provides a reported arrival reference; You continues the discouragement. Connect these phrases to one listener candidate without inventing age, agency or belief. Gloves mentioned in speech are not independently seen objects. Title and address ground the scene; you does not automatically mean the person currently reading Book Isle.

Unquoted reply does not mean muteness

All four lines use one presented voice and quote no inspector reply. This does not establish inability to speak, absence of action, no questions or permanent silence. The key? may be self-questioning, topic repetition or response to an unquoted question; the text does not decide uniquely. Separate the visible lack of direct reply from hypotheses. Do not insert listener words into the source. Wider context may supply reactions, so a four-line window cannot establish only one person exists in the whole work.

Separate self-report from reader inference

The speaker claims sweeping ash, placing a key in the river and discourages looking behind a door. Possible interpretations include directing attention, explaining actions or evading inspection. None proves lying, crime or hidden contents. These are self-reports, not independently certified by an omniscient narrator. Attach wording to each inference and consider alternatives, such as ordinary storage behind the door. Suspicion may remain, with limits; suspense is not a verdict.

Need not means no necessity, not must not prohibition or cannot inability. Discouraging inspection can guide attention, but these lines do not state whether the listener eventually looks. Preserve modal scope instead of strengthening it into you must never open the door for suspense.

Use controls rather than classify every I

Control L says I watched the moon and named my grief, with no explicit listener cue; first person alone does not give D1’s reasoning. A third-person account of a caretaker directing the inspector elsewhere changes direct presentation of voice. Adding Inspector: Where is the key? as a present directly quoted turn introduces a second explicit voice and needs renewed dialogue analysis. This differs from a possible unquoted question in D1. Keep rewrites labelled controls, not additions to the poem.

Separate voice evidence and conjecture in Book Isle

Highlight address/second person separately from self-reports, and keep unresolved inferences in notes; use line locations externally if needed. At the last line verify the original question mark and any invented reply. Author-speaker confusion calls for revisiting fiction/title; listener muteness calls for distinguishing presentation from ability. Reread all lines after corrections rather than search key alone. Leave unperformed marking/reopening pending; notes do not detect true motives.

Reopen and deliver roles with four-line evidence

Reopen D1 and distinguish author, fictional speaker and internal listener, then locate address, time reference and absent direct reply. Preserve at least two readings of The key? and unknown door contents, and explain why L’s I alone is insufficient. Deliver poem, definition scope, line ledger and actual observations; leave reopening blank if unperformed. Longer poems need continuing voice/listener checks, not this model substituted for full reading or fictional speech recorded as real behavior.
