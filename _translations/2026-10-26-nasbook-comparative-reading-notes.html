When two books discuss the same topic, it is easy to remember opposite conclusions while forgetting their subjects and conditions. Organise a comparison around one small question. Keep each book’s extracts and notes in Book Isle, then compare them in a separate document. You create the comparison rather than relying on automatic judgement, and a high highlight count does not establish completed research.

Choose a question that supports comparison

Narrow the topic, for example to how much flexibility a personal plan should retain, rather than comparing two productivity books generally. Record each author, translator, edition and intended audience, then find a passage directly addressing the question. Translation and chapter differences matter; establish sources before ending up with two unattributed screenshots.

Read the surrounding paragraphs to establish whether an author offers a general principle, describes an exception or reports another person’s opinion. Apparently opposing sentences may address beginners and experts, or short-term and long-term planning. Without these boundaries, even a tidy comparison can put answers to different questions beside each other.

Save the minimum useful extract from the first book

Where text selection is supported, choose a short extract that retains its assumptions and add your own explanation. Highlighting a whole chapter merely postpones the reading work. Structure a brief note around conditions, the author’s recommendation and your questions, keeping quotation and personal analysis recognisably separate.

Return through the saved note and confirm the chapter and paragraph. A scanned PDF or image document may not support equivalent selection. Record page numbers and a short phrase in your comparison text instead. Lack of a text layer is not missing notes, and the original file need not be changed for this comparison.

Find the corresponding idea in the second book

Use the title, contents or text search to locate relevant material, then read the context before extracting it. Authors may use different terms for a similar concept, so identical search words can miss the true counterpart. Define the idea, try related expressions and record the terms used. An empty search does not prove the second book never discusses the issue.

Use the same note structure for conditions, recommendations and questions while preserving the second source independently. Do not remove a crucial qualification just to make both extracts equally long. If one book gives a broad direction and the other a concrete procedure, retain that difference rather than forcing both into identical-looking conclusions.

Organise the comparison in a separate document

Create three columns or three sections: the first book’s view, the second book’s view and your comparison. Attach the title, chapter and short phrase to each, adding full edition details for formal citation when needed. Use your normal editing tool. Book Isle notes remain the routes back to the originals, without requiring every detail to be duplicated in both places.

First ask whether the authors discuss the same subjects, then compare time scales, evidence and conditions. Separate shared recommendations from differences in implementation. Explain the disagreement before judging who is right. Mark unsupported ideas as questions to investigate so they cannot later be mistaken for conclusions stated in either book.

Test the difference with one practical example

Apply both recommendations to the same life or work scenario and describe the actions each would suggest. Label this as your own reasoning, not an event the author actually carried out. If a recommendation does not fit, check whether the source already excludes that situation rather than declaring the whole book wrong from one personal example.

Choose another example with one changed condition and see whether the outcome reverses. This can reveal a disagreement rooted in context. Retain both source links instead of only a merged personal summary; later reading may require revisiting your original understanding and omissions.

Check exported records and attribution

Export notes or highlights through the currently available flow, choose a known folder and reopen the result. Search for one distinctive phrase from each book and confirm that quotation, personal annotation and title remain clear. Format changes can alter appearance, but missing attribution still needs attention; supplement the comparison document where necessary.

A readable export is not a complete restoration backup and does not automatically include both original books. Preserve the files separately and record any later edition changes. Avoid overwriting every dated output with the same name. Keeping important comparison versions allows you to trace how your reasoning changed after discovering a misunderstanding.

Return to the passages a week later

After a few days, read your comparison first, then revisit both originals through the notes. Correct omitted conditions, examples mistaken for conclusions or confusion between author and translator commentary, and record the reason. Revision preserves traceable evidence and prevents a polished summary from carrying an inaccurate interpretation forward.

Completion means explaining each author’s view and assumptions independently, then stating the basis for your own choice. Leave genuinely incomparable parts unresolved instead of forcing a single answer. When adding a third book, use the same record structure so the new material extends the question instead of creating an unattributed collection of extracts.
