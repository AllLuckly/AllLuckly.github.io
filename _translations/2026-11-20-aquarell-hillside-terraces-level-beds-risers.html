Paint an imaginary terraced hillside, distinguishing relatively level cultivated beds from the slopes connecting tiers before adding crop bands. FAO material on sloping land explains contour direction, level beds and riser protection. Terrace count, planting and colors here are composition choices; the image cannot establish the engineering stability or agricultural results of a real site.

Place the hillside and main tiers

On an approximately 2000 × 1400 landscape canvas sketch a hillside descending from upper left to lower right, with sky above and near ground below. Mark four gently curving bands following the slope. Their edges need not be horizontal on the page: a relatively level real bed can appear diagonal in perspective. Separate sky, distant hills, beds, risers, crops and sketch into layers and save. If the reduced image resembles parallel paper strips, first adjust curves and widths rather than adding colors.

Enclose a bed between upper and lower edges

Choose a middle tier and use two roughly corresponding curves to enclose its bed. It may appear wider near the viewer and narrower farther away. Keep the bed continuous instead of breaking it abruptly into disconnected shapes. Connect an end to the hillside or beyond the frame so it does not float like a green ribbon. Viewpoint determines page-space curves and width. Pixel separation is not real field width; do not force identical vertical distances between every edge in perspective.

Connect adjacent beds with risers

Below each bed's front edge draw a riser connecting it to the back of the next lower bed. Use a warmer, slightly darker earth color against the lighter bed, sharing one boundary rather than leaving unexplained white gaps. A riser need not be a vertical wall, but the scene's connections must remain coherent. If the terraces look like four floating bands, hide the crops and trace each front edge to the next bed; repair missing junctions before restoring details. Temporarily label upper bed, riser and lower bed on the sketch, then hide the labels after checking.

Handle turns and overlap on the hillside

Let tiers bend gradually around the same hillside, with nearby tiers hiding parts of distant edges. Each terrace boundary should follow its slope instead of suddenly curving the opposite way around the back. Nearby vegetation can obscure a little riser, but should not fill every slope. Use two temporary sketch colors for near and distant tiers, verify overlap, then replace them with the final palette. The temporary markings help inspection and do not represent separate locations.

Place crop rows along each band

Suggest crops with low-contrast short strokes following each bed's own direction. Draw a few spaced rows nearby and simplify distant rows into bands; do not make every row run vertically from summit to bottom. Small darker green patches can suggest protective riser vegetation, but the image is not a construction or planting prescription. If crop marks obscure the bed boundary, reduce them and recover clear front edges between tiers.

In a separate copy hide all crops and check that four beds and risers still read. Restore crops and compare whether detail reinforces direction instead of inventing new outlines. If a row crosses a riser into the next tier, erase the excess and stop it inside its own boundary. Do not blur the whole picture to hide crossings: the reduced image may simply become one green mass again.

Use light and temperature for depth

Choose upper-left light: brighten lit beds and darken the downward-facing riser planes somewhat. Use warmer greens and earth nearby, reducing saturation and shifting slightly cooler in the distance. Tiers need not alternate mechanically light and dark; each bed and its connecting riser need readable boundaries. If sky and ridge merge, adjust background value or ridge shape rather than outlining every tier in heavy black. Color indicates pictorial depth, not moisture, ripeness or season.

Reduce the view and repair one broken edge

Fit the image to the window and identify one bed's upper edge, front edge and adjacent riser, following the same tier through the hillside turn. If the path is lost, check whether a distant tier incorrectly covers it. Zoom in to repair one break, then reduce again to see whether the tier is restored. Keep separate drafts before and after; do not change all colors and outlines at once, or the source of improvement is unclear. If no irrigation channel was designed, do not add a random blue line as an explanation of drainage.

Save and state the illustration's scope

Save the native draft, export through an entry actually available in the installed version and open the image elsewhere to inspect edges and depth. Reopen the original and hide only crops to check that beds and risers remain editable. If the background disappears too, inspect the selected group and file first. Describe an imaginary hillside with simplified tiers; mark unperformed export, reopening or device steps pending. This is drawing practice, not terrace construction dimensions or an assurance that any real slope is safe.
