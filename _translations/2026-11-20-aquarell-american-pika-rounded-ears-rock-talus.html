Paint an American pika resting in side view on a rock. Its short rounded ears, compact body and lack of a visible tail work together as a silhouette. The U.S. National Park Service describes the rounded ears, tail that is not visible and rocky habitat with gaps. This exercise's pose, colors and stone placement are drawing choices; the picture cannot establish an individual's age, activity or exact proportions.

Set the frame and animal silhouette

Create an approximately 1800 × 1400 landscape canvas, leaving breathing room above and to the right of the animal. Sketch a central rock in light gray, then place a slightly elongated body oval on it, with the head pointing right and a little above the back. Use separate sketch, animal, rock and background layers and save an editable draft. Dimensions organize brushwork rather than specify the animal's size. If the reduced picture becomes one gray patch, adjust value separation from the background before adding fur.

Use two short rounded ears to show direction

Place the near rounded ear on the head, then draw the farther ear partly obscured by the crown; attach both roots to the head rather than floating them outside the outline. The two need not look identical: less of the far ear may show in a side view. Lay gray-brown around the ear, darken the inside slightly and leave a narrow light edge. Do not stretch them into a hare's long ears or ring them as independent black discs. Temporarily hide details and check that ear roots meet the crown.

Connect face, shoulder and back as one form

Connect a smaller rounded head to the shoulder. Let the back slope gently toward the rear and meet the shorter belly line. Give the nose a small, clear dark mark and put the eye within the profile instead of on its edge; retain a short dip where the chin joins the body. Study the reference's head-to-body relationship first: the sketch oval is a construction aid, not exact anatomy. If the head seems pasted on like a second animal, check for an overly narrow neck and join it with a shoulder color plane rather than adding whiskers.

Use overlap for feet and rock contact

Establish the body resting on the rock before adding the visible near forefoot and hind foot. Other limbs may be hidden by body or rock; do not force four feet into a row when the reference does not show them. Stop the upper rock edge behind the body, and let the foot hide the part it covers. Add a narrow contact shadow instead of an identical black line along the entire belly. If the animal floats, first join the contour and contact point, then adjust the shadow rather than darkening the whole rock.

Block gray-brown fur before adding short strokes

Fill the body with middle gray-brown, slightly warmer on the back and lighter below, using a transition color between head and shoulder. Choose a brush size suitable for short marks; add a few along the contour in the body's direction and then selected groups on shoulder and back, without filling every space. Fur varies with light, season and individual; this gray-brown is one example. If marks divide the body into stripes, lower contrast and reconnect it with broad color. Keep nose and eye legible instead of covering them with fur strokes.

Give each rock a direction and a gap

Add two or three differently sized rocks around the main one, using straighter angled planes to distinguish hard surfaces from soft fur. Choose one light direction: lighter faces toward it, middle gray on side planes and darker small faces below. Leave irregular narrow dark gaps, but do not connect every crevice into a black grid. The stone under the pika needs a stable contact edge. If using a photograph for environmental reference, redesign the stone arrangement rather than copy the full composition of a copyrighted photograph.

Place a distant stone behind the body and let a foreground stone overlap only a small part of the main stone's edge; inspect the resulting depth order. If the foreground stone hides the entire forefoot, move it first. If a dark crevice behind the mouth appears to continue the whiskers, move that crevice. These gaps depict environment; they do not establish that the illustrated animal has a burrow or food cache in this scene.

Hide textures and check recognition

Turn off fur texture, rock texture and highlights in turn, leaving animal, rocks and background as color shapes. Can the ears still be distinguished from rock edges, and can you read head direction, back and forefoot? If the outline disappears, separate neighboring values slightly or shorten a stone line beside the ear instead of uniformly outlining the whole animal in white. Do not add a long tail line. A source saying the tail is not visible does not prove that a real individual has no tail tissue. Restore textures one layer at a time, keeping rock marks off the animal layer.

Export and record what was actually completed

Save the editable draft. Use an actually available share or export control in the installed version to output an image, then open it elsewhere and inspect ear roots, foot contact and the frame edges. Reopen the original and hide only the animal layer to see that rocks do not disappear too. If export differs, check the selected file and hidden layers before exporting again. Deliver the exercise and reference link; mark any unperformed export or reopening as pending. This is manual drawing, not a claim that Aquarell identifies species or generates poses; the illustration is not field-monitoring evidence.
