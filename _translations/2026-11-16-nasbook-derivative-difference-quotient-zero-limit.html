Write both function values in the numerator

Write the quotient as (f(3+h)−f(3))/h with h≠0. Its numerator is the difference of two outputs, not f(h), and 3+h does not replace the denominator. Pair each input with its output in an external note. If highlighting is available, select the complete fraction rather than an isolated h. This square function exists on both sides, so h can approach through positive/negative values. A different domain requires a new check before reusing this two-sided setup.

Expand and cancel without erasing the condition

Expand (3+h)² to 9+6h+h², subtract 9 and factor h(6+h). Only h≠0 permits cancellation to 6+h; the original quotient is still undefined at zero. Keep original and simplified forms with the condition between them, rather than claiming the original 0/0 was evaluated as six. A missing 6 h requires restoring both 3 h cross terms, not merely recoloring an incorrect expansion.

Substitute finite increments before taking the limit

Use h=0.1,−0.1 to obtain 6.1, 5.9, then h=0.01,−0.01 to obtain 6.01, 5.99. None is the exact derivative, though both sides approach six. The conclusion follows because 6+h tends to 6 as h tends to zero, not from selecting a smallest decimal or defining derivatives by averaging four samples. Keep signs/decimal places:−0.1 is not−1. This is a real-number model, not a measured physical velocity.

For a distant control use h=−6: inputs 3/−3 both give nine and quotient zero. This does not refute derivative six at 3; the secant spans a large interval rather than a zero-increment limit. Keep it separate from the four small increments to distinguish one zero quotient from a zero derivative.
