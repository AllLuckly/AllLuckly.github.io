A change from clear air to mist does not let a percentage alone prove more total water. Read an original constant-volume cooling model in ManYue, tracking same-temperature saturation reference, vapour and liquid. Table densities are teaching assumptions, not weather observations for a city or date, and no in-app humidity sensing is assumed.

State the closed fixed volume and stipulated table

Externally draw a fixed 1m³ space initially containing 6g water vapour and no droplets, with no water entering or leaving. Define decreasing temperature positions TA>TB>TC with saturation vapour densities 10,6,4g/m³, without assigning real Celsius values. Assume phase equilibrium and conditions allowing condensation, setting supersaturation aside. Make P1 conditions, P2 ledger and P3 controls as a PDF. After import check headers/markers and bookmark P2; temperature letters are not measured weather stations.

Use a same-temperature reference for the relative quantity

RH is normally actual vapour pressure divided by saturation vapour pressure at the same temperature, times 100%. For approximately ideal vapour here, pressure and density share the same temperature factor within each row, permitting actual-density/saturation-density. This cancellation does not freely mix pressure and mass across temperatures. Retain a temperature label per row rather than divide TA’s actual density by TC’s reference without a state-change account. Vapour means gaseous water, excluding visible liquid droplets.

Check the first panel as six divided by ten

At TA, 6g in 1m³ gives vapour density 6g/m³. Divide by saturation 10 for RH=60%. The remaining forty percentage points describe a same-temperature ratio gap, not forty percent empty space or capacity for another 40g. Molecular composition and geometric empty space are not determined by RH. Keep 6g and 60% in separate columns—sample mass versus relative ratio. No sensor reading is supplied; retain unperformed or unmeasured observation fields.

closed volume=1 m^3; total water=6 g
TA: rho_sat=10 g/m^3; vapour=6 g; liquid=0 g; RH=60%
TB: rho_sat=6 g/m^3; vapour=6 g; liquid=0 g; RH=100%
TC: rho_sat=4 g/m^3; vapour=4 g; liquid=2 g; RH=100%
separate D: rho_sat=20 g/m^3; rho_vapour=8 g/m^3; RH=40%

Reach saturation in the second panel without adding water

Cooling TA to TB leaves 6g vapour before condensation in the same 1m³, while the stipulated saturation density becomes 6g/m³. RH reaches 100% without added water because the temperature-dependent denominator changed. TB is the saturation point of this constant-volume model, not a real meteorological dew-point value in unspecified Celsius degrees. Visible fog is not mandatory, and 100% RH does not automatically prove rain. Condensation, cloud droplets and precipitation have further conditions; a percentage cannot supply an entire weather story.

Account separately for vapour and liquid on further cooling

At TC under the stipulated equilibrium, retain 4g vapour and condense 2g liquid, totaling 6g. RH stays 100%, not old 6/4=150% as the equilibrium answer. Supersaturation belongs to different conditions; this ideal ledger is not every real cloud’s instantaneous state. Visibility depends on droplet sizes/distribution. Draw representative droplets without making their count equal two grams. Removing liquid would change the closed-water condition and require a new ledger.

Lower relative humidity may accompany more vapour

Use a separate D state with saturation density 20g/m³ and actual vapour density 8g/m³: RH=40%. D has more vapour per volume than TA’s 6g/m³ but lower RH than sixty, so cross-temperature percentages cannot rank vapour content alone. D is an independent sample, not a next frame in the closed space with magically added water. Choose a consistent content metric and units first. RH alone also cannot fully predict human heat discomfort; it does not replace temperature and other conditions.

For a separate 2m³ sample with the same densities, TA needs 12g vapour; TC equilibrium gives 8g vapour plus 4g liquid. RH values remain 60%,100%,100%. Percentages alone cannot determine grams without volume and state. Visible white fog consists of droplets, not invisible vapour itself painted white, so retain separate gas/liquid ledgers.

Troubleshoot vapour identity and denominator temperature

For 150% at TC, check the stipulated condensation/equilibrium. A total of 4g may delete 2g liquid; 60% at TB may retain TA’s denominator ten. If blue dots mean both molecules and droplets, revise the external legend before counting, rather than infer phase from shade. Repair one row and return to its temperature/units. Without a saturation reference, RH may be unknown; this hypothetical table does not replace real meteorological data.

Reopen and report both percentage and phase masses

Reopen with answers hidden and predict 60%,100%,100%, then vapour/liquid grams 6/0,6/0,4/2. Return to assumptions to explain why the percentages do not imply three different total-water masses. Deliver the stipulated table, same-temperature ratios and closed mass ledger, leaving unperformed bookmark/reopen actions pending. This tests conditional reading, not ManYue humidity measurement, forecasting, observed fog or a proof that supersaturation never occurs in nature.
