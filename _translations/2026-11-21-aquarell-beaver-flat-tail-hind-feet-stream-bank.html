Use public North American beaver references to draw a brown body, flat tail and visible limbs at the shore.

Choose a reference with a visible tail

The US National Park Service describes North American beavers as having brown fur, a flat scaly tail, webbed hind feet and orange incisors. For this exercise choose a side-view shoreline reference that clearly shows the tail joining the body. Water or plants may hide some features, and the picture need not display all of them at once. Record the reference and permitted use; do not approach, feed or disturb wildlife. The pose and background are an art exercise, not a reported sighting.

Set a landscape canvas and ground

Create an approximately 1800 × 1400 pixel landscape canvas using the current Aquarell interface and save the editable original. Separate sketch, shore and water, body, tail and details using available layers; if labels differ, use identifiable controls on the device. Lightly divide water from bank, then place an elongated body oval with head facing one side and room for the tail on the other. Canvas dimensions guide composition, not actual animal size.

Connect the head and stocky body

Place a smaller head at the front and connect muzzle, forehead and neck in a continuous contour from the reference, avoiding a separate ball attached to the oval. Curve the back smoothly toward the rear and leave the visible gap or contact between belly and bank. Before drawing fur, reduce the image to check head direction and the locations of torso and tail root. If it reads as only a brown oval, revise head-neck and body contours first.

Draw the flat tail’s root and direction

Extend the tail naturally from the rear of the body. Use a center line for direction, then outline a broad flat form. A flat tail can look narrow from the side and wider from above; if the photograph shows only part of it, draw only what is visible rather than forcing a complete paddle shape. Keep its root connected to the body. If it enters water, use the waterline or occlusion to distinguish exposed from submerged areas.

Handle front paws and webbed hind feet

Mark visible front and hind feet from the reference, locating where each leg leaves the body before outlining contact with the bank. The webbing in the source belongs to hind feet. If a hind foot is hidden by body, plants or water, do not turn a front paw into a webbed foot to display the fact. Where a hind foot is visible, a few connections can suggest the toes without creating an unrelated fan. Let near limbs occlude far limbs and retain convincing contact with the ground.

Place values and selective details

Block the body in dark and middle browns while keeping back, belly and face distinguishable as shapes. Use a flatter dark area to separate tail from fur. Orange incisors are a documented feature, but if the reference shows a closed mouth or hidden teeth, do not add a bright orange rectangle. Keep muzzle and eye readable at small size, then add a few short strokes following the body volume rather than covering the animal in dense parallel lines.

Connect water, bank and body

Use muted blue-gray water and neutral brown-green bank. Decide whether the body is on shore, partly submerged or afloat, then let the waterline occlude the appropriate area. Water highlights run roughly horizontally and should not cross parts on land. Reduce visibility of a submerged tail and preserve the waterline; if it rests on shore, use a local contact shadow. Background branches are environmental elements and do not imply the animal is gnawing.

Compare tail views in two thumbnails

Save two small variants with a narrower and a wider-looking tail, noting a side or higher viewing angle beside each. Compare the tail root’s connection to the body and the water-bank occlusion, then continue with the one matching the selected reference. Do not interpret the projections as two species or two tails at once. If the flat tail looks like a fish fin, check that it continues from the rear of the torso and remove unnecessary points.

Review visible and hidden parts as a silhouette

If fur, water reflections and background vegetation have been drawn on separate layers, temporarily hide those layers; otherwise use a sketch without those details, leaving the large shapes of head, torso, visible limbs and tail. Check that the eye lies on the head rather than the neck, feet emerge in the right places, and the tail root is connected. Mark hidden areas “not visible” in your working notes rather than inventing anatomy. If the silhouette is unclear, adjust the value difference between bank and body before restoring restrained details. Record the most useful before-and-after change for another study.

Before finishing, review the shoreline: a foot on land should meet the bank, while water should occlude the part of a tail that enters it. If those relationships conflict, decide on the pose and viewing angle before changing a small color area.

Save and recheck the output

Save the editable original, then use the share or export entry visible in the current version to save a separate image. Reopen it and inspect head, tail tip, visible feet and four canvas corners; return to identify shore, body and tail layers in the original. If the tail or color is missing, check crop, layer visibility and location before overwriting the original. Include the public animal source and reference, and mark device, export and reopening steps as pending if unperformed.
