Repetition may return within one sentence or link its ending to the next beginning; position changes the evidence. Read an original three-sentence passage in Book Isle, testing anadiplosis before interpreting its two shifts of attention. The reader establishes the device from text; search counts are not automatic rhetoric detection.

Preserve three sentences, not three screen lines

Use original card A below, with full stops defining three sentences. S1–S3 are reference labels excluded from the text. Keep B/C controls separately, exporting text-preserving PDF or clearly divided EPUB and retaining editable originals. Verify each ending and following beginning and bookmark A. Wrapping one sentence into three screen lines creates no sentence boundary. Even in one paragraph, use the stipulated full stops rather than cut authorial sentences to manufacture links.

A
S1: I kept the light.
S2: The light marked the gate.
S3: The gate faced the road.

B
S1: I kept the light.
S2: A lamp marked the gate.
S3: A road crossed the hill.

C
S1: Light guided me.
S2: Light warmed me.
S3: Light stayed.

Use the previous ending and following beginning

Silva Rhetoricae defines anadiplosis through the previous line, clause or sentence’s final word/phrase repeated at the next beginning. Here use complete sentences and matches of two whole words. Record previous end, next beginning and exact match, not merely repeated tokens anywhere. Intervening sentence punctuation is compatible with this cross-unit device, but a return several sentences later fails the current adjacency test. If another work uses authorial lines, declare that unit before comparing rather than mix sentence boundaries with screen rows.

Number A’s words to recover two links

Excluding labels, A has sentence lengths four, five, five, fourteen words total. S1’s final the light occupies global positions 3/4, S2’s initial The light 5/6. S2’s final the gate is 8/9, S3’s initial The gate 10/11. There are two links, not three, because three sentences have two given adjacent boundaries. Ignore only sentence-initial case and final punctuation for matching while preserving the original. If highlighting is available, give each bridge a separate identifier; do not retain only half a phrase split across a page.

Separate B’s related meanings from C’s repeated openings

B’s light and lamp may refer to related things, but S1’s the light does not match S2’s A lamp, nor S2’s the gate S3’s A road. Neither bridge holds. C repeatedly begins Light without a preceding sentence ending Light, giving a different opening-repetition structure. Shared topic, scene or three occurrences of Light cannot replace end-to-start positions. A failed structural test does not mean meaningless or poor writing; it answers a defined form question, not overall quality.

Fix match length before comparing variants

Changing S2 to Light marked the gate preserves a one-word light bridge but loses the fixed two-word the light match. Record one-word linkage retained, two-word test failed, rather than declare every form of anadiplosis absent. Lights marked also changes exact word form and cannot be silently lemmatized. Keep source, current rule and possible alternative granularity separate to explain different judgments. Do not remove the internal space and count the light as one word.

Interpret the shift of attention after establishing form

A moves from a speaker retaining light to light marking a gate, then the gate facing a road. Repetition promotes a previous ending into the next topic. A supported reading moves attention from nearby objects toward an outward path, citing marked and faced. It proves neither a character’s actual departure nor a fixed causal chain among objects. Linkage may feel coherent or insistent depending on context. Keep form and proposed effects separate, without claiming a universal reader emotion.

A has two light and two gate occurrences, but counts alone do not locate adjacent sentence endings/beginnings. Return to S1–S3 and extract each bridge.
