Missing “only,” “if” or “if and only if” can change a definition. Preserve the source and context in Book Isle, then check directions manually. The example is an invented card classification, not a medical, legal or real-world admission rule. Use reading, references and available annotations without assuming automatic proofs or correct counterexamples.

Preserve the definition’s full domain

Choose a short completed textbook definition and record edition, section, term, domain and qualifications. A finite-set scope cannot be tested against arbitrary objects. Separate quotation from paraphrase and preserve usually or possibly. Learn the method with the invented rule below before applying unfamiliar subject knowledge. Distinguish definition, empirical generalisation and local convention; observing that most cases fit does not establish logical necessity.

Build a harmless invented rule and sample

Define a qualified card in this exercise if and only if it has both a number and a circle mark; these are graphic attributes, not real passes. A has both; B has a number but only a triangle; C has a circle but no number; D has neither. Only A qualifies, and colour and size are irrelevant. State the rule before the sample rather than making readers guess. Version changed rules instead of silently adding conditions while discussing the old sample.

Express necessity as what cannot be absent

The definition implies that a qualified card has a number, making a number necessary; a circle is also necessary. B has a number but fails, so a number alone is insufficient. Write both facts so necessary is not read as enough. Necessity asks whether a qualifying card can lack the attribute, not whether any numbered card exists. Here necessity follows from the stated definition, not merely the four observations; those observations cannot establish it under unknown rules.

Express sufficiency as what establishes the result

Having both a number and a circle suffices under the rule. C shows that a circle alone is insufficient, while B shows that a number alone is insufficient. Two individually necessary attributes are not automatically jointly sufficient in every definition. The present biconditional supplies the reverse guarantee. If a source only says qualification requires both, without saying both establish qualification, retain a one-way relation because other requirements may exist.

Rewrite directions and check permission to reverse

Write qualification-implies-both and both-imply-qualification separately, citing support for each. “If a condition, then a result” initially supplies that direction, not its converse. “Only if” identifies a requirement without automatically making it sufficient. Resolve ambiguous natural language through context and author examples rather than treating one word as a proof. Your arrows are manual aids, not app-computed relations; read each back against the source to catch reversed direction.

Make a counterexample satisfy the challenged premise

To refute “number implies qualified,” use numbered but unqualified B; unnumbered D does not challenge it. C refutes “circle implies qualified.” Keep domain and rule version fixed instead of changing definitions or importing real cards. One valid counterexample refutes the corresponding universal claim, but failing to find one is not proof. For a textbook case, log true premise, false conclusion and support, leaving uncertain existence or properties unresolved rather than promoting an imagined case to evidence.

Keep source definition beside personal derivation

Keep source location, directional paraphrases, four-card results and counterexample identities together in supported notes or externally, labelling invented material. Keep a usable bookmark at the original passage, leave the reader and return to read its qualifiers, checking that your note has not changed “and” into “or.” Plain prose suffices without a special logic editor. Recheck negation and attribute matching after export. Reinspect scope after edition changes instead of transferring derivations merely because the term matches.

Finish the directional check and explain errors

Finish by explaining why a number is necessary but insufficient, why number-plus-circle suffices here, and why B is the right counterexample. Revisit B for the claim that all numbered cards qualify, or the premise when D is offered. Preserve domain and all conditions in harder textbook cases rather than substituting this example for subject-specific derivation. Separate stated, derived and unresolved conclusions so notes do not strengthen the source. Success is justified direction, not memorised terminology.
