The minus one in inverse-function notation does not mean putting the function value in a denominator. Read original cards in Book Isle, stating input/output sets before solving the reversed relation and both compositions. Check reversibility and the conditions requiring domain restrictions. The reader performs the algebra without automatic inversion or exhaustive real-number checking in the app.

Attach input and output ranges

Make original card A, f(x)=2*x+3, with all real inputs and all real outputs. Record source, location and set declarations rather than only the formula. Four maps to eleven here; this is not a universal mapping. Open a readable TXT, EPUB or PDF and keep external notes if needed, without assuming graphing or arrow-editing controls. Preserve the source for comparison.

State the reverse task as recovering an input

Given output y, solve y equals two x plus three by subtracting three and dividing by two. Name g(y)=(y-3)/2, whose inputs are the original outputs. Renaming y to x does not apply f again. Eleven maps back to four, reversing the selected arrow. This numerical pair checks one case; symbolic identities below verify the declared full range.

Check both composition directions

Pass f into g: subtract three from two x plus three and divide by two to recover x. Pass g into f: double the whole quotient and add three, again recovering x. Preserve brackets and intermediates so “subtract then divide” is not misread as subtracting three-halves. Interpret each identity on its permitted set. One successful direction at one number does not validate every candidate. Here algebra and sets provide the proof, not search-hit counts.

Compare the reciprocal at the same argument

Define reciprocal control h(x)=1/(2*x+3), not the inverse. At the same argument eleven, g gives four and h one twenty-fifth. h excludes minus three-halves, where its denominator vanishes; g permits all reals here. Separate the notations instead of merging them because both involve one or a minus sign. Read the source definition for inverse notation and explicit fractions or wording for reciprocals, rather than infer meaning from superscript placement alone.

Find the square function’s nonunique reverse

For B, square all real inputs. Both two and minus two map to four, so reversing four has two candidates. The positive square root is not the inverse of unrestricted squaring. The original remains a valid function; one-to-one correspondence fails. Do not silently delete minus two to force one answer. Preserve both arrows to show why reversing a relation need not yield an inverse function on that range.

Restrict the domain before using the principal root

Save a restricted B permitting nonnegative inputs, with nonnegative outputs. The principal square root now reverses it: four returns two, not excluded minus two. Zero remains allowed; nonnegative is not strictly positive. Squaring then taking the principal root recovers an allowed input. Out-of-domain negatives do not refute this restricted claim. Domain is part of the function, and this does not imply every noninjective function becomes invertible after arbitrarily deleting one point.

Check brackets and out-of-domain trials

If g at eleven becomes nine point five, check quotient brackets. If h becomes four, revisit its denominator. A negative result on restricted B may mean the old unrestricted card was reopened; verify identity rather than alter the answer. Enlarge unclear signs and fraction bars and retain uncertainty. Reopen with sets and two-way arrows

Reopen and identify A and restricted B, reconstruct four→eleven→four and compare g/h at eleven: four versus one twenty-fifth.
