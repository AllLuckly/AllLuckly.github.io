Observe Half Dome’s rounded summit and steep face in a permitted photograph and build foreground and background valley layers.

Choose a usable photograph

The US National Park Service explains that exfoliation joints helped round Half Dome, while its steep face formed along a near-vertical joint and glaciers and rockfalls also contributed. It was not simply cut in half in one event; more than half remains. Choose a permitted photograph showing both rounded top and steep face, recording direction, source and usage rights. This is an observational painting, not a timeline inferred from the image.

Arrange the canvas and large areas

Create an approximately 1800 by 1400 landscape canvas in the current Aquarell interface and save an editable original; the dimensions are suggestions. Give Half Dome the main upper area and reserve space below for valley and foreground trees. Leave clear sky above the summit. Use available layers for sketch, distant mountains, main rock, foreground and sky, following actual device labels. Lightly mark summit, steep face and valley floor first.

Check the rock mass in a box

Draw a box around the visible form and compare distances from the summit to the steep edge, foot and sides. The photograph determines which side the steep face occupies; do not force a perfect semicircle or vertical slice because of the name. Use two simplified masses for rounded top and steep face and inspect their connection. If the subject looks like an isolated geometric symbol at thumbnail size, revise contour and foothill occlusion against the photo.

Connect the rounded top and steep face

Follow the reference’s continuous summit contour, observing its turn toward the steep face without requiring a mathematical circular arc. Block the face as a comparatively straight large mass and introduce a few observed variations; it is not a uniformly flat board. Avoid drawing a huge gap between planes or inventing evenly spaced decorative exfoliation lines. Establish the light-dark boundary before details.

Build valley occlusion

Place nearer slopes or tree crowns in front of the main rock and distant valley layers behind, following the photo. Stop a hidden rock contour at the trees rather than drawing it through them. If the original lacks a clear foreground tree, a simplified silhouette may be added as a composition choice without claiming it represents actual vegetation at a particular location. Hide the tree layer to inspect the rock mass, then restore the occlusion.

Separate rock planes with three values

Choose the main light direction from the photograph. Use a warm pale gray for the illuminated dome, mid-gray for a turning plane and deeper gray for a shaded face or groove. Actual value relationships depend on light, so the steep face is not always darkest. Start with three large shapes, verify depth, then add local warm and cool variations. If the boundary resembles a ruler-straight black line, soften selected transitions while retaining edges that are clear in the reference.

Simplify rock texture

Observe visible fissures, shadows and value changes and select only a few directional marks or shapes. Texture should support the planes and lighting rather than continue through sky into trees. NPS explains how different joints shape landforms, but a distant photo does not establish the origin of every visible line; do not label every stroke as a real geological structure. With texture hidden, the dome and steep face should still read.

Use value and color to suggest distance

Keep distant mountains somewhat lower in contrast and optionally cooler than foreground trees, while maintaining a clear boundary between the main rock and sky or distant land. Atmosphere, weather and light vary by photo; these are drawing choices, not fixed colors for every moment. If distant mountains merge with the subject, adjust value contrast first and hue slightly afterward. Avoid a black foreground that hides important foothill shapes.

Compare two silhouette thumbnails

Duplicate the sketch for two thumbnails: make the summit an overly regular semicircle in one and follow the photographed dome-to-face transition in the other. Keep sky, foothill and canvas the same, and compare resemblance to the reference. Record differences at summit, face edge and foreground overlap. This is a silhouette exercise, not a way to estimate mountain dimensions; mark any hidden outline unknown.

Check stacking and crop

Zoom into the summit against sky, steep face against distant mountains, and foothill against trees, checking for accidental white gaps or lines showing through. Shrink to phone reading size to judge values without adding dense distant rock marks. Check whether summit, face edge or foreground tree is accidentally cropped; if the crop is intentional, record its purpose. Save an original with separate subject and background.

Export and recheck

Save the editable original, then use a share or export entry actually shown in the current version to save an image separately. Reopen it to inspect dome, steep face, foreground occlusion and corner crop, then return to identify subject and background layers. If rock or trees are missing, check visibility and crop; a flattened image without layers does not establish damage to the original. Keep photo source and permission, marking unperformed device or export steps pending.

Separate observation from explanation

The painting records contours and values visible in the photograph; explanations of exfoliation, glaciers and joints come from the park’s materials. Record sources for these separately. A dark painted line does not establish a particular mapped geological joint. Leave unknown photo time or direction as unknown.
