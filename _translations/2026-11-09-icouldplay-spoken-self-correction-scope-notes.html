A speaker can change wording mid-utterance while captions preserve either the original phrasing or an edited final version. Notes taken at the first word can therefore retain something later withdrawn. Use your own paper-card demonstration and original sentences, listening to the complete turn before marking repair scope. No automatic transcription, translation or semantic correction is required, and the exercise is not for professional tasks that demand immediate execution.

Record an original turn about two paper cards

Prepare red and blue cards and record: “Move the red card—sorry, the blue card—into the circle.” After a pause add, “Leave the red card where it is.” Speaking alone is enough; physical movement is optional because the repair scope in speech is the focus. Preserve the exact script in production notes and keep any self-made captions separately named. Without recording facilities, analyze the written example and leave listening untested rather than reporting silent reading as successful iCouldPlay listening.

Listen through before acting

Play from before the turn at a comfortable level and pause after the added sentence. Record the change from red to blue while retaining the move-into-circle action. Do not act at the first mention of red. This is a paper-card language exercise, not equipment operation, medication or financial instruction. If unclear, note the uncertainty and content landmark without uncomfortable volume or a certain answer copied from captions. Pausing before the repair removes essential evidence.

Separate initial wording, repair signal and replacement

Make separate fields for the initial red card, the signal “sorry,” the replacement blue card, and the retained move-into-circle action. The Cambridge introduction to conversational repair concerns problems in speaking, hearing and understanding. This exercise analyzes an explicit self-correction, not every pause as an error. A repair signal alone does not establish scope; inspect the specific words that follow.

Compare two original repairs with different scopes

Write two further original examples: “Move the red card into the circle. No, cancel that whole instruction; move neither card,” and “Move the red card into the circle—no, into the square.” The first withdraws the operation; the second retains color and replaces destination. Mark scope in each rather than adopting “the last noun wins.” These are written counterexamples, not claimed content of the first recording. If separately recorded, give them their own file identities and positions.

Treat captions as a separate text to check

If you made captions, replay the same section and record whether speech and text each retain the initial wording and repair. Edited captions may show only “move the blue card,” while a verbatim version preserves red and its correction. Length alone does not establish fidelity. Production notes can check your sample; real videos need speech and dependable context. Preserve a missing negation as a discrepancy without editing production sources or claiming automatic intention correction by iCouldPlay.

Leave unfinished repairs unresolved

Consider the written fragment “The red card—sorry, I meant…” followed by the clip ending. Repair has begun without a replacement; blue cannot be supplied automatically. For a real cut, inspect neighboring material for the complete turn. Otherwise mark final meaning unconfirmed and do not act on an assumed instruction. Another person can also interrupt a repair, so record speaker identity and completion. A bystander’s suggestion is not automatically an accepted final version.

Change one listening condition at a time

Replay from before the uncertain passage with the same audio and captions. Only use a modest speed reduction if actually available, recording the original setting. Do not change language track, captions and output device together. Persistent uncertainty needs a question mark; repetition is not proof. Restore speed and listen to the complete turn again to check scope, rather than looping “sorry” while missing a later cancellation of the whole instruction.

Separate interpreted meaning from performed action

Understanding “move blue” establishes the spoken target, not whether the action occurred. If speech says blue while a hand moves red, retain the conflict instead of using one channel to certify the other. This exercise permits speech without movement, so absent action is not playback failure. In a real tutorial, a verbal repair does not prove an earlier wrong action was undone. That needs visible state or a reliable correction record. This guide checks language scope manually.

Save the final interpretation and unresolved fields

Keep file and turn location, initial wording, repair signal, replacement scope, retained content, caption differences and unfinished elements. Restate the final request and point to the complete supporting turn, or mark it unconfirmed where evidence is insufficient. Restore settings and position while preserving recording, captions and notes separately. No automated intention recognition, sentence-by-sentence physical-device listening validation or safety judgment of professional instructions is claimed. Recheck scope for another language or edit.
