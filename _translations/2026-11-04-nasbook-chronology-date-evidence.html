A year in a book may refer to an event, a diary’s writing, editorial work or later publication, not automatically the same timeline. Use Book Isle to preserve sources for manual chronological judgement, establishing who recorded what and when before relative expressions such as “last year” or “earlier.” Example dates are invented rather than real historical events. The aim is to keep notes from becoming more precise or certain than the source.

Start with one date in context

Choose one dated record in your history, biography or diary material, keeping heading, surrounding passages and notes. Record title, edition, section and distinctive text, bookmark it and add a file page for PDF if useful. Read completely to identify narrative, quoted heading or bibliographic date rather than rely on a search snippet. If a page contains many dates, start with one event. A tractable scope makes each source explainable; begin with one traceable record instead of a long list of unidentified years.

Assign each date a role

Label event, contemporary recording, later recollection, editing and publication separately using ordinary text; no new database fields are required. A work may have several dates, and tidiness does not justify retaining only the most prominent. Mark an unclear role unresolved and check notes or the edition page. A catalogue year dates that edition, not automatically its story. An exact-looking date does not replace role identification. Establish what it dates before sorting or comparing.

Practise with three explicitly fictional dates

Use an explicitly invented entry written in March 1912 saying “moved last autumn,” published in a 1920 printed edition. First record March 1912 as writing time and 1920 as that edition’s publication time, neither in the move-date field. If the source date and “last year” reference are clear, infer only autumn 1911 provisionally, not a fabricated month and day. Clearly label and separate this exercise from real reading notes so it cannot later appear to be an actual historical record or data supported by a real book.

Find the reference point for relative time

Expressions such as last year, the following year or three months earlier require the speaker’s time or narrative baseline. “Last year” inside a letter may refer to the letter date rather than the whole book’s publication; reported speech may also change speaker. Identify speaker and date source before calculation. Keep unresolved relative wording and note an unclear baseline rather than count backward from the present year. Similar terms do not guarantee the same use. Inspect the complete passage and preceding heading instead of only finding one year through full-text search.

Preserve the source’s date precision

Retain year-only, seasonal or approximate-month precision rather than invent January 1 for sorting. An interval differs from a single day; preserve original wording separately from any labelled normalisation. A season need not become a fixed date. Uncertainty does not make information useless. A supported “before another event” relation can be recorded without a particular year. Plain Book Isle notes do not need every item to resemble a calendar event. Faithful precision is a prerequisite for meaningful comparison.

Record era and calendar before conversion

For different era systems, calendars or a translator’s converted date, copy original notation and explanation first, identify the system and then check manually against reliable references. Similar year/month/day strings need not identify one day, and a book may not convert all dates uniformly. No universal conversion table is supplied here. Keep conversion beside the original with its source and basis; mark insufficient information unresolved. Separate calendar conversion from event identity: a correct conversion still does not establish that two accounts describe one event.

Do not substitute recollection date for event date

A late memoir may recount early events, so separate writing and event years without automatically rejecting a long delay or exempting an eyewitness from checks. Note whether diaries, letters or other evidence support a particular date range. Original records can also be mistaken; source category is not an automatic truth score. Describe how this material supports a date rather than label it simply good or bad, retrieving references when necessary. The workflow links questions to evidence rather than turning one date field into an entire historical conclusion.

Check event identity before treating dates as conflicting

When dates differ, compare object, place, action and event phase first: arrival and formal registration may be different stages. A shared person does not alone establish contradiction. For the same event, retain both dates, sources and precision rather than overwrite with a preference. Check editions, editorial notes or corrections and record what is and is not checked. Preserve an unresolved conflict rather than average the years. A chronology may retain alternatives; readers should see why they remain separate, since more material need not produce a unique conclusion.

Separate event order from certainty

List supported event order separately from dates explicitly given, inferred through relative terms and still unresolved. Longer inference chains require each source step, not only a final year. Plain statuses suffice without assumed automatic sorting, timelines or confidence scores. Repair exported notes that lose approximation markers or range signs before using them. Back up book files and reading data separately, and reverify pages after edition changes because old bookmarks may not map to rearranged text. Date evidence needs location and original wording together.

Verify that the timeline does not overstate the source

Return from each note to its bookmark and check that event identity, date role and precision have not been strengthened. In the invented example, explain why 1912 writing does not mean a 1912 move and 1920 publication does not date the event. Choose the next consequential record before filling a complete biography. Library of Congress teaching material supports contextual source reading, but actual dates still need their specific evidence. Completion means sourced order judgements with visible unknowns; a clear timeline need not eliminate every uncertainty.
