Observe a male Downy Woodpecker’s black-and-white plumage, short bill and red nape in a permitted reference.

Choose an adult male reference

Choose a permitted photograph of an adult male Downy Woodpecker, preferably clinging side-on to a trunk with the head visible. The Cornell Lab describes a short bill, black-and-white head markings and white back; males have a red patch on the nape, unlike females, while juveniles can differ. Record the source and do not combine features from another species or photograph.

Build a portrait composition

Create an approximately 1400 by 1800 portrait canvas in Aquarell; the dimensions are adjustable practice choices. Separate trunk, sketch, bird base colors, feather marks and background using the installed version’s visible controls, and save an editable original. Place the trunk to one side and leave room around the body, bill and tail, avoiding a bill tip or tail tip tangent to the frame. Lightly box the areas occupied by trunk and bird, placing the subject slightly off center and leaving space for the trunk above and below. Hide the guide boxes and check balance.

Block trunk and body direction

Use two not-quite-parallel lines to suggest trunk edges and a long oval for the body, marking the head-to-tail axis. Compare the body’s tilt with the trunk in the photograph rather than assuming they are parallel. Compare head, breast and tail positions in the photo and mark corresponding light points on the canvas. If the connected pose looks stiff, adjust the large forms before adding fine lines. Mark the head-body junction lightly and leave feathers for later. Zoom out to inspect balance and the approximate grip location so the posture reads at a glance.

Compare head and short bill

Block a rounded head above the body, then draw a short, pointed straight bill from its front. Match its size and angle relative to the head in the reference. Cornell uses bill length among features distinguishing similar birds, but this exercise does not treat a single proportion as proof of identification. Ensure the bill emerges from the face rather than merging with bark texture.

Place feet and tail

Find the visible parts of both feet in the reference. Draw legs from body toward trunk, then curved toes and contact points; do not invent a complete anatomical diagram for obscured toes. Observe tail direction and any contact with the trunk, and draw its feather outline without making the tail look like another foot. Hide bark detail to check that the feet connect body to trunk.

Separate black-and-white head marks

Lay in a light face, then follow the reference for dark stripes across the head, comparing eye placement and stripe direction near the bill base. Do not make the whole head black or force both sides to be symmetrical: the photograph shows one view. Preserve light cheek and eyebrow areas, and ensure the eye remains discernible in the dark markings at small size.

Locate the male’s red nape

Add a small red patch on the back of the head at the nape, matching its placement and extent in the adult male photograph rather than extending it over the entire crown. If its boundary is obscured, preserve the uncertainty instead of copying a juvenile or another woodpecker. Hide the patch briefly to check the black-and-white head and bill, then restore it and ensure it does not cover the eye.

Build back and wing color areas

Separate the light back, dark wing and visible white spots according to the reference. Establish large black-and-white areas before adding a few organized spots on the wing. Do not draw the entire hidden back or spread equal white dots over the whole bird. Use subtly varied dark grays so wing contours, feet and trunk do not merge into a single black mass. Compare a few visible spots near the shoulder and wingtip first, then add intervening ones; do not continue obscured spots onto the trunk.

Bark and contact

Add a restrained vertical bark texture on the separate trunk layer, reducing it near toes and body edges so it does not overwhelm the silhouette. If a shadow is needed, identify the light first and add a small contact dark at the grip without blacking out the feet. Zoom in to check toe contact and out to separate bird from trunk; adjust local background value if needed.

Troubleshoot and export a copy

If the bird looks as though it is perched horizontally on a branch, compare trunk, body axis and feet with the reference. If the red patch resembles a crown, reduce it and move it back to the nape. Save the editable original and export a separate PNG or JPEG using a visible control in the installed version. Reopen it to inspect bill, patch, wing spots and tail for clarity and cropping; record the source without claiming unperformed device or print tests.

Compare bark detail

Duplicate the original, keeping a simplified trunk in one copy and adding restrained bark texture in the other while preserving the bird’s shape, colors and grip. Compare at the same zoom. If texture hides the bill tip or claws, reduce lines near their contact regions. Keep both editable versions and the source record so bark can be practiced later without disturbing the established bird structure. Also compare both at phone width to see whether the eye, bill and foot contacts remain legible, then adjust local values in the original if needed.
