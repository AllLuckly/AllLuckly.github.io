“The clock refused to answer” need not report a clock making a decision. Read an original passage in Book Isle, preserving subject and predicate before checking how context attributes human attitudes/actions to a nonhuman object. These are reader-made rhetorical notes, not automatic detection of personification, authorial intention or an object’s actual mind.

Prepare text and a retrievable location

Externally prepare a small document you may use: “I asked how much of the night remained. The clock on the wall refused to answer, leaving only ticking in the room.” Label it an original exercise, import a genuinely readable format and retain the source. Record title, section/paragraph and opening phrase; for PDF add printed page and reader location rather than reflow-dependent screen lines. The Academy of American Poets definition supplies the nonhuman/human-quality concept, not original quotations for this exercise or an automatic Book Isle classification.

Extract subject and predicate separately

List clock as subject, refused to answer as predicate and ticking as adjacent sound description. The presence of “I” does not make every action the speaker’s, and clock/night are not one character. Quote only necessary wording, keeping your explanation separate and retaining the volitional sense of refused. Where supported, save a short selection and revisit its scope; otherwise record externally without inventing a universal button. First establish who is represented as doing what, before conclusions about mood, theme or biography.

Locate the attributed human quality in context

Ticking can be a literal mechanical event, while refusing an answer attributes intentional response within a question/answer relation. In this night-question context, note a personification candidate supported by a human response posture, not merely an object making sound. Nonhuman subjects are not automatically personified: “the clock stopped” can be literal in a fault record. Do not infer actual concealment or thought by a real clock. Textual representation and real cognition are different levels, and the passage supplies no evidence for the latter.

Keep machine-function contexts as alternatives

Use a control: “The speaking clock did not respond to the button; its battery was exhausted.” Nonresponse can describe function without intentional neglect. When a work depicts a smart device answering/refusing, inspect its fictional setting and functions before classifying from the verb alone. Separate original, functional control and evidence, retaining uncertainty rather than using a definition as a mechanical scoring rule. These are not measured reports of one event, and the control implies no Book Isle voice-device control or diagnostic feature.

Separate character wording from narrative presentation

For a character saying “The wind will not let me leave today,” record that speaker’s wording, not narrator-confirmed intention in the wind. Inspect humour, exaggeration, fantasy setting or replies without declaring the speaker unreliable from one personification. Keep sentence, scope of refusal, speaker and interpretation separate. Unmarked speech attribution may remain unresolved; first-person “I” is not automatically the author. Do not add an actual storm, injury or duration of confinement absent from the passage.

Make a one-predicate control

Rewrite the original as “The clock on the wall kept ticking; nobody in the room answered.” Subject/action relations change, and nonanswer is no longer explicitly attributed to the clock. Preserve both labelled versions without overwriting the sole original or also changing the scene to daylight. Compare the removed human attitude and retained sound, without demanding identical effects. Changing refused to did not answer may weaken intentionality but still needs context: one word is no universal rhetorical switch. This is reader rewriting, not automatic literary editing or validation.

Check that rereading/export preserves inference status

Return to the passage after leaving it, reading original before notes and checking that a candidate has not become certain object cognition. Open exported notes to verify subject, predicate, context, rewrite status and provenance, including negatives and possibility markers. Failed search requires text-layer/scope checks rather than declaring no personification in the book. Scans may use manual locations without invented OCR claims. If only “the clock is lonely” remains, restore the wording evidence instead of treating your feeling as the sole interpretation.

Accept evidence for subject and human quality

Retain original, literal ticking, refusal-as-personification candidate, functional control and controlled rewrite, so another reader can recover the evidence. Grammar resolves subject confusion; context distinguishes malfunction from refusal; attribution limits emotional claims. Keep alternative readings where justified rather than claiming one definition exhausts all rhetorical traditions. Unlike source/target metaphor pairing, this task tracks human agency attributed to an object; unlike fable study, it does not infer a story’s moral. It produces retrievable reading evidence, not automatic detection, object-mind measurement or certified authorial intent.
