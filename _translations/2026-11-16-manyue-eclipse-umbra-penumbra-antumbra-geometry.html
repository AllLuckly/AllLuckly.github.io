A darker shaded region does not by itself identify an eclipse. First locate source and blocker, then ask how much of the Sun is visible from a stated point. Separate an obscured Sun from a Moon entering Earth’s shadow. Read two original, non-scale pages in ManYue. The task checks diagrams, not solar viewing, local eclipse prediction or an assumed astronomical tracker.

Draw the Sun as an extended source

Externally draw a solar disk, a smaller blocking disk and an observation region, labelled S/M/E with light direction. The Sun needs upper/lower edges; a point source cannot illustrate the complete penumbra structure. Mark sizes/distances not to scale. Four boundary rays explain geometry, not measured umbral length. Label the blocker on each page and distinguish actual rays from auxiliary extensions with text/line style even when colors coincide, rather than memorizing shadow names by darkness.

Identify the middle body in both arrangements

For a solar eclipse order Sun–Moon–Earth: the Moon blocks and its shadow can reach Earth. For a lunar eclipse order Sun–Earth–Moon: Earth blocks and the Moon may enter Earth’s shadow. Renaming a solar diagram without moving bodies does not make a lunar diagram. Follow incoming light before stating whether the observer’s Sun view or sunlight reaching the Moon is obstructed. Left/right is layout; reflecting the whole consistent diagram does not swap the physical event names.

Distinguish umbra and penumbra from an observation point

Place P in the central umbra and Q in the outer penumbra behind the blocker. In ideal geometry P sees the whole solar disk obscured, Q only part; the Sun itself has not dimmed. Boundaries follow tangencies involving different source edges, not arbitrary parallel black lines. In a solar diagram only observers in the relevant shadow region experience that event, not all Earth. Earth’s night side is not automatically the Moon’s umbra: night and eclipse have different blockers.

Create a visibility card: P sees no direct solar disk, Q sees part, R outside shadow sees the whole disk. Match regions after visibility rather than assigning meanings intrinsically to letters. Reclassify changed relative positions: a point still called P after leaving the umbra no longer retains total obscuration simply through its marker.

Identify antumbra beyond the umbral tip

When the Moon’s umbral cone ends before Earth’s surface, the central region beyond the tip is the antumbra. The Moon’s apparent disk there is too small to cover the Sun fully, giving an annular configuration. Light has not passed through the Moon, and antumbra is not merely a darker penumbra. Mark tip versus observation plane and compare reaching/not reaching it. A non-scale ruler cannot classify an actual event; distances/angular sizes matter. Invent no current-year dates, durations or visible cities.

Compare the whole lunar disk with Earth’s shadow

In Sun–Earth–Moon order place the lunar disk only in penumbra, partly in umbra and wholly in umbra as distinct cases. A total lunar eclipse is a whole-disk umbral configuration, not a vanished Moon or halted reflection; real color also involves the atmosphere. Solar classification used a ground observer, whereas this lunar diagram compares the whole lunar disk with shadow regions. Keep those levels separate. State the near-full-Moon condition without claiming every full Moon is eclipsed.

Add orbital alignment to new/full Moon conditions

The Moon’s orbital plane is tilted about five degrees to Earth’s solar orbital plane, so new/full Moons often pass above or below the shadow. Add a side view separating apparent alignment on paper from actual three-dimensional alignment. Solar events require near-new Moon and lunar events near-full Moon, but these are only part of the necessary geometry, not a monthly prediction rule. Note proximity to orbital-node directions. Without precise orbital parameters keep this qualitative instead of inventing arcsecond accuracy.

Contrast“new Moon is one necessary condition for a solar eclipse” with“every new Moon produces one.” The first still needs alignment; the second is too strong because orbital tilt can miss shadow regions. Check full Moon/lunar eclipse similarly. Do not turn association into a sufficient guarantee or discard side-view conditions from a frontal picture.

Change one element as a reading counterexample

Keep a counterexample on a report page: retain solar ordering but label penumbral Q as total. In ManYue use Q’s partial solar visibility to repair the label without moving P. In another round read the separately prepared complete Sun–Earth–Moon lunar-eclipse page, checking the blocker and the body losing sunlight while retaining its existing region labels. Do not replace only the middle identity while leaving Earth at the far end. Mark corrections by page identity and external notes rather than automatic celestial/shadow recognition. Split dense pages instead of deleting necessary words such as partial, whole and near.

Reopen and identify the blocker before naming the event

Reopen both pages and answer source, blocker and observation location before naming umbra, penumbra or antumbra. Explain why Q is not total, why a whole Moon in Earth’s umbra differs from one ground point in lunar shadow, and why eclipses need not recur monthly. Keep non-scale and necessary-condition notes; thumbnails are not observation photos. Retrace boundaries after changing sizes, distances or observation planes instead of retaining familiar shading. The result is reviewable diagram reading, not an actual eclipse calculation or universal display test.
