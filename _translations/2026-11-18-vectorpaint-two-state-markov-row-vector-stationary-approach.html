Build a fictional two-state probability model with four directed conditional transitions, then calculate two-step distributions and stationarity. Probability bars are not observed headcounts or an assumed model of independent repeated choices. Declare states, time steps and matrix orientation before explaining approach through a recurrence.

Declare exhaustive states and a row-vector convention

A and B are mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Given the present state, the next-state distribution does not depend on earlier history, and the same transition rule applies at every step. Use row probabilities p=(a,b), updated by right multiplication p_next=pP. The reference uses column-stochastic matrices/column vectors; transposition reconciles conventions. Do not apply a column formula unchanged to this row table. Create state, arrow, matrix, distribution-bar and condition layers; save native work. p0=(1,0) means certain initial A, not an observed survey headcount.

Check four arrows by their source-state row sums

Set A→A=3/4, A→B=1/4, B→A=1/2, B→B=1/2. These are rows(3/4,1/4),(1/2,1/2) of P, with nonnegative entries summing to 1 per row. Sum outgoing edges for each source state, not incoming edges into A. On a suggested 2200×1600 canvas use centres(650,650),(1550,650), separated reverse arcs and self-loops. Check arrowheads, fraction labels and legend together; colour alone must not identify a state.

Calculate two steps from the certain initial state

p1=(1,0)P=(3/4,1/4). Multiplying again gives A=(3/4)(3/4)+(1/4)(1/2)=11/16 and B=(3/4)(1/4)+(1/4)(1/2)=5/16, still summing to 1. A’s second-step probability is not simply copied as 3/4 because B contributes incoming probability. For total bar width 960px, use 720/240 for p1 and 660/300 for p2, with exact fraction labels. Width maps probability;11/16 does not report eleven observed occurrences out of sixteen.

Independently check two-step B through two paths

Two mutually exclusive paths reach B after two steps: A→A→B with probability(3/4)(1/4)=3/16, and A→B→B with(1/4)(1/2)=1/8, totaling 5/16. Products use conditional transition probabilities, not an assumption that successive states are independent. Draw this path ledger and compare the matrix result; omitting self-loops loses a path. Reusing one matrix over time does not detach transitions from the current state: time homogeneity is not independent identical sampling.

Solve stationarity and substitute both components

Let π=(a,1−a) satisfy πP=π. Then a=(3/4)a+(1/2)(1−a), giving a=2/3 and b=1/3. Substitution yields 1/2+1/6=2/3 for A and 1/6+1/6=1/3 for B, summing to 1. Split the 960px stationary bar 640/320. Stationarity is not the simple row average(5/8,3/8), nor equal transition probabilities. Normalization matters: the delivered vector is a probability distribution, not an arbitrary scalar multiple. At stationarity A→B probability flow is(2/3)(1/4)=1/6 and B→A is(1/3)(1/2)=1/6. An unchanged distribution does not mean every trajectory stays put. Retain self-loops and cross-state edges rather than erase arrows at stationarity.

Prove approach rather than finite-step equality

A probabilities satisfy a_next=1/2+a_current/4, hence a_next−2/3=(a_current−2/3)/4. From a0=1, a_n=2/3+1/(3·4^n). The extra term is positive for every finite nonnegative integer n, so the distribution approaches stationarity without exactly reaching it in finite steps. Distinguish equality from approximation; a displayed 0.667 does not prove exact equality. This recurrence proves convergence for this model, not unconditionally for chains with other rules, absorbing classes or periodicity. Starting at π would keep π at every step, consistent with the strict finite-step deviation from certain A. Deliver the initial condition with the closed form instead of applying that solution unchanged to every starting distribution.
