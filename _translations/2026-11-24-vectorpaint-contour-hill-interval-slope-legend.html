Draw closed contours for a fictional hill, label the elevation interval, and compare slopes.

Define the teaching scope

This is a plan-view diagram of a fictional hill, not a real location, survey or navigation route. The US Geological Survey explains that contours connect points of equal elevation, adjacent contours differ by a contour interval, and closer spacing generally indicates a steeper slope. Use a simplified single hill without cliffs or depressions and state those conditions alongside the image.

Arrange canvas and notes

Create an approximately 1600 by 1200 canvas in VectorPaint, with dimensions adapted to the reading context. Separate background, individual contours, text and legend using layer controls visible in the installed version; save an editable original. Reserve the left for the hill and the right for title, contour interval and slope explanation. Avoid real place names or roads so the illustration reads as a conceptual example.

Set elevations and interval

For the draft, choose four contours at 100, 110, 120 and 130 meters, each separated by a 10-meter elevation interval; the values are illustrative. Elevation rises inward, so this particular example represents a hill rather than a closed depression. Label the figure “Fictional example; contour interval 10 m” so the numbers are not mistaken for measurements. First list the four values and their inside-to-outside order on paper, then check the finished contours against that list for missing lines or repeated labels.

Draw the lowest closed contour

With a path or curve tool draw a large, slightly irregular closed outline for the 100-meter contour. Leave it unfilled at first and inspect the join between its ends. It may be roughly oval but should not be a sharp self-crossing flower shape. Zoom out to confirm margins on all sides, no tangent against the canvas edge and enough room for notes on the right.

Draw progressively higher contours

Inside the outer line, draw closed 110-, 120- and 130-meter curves in order, each surrounding the same summit area. Each line represents one elevation; do not let lines cross. Place the lines closer together on the right and farther apart on the left. Avoid simply scaling evenly spaced concentric circles, because the exercise must show a difference in slope. Adjust the widest spaces first and inspect adjacent relationships one by one. If a point drags a line outside its enclosing contour, undo that move and try a smaller adjustment.

Check adjacent elevations

Trace a fictional path from outside inward on the left: it should cross 100, 110, 120 and 130 meters in order. Check the right side the same way. If a line is missing, duplicated or out of order, correct the drawing rather than changing only the labels. Contour interval is a difference in elevation, not the pixel spacing between lines; the right side still has a 10-meter interval.

Add labels without hiding lines

Place four elevation labels in available space and connect each to the proper contour with a thin leader. If the installed interface supports legible text positioning, labels may sit near the line, but do not hide line joins. Include meters in the explanatory text and verify every number has one corresponding contour. If crowded at small size, move some notes to the right rather than shrinking them illegibly. Ask another reader to follow each leader to its contour; rearrange any labels that remain ambiguous.

Explain the two slopes

Write “Right: closer lines, steeper slope; left: wider spacing, gentler slope” on the right and use fine arrows toward the two sides. State that this comparison assumes the same contour interval shown here. Arrows should not cross numbers confusingly and do not represent water flow. Extra elevation color bands are unnecessary because this exercise concerns spacing and slope.

Inspect closures and crossings

Zoom in to inspect each curve’s closure and accidental crossings, especially in the closely spaced right-hand region. If lines appear merged, move control points or redraw the local section while retaining elevation order and closer spacing on the right. Preview in grayscale or reduced saturation and check contour-to-background contrast; do not rely only on color to distinguish contours from leaders.

Export and troubleshoot

Save the editable original and export a separate PNG using a control visible in the installed version. Reopen at the intended display size and check numbers, leaders and the closest contours for legibility. If lines merge, enlarge the graphic or space the lines a little farther apart, then recheck the relative slopes. If legend arrows are confused with contours, give the arrows a different line style and explain it. If labels crop, increase the margin and export again. Do not navigate from this illustration.

Make a reversed-slope comparison

Duplicate the original and move the closer contour spacing to the left and wider spacing to the right, keeping all four elevations and the interval unchanged; update the accompanying explanation. Compare both images side by side at the same size, checking which side is steeper and whether labels followed the change. Keep both editable files and source records. For real terrain later, obtain a reliable dated map with scale rather than renaming this fictional example. Check that the highest line remains inside in both versions; do not move 130 meters outside when reversing the slope. Record the export size for a future comparison under the same reading conditions.
