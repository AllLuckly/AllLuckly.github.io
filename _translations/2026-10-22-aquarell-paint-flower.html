Establish a complete flower around the centre before adding details. Use a five-petal flower to practise Aquarell layers, colour and brush control. Arrange your own petals, establish large shapes and then handle colour.

Aim for a small flower that stays clear when reduced: varied petals, a focused centre and shadows explaining overlap, rather than equally black outlines everywhere.

1. Arrange petals around a centre in five directions

Create a 1400 × 1400 pixel canvas and lightly place the centre slightly above the middle. Imagine five directions and mark them with short lines before drawing complete outlines.

Let the flower occupy about two-thirds of the canvas width, leaving at least around 150 pixels at the edges. Slight changes of angle and size look natural; avoid crowding three petals on one side and leaving the other empty unless deliberate.

2. Paint large shapes before fine lines

Sketch rounded petal outlines, like small fans narrow at the base and wider outside. Check the overall flower silhouette before ensuring each petal connects naturally to the centre.

Add a colour layer with soft pink or lilac. Try a lower-saturation pink and reserve pale yellow centrally. Fill broad interiors first, then refine edges with a smaller brush instead of slowly filling every petal with tiny marks.

3. Use shadows at the bases to show overlap

Choose a slightly darker, cooler related colour for petal bases. Begin at two or three clear overlaps instead of outlining all five petals in black.

Keep shadows on a separate layer for overall adjustment. Try about 40% layer opacity, then judge against the base. This is a comparison setting; brushes, colours and stroke repetitions produce different results.

Lighten shadows outward along each petal by reducing repeated coverage and stroke area, not by assuming an automatic gradient button. Undo a hard recent stroke and try lighter coverage instead of repeatedly muddying the base.

4. Make the centre a clear but restrained focus

Paint the centre warm yellow on the detail layer, adding a few darker orange-brown dots. Begin with a centre one-tenth to one-sixth of the flower’s width and adjust to the shape without exact measurement.

Vary dot size slightly without making each a separate bead. Start with three to five dots, zoom out and decide whether more are needed. If the centre dominates, reduce its contrast or area instead of detailing every petal to compensate.

5. Add a few petal marks to suggest direction

Paint a few short marks outward from each petal base. Let them diminish or stop before the edge. One or two main lines per petal, supplemented by a few lighter short ones, are enough.

Put a stem or two leaves where they do not obscure the centre and manage them separately. Leaves can contrast in temperature but should not exceed the flower’s area, or the exercise becomes a complex plant composition.

Final checks: return from the whole to the details

View the flower at roughly 200–300 pixels wide. Check silhouette and centre. If it becomes a pink lump, separate neighbouring petals through value and space before adding fine lines.

Zoom in again for white gaps at joins and shadows spilling into the background. Intentional empty space supports form; accidental gaps may break connections. Export the image and keep the layered original for later recolouring.

Check hierarchy with a smaller second flower

Add a secondary flower half the main flower’s size. Start with outline and base colour, without dense texture. It should support, not compete with, the main focus. Reduce contrast and detail if both demand attention; adjust background or colour if the small one disappears. This practises scale hierarchy and prepares for bouquet compositions. Keep both one-flower and two-flower originals for different layouts.

Questions and limitations

Must all five petals be equally large?

No. Identical petals look patterned; slight variation feels hand-drawn. Balance the distribution and maintain clear connections to the centre rather than pursuing equal dimensions alone.

Why do repaired petal edges keep becoming thicker?

Repeated dark tracing may thicken the outline. Stop adding lines and check whether colour shapes already describe the form. Repair smaller sections rather than retracing the whole perimeter.

Must I redraw to change the whole flower’s colour?

Layers preserve editing options, but recolouring depends on the version. Safest is to retain sketch, shadow and details while choosing new colour on a separate base layer, without assuming an unverified batch-recolour feature.

Can this flower go directly into printed stickers?

First check actual export pixels, transparent edges and printer requirements. This tutorial covers drawing and file checks only, not guaranteed dielines, bleed, colour management or commercial asset rights.
