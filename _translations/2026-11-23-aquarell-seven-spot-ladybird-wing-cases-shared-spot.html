Observe the wing-case seam, seven spots and leaf contact from a dorsal seven-spot ladybird reference.

Identify the species and dorsal reference

Woodland Trust describes the seven-spot ladybird as having three spots on each wing case and a seventh at the front spanning both. Choose a permitted adult seven-spot photograph with back and center seam visible, and record source and name. Ladybirds vary by species; this spot arrangement applies to the chosen reference and is not sufficient to identify other insects from a drawing. Do not capture or disturb a live insect for the exercise.

Compose leaf and canvas

Create an approximately 1600 by 1600 square canvas in the current Aquarell interface and save an editable original; this size is only a suggestion. Place the ladybird slightly above center, leaving room around body, antennae and leaf edge. Use available layers for sketch, insect, spots, leaf and background, following the labels actually displayed. Mark the leaf direction and insect body axis first, decide head orientation, and do not draw leaf veins through the body.

Block the oval back and head

Use a rounded oval for the back silhouette and reserve a smaller head and pronotum region at the front. Compare length and width against the reference rather than making the wing cases two unrelated red circles. The junction may be hidden by the view, so establish visible large shapes first. Hide the background and inspect at thumbnail size to confirm head and body axis align; revise an overly flat or elongated back before adding spots.

Draw the wing-case seam

Draw a light line along the body axis from front to rear to represent the seam between closed wing cases. It follows the curved back and need not lie at the geometric center of an oblique image; do not make it a wide black split. The outside contours form one overall body and meet toward the rear as in the reference. Temporarily remove color and check that the seam does not continue into the head or leaf.

Place three plus three plus one spots

Using the reference, mark three dark spots on each wing case and one shared spot across the front of the seam, for seven in total. Number small guide dots first, then adjust their shapes, size and projection from the photo. Do not count the shared spot twice because the seam passes through it. Back curvature and perspective may narrow side spots; mark any hidden position unknown.

Place head and visible appendages

Draw the dark head and pronotum from the reference, observing their junction with the red wing cases. Insects have three pairs of legs, but some may be hidden beneath the body or by the leaf in a dorsal view; draw identifiable legs first and record hidden ones rather than forcing all six into view. Antennae extend from the head region and need enough background contrast; do not attach them to the red wing cases.

Model the curved back with value

Block the wing cases in mid-red, choose one exercise light and use lighter red on the lit side with a somewhat darker unlit edge. A few soft highlights can suggest curvature; every spot does not need a white dot. Spots belong to the wing-case surface and should not cast separate shadows off the body. At thumbnail size check seam, shared spot and silhouette; if red and black merge, adjust contrast and spot spacing first.

Paint leaf and contact

Block the leaf in muted green and mark a main vein from the reference, stopping it where the insect occludes it. Place visible foot ends near the leaf and use small dark contact marks where helpful; do not connect every unseen leg to the same vein. If the reference uses a plain background, the leaf is your own scene design and does not establish the original host plant. Hide the shadow and check the insect silhouette against the leaf.

Audit spot count and seam

Make a seven-cell checklist for three left spots, three right spots and one shared front spot. Mark unseen spots unknown rather than placing them on the leaf merely to reach seven. Save a sketch copy with temporary numbers, then hide the numbers and inspect the image. If the seam visually divides the shared spot, ensure the design still represents one cross-seam area. Other ladybird species have different counts, so this is not a universal identification key.

Check crop and occlusion

Zoom into the front of the wing cases, seam, head and visible foot-to-leaf junctions. If white gaps appear, decide whether background should show there or layers fail to meet. If a vein runs through the insect, adjust stacking or erase the hidden line. At actual reading size, distinguish visible spots without over-thickening the silhouette to show tiny details. Leave room in the corners and avoid cropping antennae.

Save and recheck output

Save the editable original, then use a share or export entry actually shown in the current version to save an image separately. Reopen it and inspect spots, seam, head, leaf and crop, then return to identify sketch, insect and background layers. If a spot is missing, check visibility, color and scale first; a flattened image without layers does not establish damage to the original. Keep source and permission notes and mark unperformed device and export steps pending.

Note viewpoint limits

In an oblique rather than directly dorsal view, the near side may look wider and spots on the far side may be hidden by the contour. Draw the parts actually visible and note unseen positions separately. A source describing the total count does not require every spot to be completely visible in a single photograph.
