Counting successes in three fixed trials differs from stopping at the first success. Read fictional independent-trial cards and waiting/tail probabilities in Book Isle, without turning mathematical success into a real guarantee. Calculations are external, with no actual card draws or behavioural predictions.

Declare stopping and the variable’s starting value

Trials have only S, F, are independent and identically distributed, with P(S)=1/4 and P(F)=3/4, stopping at first S. T counts trials including that success, so T=1, 2, 3,…, never zero. No preset cap does not mean infinitely many trials were executed, and page-card count is not an extra stopping limit.

First success on trial three permits only FFS

T=3 requires FFS, with probability (3/4)²(1/4)=9/64. SFF stops at one; FSF stops at two, so neither belongs. Generally P(T=n)=(3/4)^(n−1)(1/4). Do not multiply by three as in a fixed-three-trials exactly-one-success question: the first-success location already fixes the order. The incorrect 27/64 combines different stopping positions.

Here p is per-trial success probability, not a probability that accumulates after failure. After two failures the next trial still succeeds with probability 1/4 in the independent constant-p model, not 1/2 because success is “due.” Separate a one-trial conditional probability from cumulative success-by-time probability, which increases without changing per-trial p.

Use the complement for success by trial three

T≤3 includes S, FS, FFS with probabilities 1/4, 3/16, 9/64, totaling 37/64. The complement FFF has probability 27/64, giving the same 1−27/64. T>3 means still no success, not success on three. Keep point, cumulative, tail columns and check 37+27=64, not 9+37. Complementary events, not matching denominators, justify a sum of one.

Failure count differs from T by one

Let Y=T−1 count failures before first success, allowing zero. Y=2 and T=3 are the same event, probability 9/64. Y=0 means success on trial one, probability 1/4, not success without a trial. P(Y=k)=(3/4)^k/4 for nonnegative integer k differs by one exponent because it counts another object. Read whether a “geometric” variable counts trials through success or failures before success before copying its formula. Different textbook conventions are not contradictory probability theorems.

Conditional waiting counts three further failures

Given T>2, asking T>5 requires failures on trials three through five. Independence and fixed probability give (3/4)³=27/64, also obtained from P(T>5)/P(T>2). Previous failures do not make success due. Distinguish total five trials from three additional trials and a tail event from first success exactly on five; conditioning removes the already-known two failures.

Check tail inequalities: T>3 means three initial failures and has probability 27/64, while T≥3 requires only two initial failures and has probability 9/16. For the failure count, Y≥3 corresponds to T>3. Derive events before exponents instead of always using q³ whenever a three appears.

Expected four trials is not a four-trial guarantee

The tail sum E[T]=Σ from n=0 of P(T>n)=Σ(3/4)^n=4 gives E[Y]=3. Expectation is a model average, not a deadline. P(T≤4)=1−(3/4)^4=175/256<1, so five or more remains possible; do not force S on card four. Three displayed sample cards do not make the mean three. The convergent formula needs a success probability with 0<p≤1; p=0 never succeeds and is outside this finite-mean conclusion.

A nonreplacement control changes the assumptions

A uniformly shuffled four-card set with one S and three F, read without replacement until S, has T=1,…, 4 with probability 1/4 each. In particular P(T=3)=(3/4)(2/3)(1/2)=1/4, not 9/64. Failure changes the remaining proportions, so iid assumptions fail. This is a paper control, not observed shuffling. Diagnose stopping, replacement and variable convention before exponents/complements, not alleged App simulation or display randomness.

In the without-replacement control P(T=1)=1/4, but after an initial F the next success probability becomes 1/3. This locates the failed constant-p/independence premise, beyond merely comparing 9/64 with 1/4. Four cards also make T>4 impossible, unlike a geometric variable with arbitrarily large positive-integer support.
