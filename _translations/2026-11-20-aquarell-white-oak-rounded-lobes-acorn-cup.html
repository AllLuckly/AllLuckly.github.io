Draw an imaginary white oak twig with a rounded-lobed leaf and a shallow-cupped acorn. U.S. Forest Service material describes uneven rounded lobes and a cup enclosing roughly one quarter to one third of the nut. This example chooses seven lobes for its picture, not as a fixed count for every white oak leaf. An illustration does not replace plant identification or an edibility judgment.

Place the twig, leaf and fruit

Create an approximately 1800 × 1500 canvas. Let a twig run from lower left toward upper right, with the main leaf above and an acorn lower right against a quiet background. Lightly mark the leaf axis, acorn long axis and twig direction before adding veins or cup texture. Separate sketch, twig, leaf, fruit, cup and background into layers and save. Spacing and direction are original composition choices, not measured size or maturity. If the leaf hides the whole acorn when reduced, move it rather than omit the cup.

Build one continuous leaf outline

From the petiole draw a main axis to the tip. Mark three rounded side lobes on each side and one terminal lobe for this seven-lobed example. Sides need not mirror one another. Notches return inward without cutting through the midrib. Trace the outline from one side of the petiole around each lobe and terminal tip, returning along the other side to close it. Actual white oak leaf shapes and counts vary; do not present three pairs plus one as a universal fixed formula.

Carry midrib to tip and side veins toward lobes

Draw a lighter midrib from petiole toward the end, then side veins toward major lobes. Taper side veins inside the leaf rather than extending them past its edge into the background. If the leaf bends, veins follow its surface; every notch does not require a separate heavy line. With colors hidden, check petiole meets twig and midrib starts at petiole. If the leaf looks like green paper pasted beside a branch, repair the attachment instead of hiding it with more dark lines.

Separate lit and shaded leaf planes

Block the leaf in mid-green and choose upper-left light. Add lighter green to lit areas and moderate shadow near notches or turns. Let value follow the volume of the whole leaf rather than outlining every lobe with identical white. A little paper or background can suggest light, but maintain a continuous edge. If leaf and background merge, adjust local background value or the leaf as a whole before thickening every outline. Color shows lighting in this picture, not tree health or season.

Overlap the acorn and shallow cup

Draw an elongated nut with a small upper portion behind the cup and a tapering lower end. Connect the cup by a short stalk to the twig. Use roughly one quarter to one third of the nut's length as the covered range in this side-view exercise; viewpoint affects visible proportion, so it is not a photo measurement. Let the curved rim surround the nut rather than float as a hat. Block shapes first and trace the nut continuing below the cup before adding a few short texture marks.

In a copy hide cup texture, trace stalk to cup and the exposed edge down to the nut's tip. If a background gap appears, check depth order and contact; if the cup covers the whole nut, shorten its visible height and retain enough nut body. Do not create a nonexistent connection with an extra ring of shadow or treat the number of decorative marks as a scientific count.

Attach objects to the twig and check overlaps

Let the twig taper somewhat, attaching leaf stalk and fruit stalk separately. Do not draw the acorn directly from the end of a leaf vein. The main leaf may hide a little twig, but the branch should not inexplicably pass in front across the leaf surface. If adding a secondary leaf, keep the main tip and cup visible and its attachment readable; the deliverable remains one example leaf and one acorn. Use low-contrast background shapes to suggest a canopy without copying a reference photograph's full composition.

On the sketch, mark leaf-stalk and fruit-stalk attachments with two differently colored points, trace them to the twig and hide the marks. They are location aids, not observed buds, flowers or counts. If the subject obscures an attachment, retain enough directional information to understand the connection instead of adding unexplained bright dots to the final image.

Hide details and troubleshoot each connection

Turn off veins, cup texture and highlights, leaving leaf outline, twig, cup and nut. Trace three side lobes, a terminal lobe and three on the other side, checking notches remain part of one leaf. If a lobe is missing, decide whether to restore the exercise's seven-lobed example; do not infer the species has a fixed count. Check fruit stalk, cup and nut junctions before restoring textures one layer at a time. Decorative layers are not structural layers.

Save and inspect an output

Save the editable draft, export through an entry actually available in the installed version and open the image separately. Reduce to inspect leaf–fruit balance and enlarge petiole, cup edge and notches. Reopen the original and hide only texture, ensuring twig, leaf and nut remain. For export errors inspect file and layer state first. Identify the reference as white oak and the seven-lobed shape as an example; mark unperformed export, reopening and device steps pending. Do not use this illustration to determine plant identity, collecting permission or food safety.

Seven lobes, arrangement and colors are exercise choices.
