Draw a male moose in profile, separating palmate antlers, dewlap and overlapping long legs.

Read the source and choose an antlered reference

The US National Park Service explains that male moose can have broad palmate antlers, females lack antlers, and both sexes have a dewlap. This exercise uses the profile of an adult male shown with antlers in a reference. Antlers vary seasonally, so absence alone does not establish sex. Choose a clear profile reference permitted for study and record its source and angle. Do not approach or disturb wildlife or present an invented pose as a sighting.

Set the canvas and overall bounds

Create an approximately 1800 × 1500 pixel canvas in Aquarell using the current interface, and save an editable original. Separate sketch, background, torso, antlers and detail into available layers; use the visible functions if labels differ. Lightly mark safe margins and a horizontal ground line, then draw a broad body oval with room in front for the head and above for the antlers. Canvas dimensions serve composition, not measurement of the animal.

Connect shoulder, back, neck and head

Build a shoulder and back contour around the body oval, raising the shoulder where shown in the reference and extending the long neck and head. Leave volume for the muzzle instead of connecting antlers to nose with a single straight line. In large shapes check whether shoulder, neck and jaw join naturally. If the head floats beside the torso, repair both neck boundaries before adding features. A profile and three-quarter view have different apparent head widths; follow the chosen reference.

Separate near and far antlers

Locate the attachment of each antler near the top of the head. Simplify the near antler as a broad palmate shape, then place a few tines along its edge using the photograph. The far antler can be partly hidden by the head or near antler and need not mirror it on the page. Reserve negative spaces between antlers and sky, and confirm their bases emerge from the head. Follow the chosen reference for width and tine count; neither is used here to determine age.

Use overlaps to draw four long legs

Mark four leg roots below the body, separating near and far pairs with a modest value difference. Upper legs should join the torso, with joints positioned from the reference rather than four equally spaced poles. Draw visible outer contours first and account for portions hidden by the body or nearer legs. Planted hooves should meet the same ground plane while a raised foot can sit higher. Reduce the image to check that the body appears supported.

Place the dewlap and facial features

Use the photograph to add a dewlap extending naturally from the underside of the neck; this structure is not exclusive to males. Place eye, ear and muzzle with a few values, avoiding a dewlap that reads as another leg or ears that merge with antler bases. If the dewlap is hidden or very dark in the chosen image, draw only the reliably visible outline rather than inferring exact length, color or shape from text.

Block color while preserving depth

Lay a dark-brown midtone for the body, separating shoulder, torso and head with large shapes before adding restrained light and shadow. Antlers may be slightly lighter than the back to separate from the background, without outlining every edge equally. If far and near legs overlap, distinguish them with a background gap or subtle value change. Avoid combining a highly saturated background with strong fine texture so the overall silhouette remains primary.

Compare two amounts of space around antlers

Save small sketch variants with the antler tips close to the top edge and with more open space, keeping the animal unchanged. Compare cropping and the clarity of the head direction. Then hide detail temporarily and check antler bases, eye, dewlap and feet. If the far antler and ear merge, adjust the visible background gap before reducing local texture. These are composition studies, not evidence that the antler shapes belong to different individuals.

Inspect overlaps and ground contact

Zoom in to see whether background color covers the hoof-ground contact and whether the near legs occlude the far legs where intended. At thumbnail size, check that long muzzle, shoulder, legs and antlers remain distinct. Fix large shapes before adding fur if a connection is unclear. A low-contrast ground shadow can explain placement, but its direction is part of this lighting choice rather than a claim about the reference photograph’s conditions.

If the antler tips crowd the canvas edge, reposition the subject in the sketch before shrinking the whole work. Keep enough space between eye and muzzle and prevent the near and far antler outlines from crossing implausibly. Save the comparison so you can return to the earlier layout.

Save and inspect the export

Save the editable original, then use the share or export entry actually visible to save a separate image. Reopen it and inspect antler tips, muzzle, visible hooves and canvas corners; return to the original and distinguish antler, body and background layers. If anything is missing, check layer visibility, crop and location before overwriting the original. Include the source and reference, and mark unperformed device and reopening checks as pending.
