Create a fictional sand-layer section distinguishing pore conditions above and below the water table.

Define the section

Draw a fictional uniform sand-layer cross-section to distinguish pore conditions above and below the water table; it is not a local geological survey, engineering design or well-drilling recommendation. The US Geological Survey describes the water table as the upper surface of the saturated zone and groundwater as water in soil and rock pores. Label the diagram “Conceptual illustration, not to scale”; do not present blue coloring as a necessarily existing underground river.

Plan canvas and text

Create an approximately 1800 by 1100 landscape canvas in VectorPaint. Use controls visible in the installed version to separate sky, sand layer, pore symbols, water-table line, legend and text; adapt dimensions to the reading context. Put the section at left and reserve the right for labels and explanation, with a title above. Save an editable original and export separate copies later so the layers remain available.

Draw surface and sand area

Draw a gently uneven ground-surface line, with one large light-brown area below it for this example’s sand layer. Leave margins and do not label the illustration’s outer frame as a real geological boundary. The surface line can run continuously across the drawing; grass and buildings are optional. Check that the area is closed, then hide the background to ensure the sand is not separate blocks with gaps.

Place the water-table line

Draw a roughly horizontal, gently sloped line inside the sand as this example’s water table. Keep it below the ground surface and connect it to the illustration’s side boundaries. The water table is the top of the saturated zone, not the ground surface or a permanently fixed container. Use a leader to label it “water table.” The chosen height is arbitrary and cannot determine a real location’s depth. Temporarily mark surface and water-table lines in different colors and inspect their endpoints, keeping the surface above the water table; restore final colors afterward and avoid accidental crossings at the edges.

Distinguish the saturated zone

Below the water-table line, overlay pale translucent blue or a light-blue fill to represent saturated pores while retaining brown sand or grain marks so water and material appear together. If the installed version lacks the transparency control you need, use a light solid color and visible grain symbols. Blue does not mean the whole lower area is an empty cave or lake; label it “zone of saturated pores.”

Show the zone above the water table

Keep the sand above the line light brown and use a few open dots as schematic pores. Label it “unsaturated zone: pores can contain water and air,” not completely dry. Both zones belong to the same hypothetical sand layer; a color change alone does not imply two different rock types. The drawn pores are much larger than real scale, consistent with the not-to-scale note.

Make a grains-and-pores key

Add spaced, not necessarily mechanically identical grain symbols in both regions: open and light-blue pores above, and light-blue dots for water-filled pores below. Put a small key on the right explaining brown as sand and blue as saturated state; the symbols do not show every actual grain. If points merge into texture at small size, remove some and improve legend text contrast. Match the open and blue dots in the key to the section. Do not let blue marks obscure the boundary or imply that the upper zone contains no water.

Mark possible precipitation recharge

Draw a downward arrow above the surface labeled “Precipitation can recharge groundwater,” ending at the ground rather than continuing through the water table like an underground pipe. The US Geological Survey explains that precipitation can recharge aquifers, but rates vary with place and materials; this image does not quantify recharge. Do not invent rainfall amount or duration without data.

Check labels and visual encoding

Follow the right-hand leaders to the surface, water table, unsaturated zone and saturated zone, ensuring the water-table label does not point at the surface. Preview in grayscale; if the zones depend on blue versus brown alone, add clear text or different symbol densities. Restore color and check that dots do not cover leaders and that text remains inside the canvas.

Troubleshoot and export

If the blue area resembles a floating river, extend it to represent saturated sand below the water table and add grain symbols and a key. If the upper region appears completely dry, correct the note to say pores can contain air and water. Save the editable original and export a separate PNG using a control visible in the installed version. Reopen it to check the water-table line, key, title and arrow for cropping and legibility at the intended size.

Compare a changed water table

Duplicate the original and lower the water-table line slightly, adjusting the saturated color and unsaturated area while leaving surface and sand extent unchanged. Label both as fictional illustrations, without claiming that a particular rainfall or pumping event necessarily caused the change. Compare at the same size and ensure labels follow the line. Keep both editable files and source records; for actual groundwater levels, consult dated local monitoring data.
