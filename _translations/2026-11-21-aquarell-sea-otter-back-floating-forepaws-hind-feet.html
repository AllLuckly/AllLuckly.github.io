Paint a sea otter floating on its back, distinguishing small forepaws, broader hind feet and the waterline. The U.S. National Park Service describes short forelimbs, large flattened hind limbs and dense fur, and shows an otter floating on its back. Pose and ripples here are drawing choices; an illustration cannot establish sex, health or the purpose of behavior.

Study sources and make a working copy

Read the source and inspect the relative locations of head, body, forepaws and hind feet, remembering that parts beneath water may not be visible. Do not pursue, approach or touch wild sea otters. Make an approximately 1800 × 1400 landscape canvas and save an editable draft, separating sketch, body, water, paws and details. Canvas size organizes the picture, not actual animal length. Follow canvas, layer and save controls visible in your installed version.

Set the floating pose with a waterline

Mark a soft horizontal waterline slightly below the middle. Tilt an elongated body oval so the head lies toward the upper left and hind feet toward the lower right, with only part of the body above water. Add a short arc for the head and connect it plausibly to the body. Leave ripples and fur until later. A thumbnail should already show head, torso and water relationship. If the animal appears to stand upright, bring the long axis closer to horizontal and adjust the waterline instead of concealing the pose with foam.

Join head and body

Use a rounded head with a short blunt muzzle, connected to the body by a short broad neck. Make a small dark nose and keep the eyes within the face rather than on its outline. Head and torso may differ in value, but should not be separated by an unexplained hard seam. Block in middle brown, then add lighter brown-gray around the face. Lighting changes apparent color; this palette is not evidence of age.

Place the two small forepaws

Put two small, slightly different dark shapes on the chest and use short marks to connect them to the visible forelimbs. One may partly obscure the other; do not force every digit into view. Each paw needs contact with the body instead of resembling a floating stone. NPS describes agile forepaws, but this static pose does not establish feeding or tool use. Hide and restore the paw layer to check the joins.

Direct broader hind feet toward the rear

At the body's right, arrange near and far hind feet as flattened, outward shapes distinct from the smaller forepaws. The near foot may cover part of the far one and water may hide some area; the chosen pose does not require two complete mirror images. Connect each foot to the body before suggesting toes with a few dark marks. If they look like a fish tail, check that there are two hind feet connected to the body rather than a single forked tail fin. A short tail can remain beneath water if the chosen angle would not show it clearly.

Separate fur and water

Keep broad dark- and mid-brown masses, adding only a few short fur strokes near the head along the body form rather than uniform stripes. Use cooler blue-gray for water, slightly darker at contact and lighter farther away. Interrupt the body contour at the waterline rather than enclosing the whole animal in black. NPS describes very dense fur; a few illustrative strokes do not represent its actual count or waterproofing.

Use a few ripples to show contact

Add two or three arcs of different widths around the body, breaking them at the animal rather than running them through the forepaws. The near ripple can be darker than the far one; avoid making every arc a concentric circle. If the otter looks pasted on, check where the waterline crosses the torso and which parts it covers before adjusting a small contact shadow. Hide the water layer to check that the body remains complete and editable; restore it to confirm only selected regions are covered.

Compare two waterline treatments

Keep two small controls in a working copy: in one let water color cover a little of the lower torso; in the other draw ripples only outside the body without covering it. Reduce both to the same size and compare which communicates contact better. Darker color alone does not prove a deeper body. In the final drawing adjust only water/body overlap, keeping head and paws fixed. If water hides a small forepaw, inspect the overlap and reduce coverage before changing body size or ripple position. Hide the controls before saving the deliverable and retain editable original layers.

Review and export

At thumbnail size, check whether small forepaws and broad hind feet are distinguishable, waterline is coherent and the head direction is clear. Save the editable draft, export a separate image through an available share or export control and open it elsewhere to inspect paws, waterline and frame. Reopen the draft and independently hide paws and water; if the export differs, check the file identity and layer visibility. Deliver the drawing, reference link and an actual operations record; mark unperformed export or reopening as pending. No physical-device or field test is claimed.
