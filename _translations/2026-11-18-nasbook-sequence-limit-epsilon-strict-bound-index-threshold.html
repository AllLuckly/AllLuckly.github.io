Mark the order of error, threshold and whole-tail guarantee

Use: for every ε>0 there exists integer N such that all n≥N have error below ε. Choose ε, then N, then guarantee the whole tail; one convenient term with small error is insufficient. Record those three quantifier positions, noting N may depend on ε rather than one fixed N serving every positive error. This convention includes n=N. If another text uses n>N, retain that distinction before comparing numeric thresholds.

Simplify exact error before reading decimals

Here a_n−1=−1/(n+1), so absolute error is 1/(n+1), with positive denominator for positive n. The first two terms 1/2 and 2/3 have errors 1/2 and 1/3, decreasing as n grows. Use this exact formula for the whole tail, not a rounded 0.99 as a limit or exact value. A two-decimal display may show 1.00 for terms still below one; equal displays establish neither equality of fractions nor an infinite-tail guarantee.

Separate ninety-nine from one hundred at the boundary

For ε=0.01, 1/(n+1)<1/100 requires n+1>100, or n>99. Integer indices therefore all qualify from n≥100. At n=99 the error is exactly 1/100, failing strict inequality. Record that failed boundary and n=100 error 1/101; later errors decrease. N=100 works under n≥N. Do not choose N=99 and silently skip its included boundary, or change < to ≤ to repair the choice.

Give a sufficient N for every positive error

For any ε>0 choose N=ceil(1/ε). Since N+1>1/ε, every n≥N has 1/(n+1)≤1/(N+1)<ε. This is algebra for the entire tail, not several sampled points. At ε=0.1, N=10 works while n=9 has error exactly 0.1. At ε=0.3, N=4 works but N=3 also works: the convenient formula need not be minimal. Existence of a usable threshold, not the smallest one, is required; larger N retains the guarantee.

For ε=0.001, old N=100 fails because its boundary error 1/101 exceeds one thousandth. N=1000 suffices: every later error is at most 1/1001, strictly below the requirement. Pair each error with its own threshold instead of assuming one very large index serves arbitrary precision. Choosing a new threshold is exactly what the definition permits.

Do not make nonattainment a universal rule for limits

Every finite n here has positive error, so a_n never equals one yet converges to it. The constant sequence b_n=1 attains its limit every time and also converges; nonattainment is not required. For c_n=(−1)^n, even terms equal one and odd terms minus one. Those infinite subsequences cannot share one limit, so it diverges. A finite beginning alone is insufficient; the given complete formula supplies this control. Separate example properties, general definition and counterexample reasoning from visual impressions of increase or oscillation.

For d_n=1+(−1)^n/(n+1), terms alternate around one but retain error 1/(n+1), the same limit and thresholds. Monotone increase is a property of a_n, not a requirement for all convergent sequences. Oscillation alone does not establish divergence; inspect whether its amplitude tends to zero rather than equate oscillation with nonshrinking error.
