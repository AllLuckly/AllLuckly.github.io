A grain-covered bench may still fail to stand: seat and backrest can miss their frame while legs point unrelated ways. Draw an unoccupied simplified wooden bench, tracing connections from slats to cross supports and from supports to legs before adding the backrest. Proportions and slat counts are invented, not production drawings or load advice. Use drawing layers to inspect perspective, overlap and negative spaces so a few lines first explain how it stands.

Begin with the seat plane, not wood grain

Use an approximately 1900×1400 px landscape canvas with a pale grey-green background. Draw a long narrow quadrilateral seat in three-quarter view, wider nearby and narrowing slightly away, with long front/rear edges sharing perspective direction. Mark ends and depth before two cross-support guides. Separate sketch, slats, frame, backrest and shadow. Inspect the seat in one mid-brown mass; if it resembles an upright sign, correct depth and tilt rather than relying on legs to explain it. Begin with a simple checkable plane instead of a complex ergonomic curve, and keep later boards within it.

Give seat slats shared boundaries and spacing

Place three or four boards lengthwise, leaving narrow gaps more visible nearby and compressed farther away. Do not give each board its own vanishing direction so it begins inside the bench and exits outside it. Align board ends first, then divide gaps. A gap reveals space below rather than an equally heavy stripe of black paint. If boards resemble round pipes, weaken individual highlights and retain flat tops. Hide grain and inspect the outer boundary against the original seat plane. Clear relationships among three boards explain structure better than ten crossing ones.

Join cross supports and legs below the seat

Place two support assemblies beneath the seat, clearer nearby and partly hidden by slats farther away. Fit cross supports against the undersides, extending legs from their frame junctions to the ground rather than adding four arbitrary vertical strokes. Establish the near front/rear legs before the far pair on the same ground. Use short contact shadows at feet; rear legs may show less but need understandable routes. If a leg seems to grow through a slat gap, correct overlap and hide its upper end behind the frame. This depicts stationary support without certifying actual furniture safety.

Build backrest posts before its slats

Raise two posts along the seat’s rear edge, sharing one chosen slight backward tilt. Attach two or three horizontal backrest boards aligned with the bench length. A visible gap may remain between backrest and seat, but it must not sever the posts. Hide backrest boards to trace the posts’ origins, then restore them and inspect support. If a wooden sign floats behind the seat, establish attachment instead of adding shadow. If it becomes a wall, reduce unintended height. The backrest pose is an illustration choice, not professional posture or body-dimension advice.

Use gaps to inspect front/rear overlap

Inspect three main negative spaces: under the seat, between legs and beneath the backrest. Show background through genuine gaps instead of filling them casually with brown. Interrupt a rear leg behind a front one rather than drawing both transparently through a support. A rear post may disappear behind boards but not vanish elsewhere without reason. Hide grain and colour variation and inspect a pale bench against a dark background. If gaps become chaotic triangles, identify each member in the sketch instead of merging all darkness into one shadow. Clear gaps let a few supports explain stability without endlessly adding braces.

Group lighting continuously across boards

Light from the upper left, keeping seat tops lighter, undersides and right frame darker, and lit backrest surfaces warm brown. Boards may differ slightly in colour without each acquiring an opposite light source. Run restrained grain lengthwise on a few near boards and simplify distance. If materials resemble a collage, reduce inter-board colour differences while retaining shared seat lighting. If everything becomes a brown wall, separate seat and back by orientation rather than whitening every gap. Detail should distinguish wood and frame without making grain louder than leg attachments; large connections remain primary.

Compare a deliberately missing support

Hide one near leg or support segment in a copy and observe which seat area begins to float, then restore the matching junction in the original. Change support alone without also changing viewpoint and shadows. This counterexample explains drawn support, not calculated structural failure. Save a preview containing only seat, back and legs, checking texture-free connections. If the original remains unclear, adjust under-seat negative space or the near-leg contour rather than hiding problems behind a seated person. People, leaves and scenery can be separate later tasks; first clarify the empty bench.

Inspect far legs and narrow gaps in output

Save the layered original and inspect far legs, narrow seat gaps and backrest-post joins in the PNG at normal size. If a far leg disappears, adjust its local background contrast rather than making it thicker than the near one. If grain becomes noise, remove detail instead of enlarging the picture to add still more lines. Keep a texture-free structural version. A later board-colour variation still needs shadow and junction checks. Record a precise fault, such as a near rear leg piercing a seat slat, to repair overlap rather than vaguely saying the bench looks unstable without locating a cause.
