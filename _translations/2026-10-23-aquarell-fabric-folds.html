Cloth folds look complex but often develop around a few support points. For fabric hanging from above, locate where it is held and how it falls before deciding the dark bands' directions. This is more reliable than drawing many arbitrary parallel stripes. Practice three main planes in single-colored fabric without patterns, letting light and dark explain the structure directly.

Establish the hanging point and outer contour

Create a 1200×1500 pixel canvas with a pale-gray background. Place a hanging point about 250 pixels below the top. Let the cloth spread downward to about 700 pixels wide at the bottom and 950 pixels tall. One side can be straighter and the other slightly curved; the hem should have two or three curves at different heights rather than a perfectly horizontal line.

On the sketch layer, mark the hanging point and three main lines radiating downward: leftward, nearly vertical, and rightward. These indicate fold directions; not all need to become black lines. Use a midtone muted purple or blue for the cloth on a separate layer, leaving separate places for background and cast shadow.

Divide the cloth into three large planes first

Use a brush around 80 to 120 pixels at high opacity to fill the entire cloth shape. With light from the upper left, make the broad left plane lighter, the central inward fold darker, and the outward-turning right plane a midtone. Vary their widths rather than making three equal vertical stripes. Tighten them near the hanging point and spread them below to follow the hanging fabric.

Paint two main dark bands with a deeper shade of the same color, narrow near the top and slightly broader below, curving before the hem. They need not extend all the way down; some can disappear midway, reconnecting the surface. From a distance, the result should show depth rather than zebra stripes. If every band is equally wide, adjust the shapes before adding color.

Distinguish inward creases from outward ridges

A narrow inward crease often has one clear edge and another that transitions gradually. Refine one side of a dark band with a small brush, then connect the other back to the cloth with a midtone. Outward bright ridges can be broad and soft rather than white lines. Different widths for light and dark planes give the cloth substance instead of carved grooves.

A small, deepest shadow may appear below the hanging point, kept to an area around 40 to 70 pixels. The shadows become less concentrated lower down. Do not darken every line around the top or it will form an unnatural black knot. For judging related edges, see Hard- and soft-edge exercise.

Make the hem follow the folds

The hem must connect to the planes above it. An outward bulge can end in a rounded downward curve, while an inward fold pulls the hem slightly upward. Add a short narrow dark section inside the hem to suggest thickness without outlining it all in black. Different heights at the left and right ends can feel more natural.

If the hem looks like lace, check whether all curves are equally wide and deep. One large curve, one medium curve, and one flatter section better express changing tension. Temporarily hide the detail layer and compare the silhouette with the main folds' directions. More internal texture cannot easily rescue an implausible outer contour.

Add texture only where appropriate

Once the structure works, add a few short texture marks on the light planes with a finer, lower-opacity brush, following the fabric's stretch. Change their direction at folds and reduce them in shadow. Cotton can appear more matte with broader light-dark transitions. For smoother fabric, narrow the bright bands and slightly increase contrast while preserving the same fold structure.

These textures are painting choices, not materials automatically recognized by the app. Change the highlight-band width in a practice copy and compare the result before adjusting the whole image. Avoid changing structure and texture simultaneously, or it becomes hard to tell which step helped. For overall opacity control, see Layer-opacity tutorial.

Compare taut and loosely hanging areas

Near the hanging point, concentrated tension creates tighter folds and denser color transitions. Farther below, the cloth has room to spread, so the planes should widen and soften. Zoom out and inspect from top to bottom to confirm that density changes. If both ends have equally wide stripes, remove a lower dark fold and join two light planes. This often conveys softness better than adding fine lines. For later practice with cloth held at two ends, locate both tension points and reorganize the directions rather than reusing this radial structure unchanged.

Use the cast shadow to place the cloth in space

If the cloth is near a wall, add a slightly offset pale-gray shadow below and to the right. Follow the outer contour with fewer details and softer edges. Keep the shadow close near the hanging point and slightly farther away below to suggest distance from the wall. It should not attract more attention than the deepest fabric crease; lower its layer opacity if needed.

Finish with three checks: do folds lead toward the hanging point, does shading follow the upper-left light, and does the hem connect with the planes? If the three main planes remain visible when reduced, stop adding texture. Save the layered original. Next time, move the hanging point to the upper left and reconsider fold directions rather than copying the old dark bands.

Why does it resemble a stiff paper fan? Common causes are uniformly sharp edges and straight, parallel boundaries between light and dark. Soften some edges, add curved turns, and let some folds disappear midway. Cloth has both a support direction and a gradual sag caused by its weight.

Can I cover poor folds with a pattern? Patterns may distract briefly, but cannot correct the forces shaping the fabric. Keep it a single color and establish the main planes first. Then let patterns bend with the planes and compress in narrow creases to add depth.
