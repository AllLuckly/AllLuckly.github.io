Manually draw two overlapping plates and one rivet, checking visible boundaries across top and side views.

Define the drawing exercise

A basic lap joint has two plates overlapping locally, with a fastener in the overlap. FAA describes adjacent sheet edges overlapping at a lap splice. This exercise draws a top and side view of two rectangular plates and one schematic rivet; it supplies no material, strength, fastener specification or dimensions for fabrication or repair. First write four objects on paper: upper plate A, lower plate B, overlap and rivet.

Set canvas and view positions

Create an approximately 1600 by 1200 pixel landscape canvas in the current VectorPaint interface and save an editable original. Reserve the upper half for the top view, lower half for the side view and a legend on the right. This is page composition, not an engineering scale. Separate plates, rivet, outlines and text with available objects or layers; use identifiable features if the interface differs. Light horizontal guides can align the views and be hidden at the end.

Draw two plates in top view

Draw a horizontal rectangle for lower plate B, then a second for upper plate A so one end of A covers part of B. Use different light colors for the exercise, but overlap is a spatial relationship rather than color mixing. The edge of the lower plate behind A should be hidden; do not draw its entire line over A. Before adding the rivet, check that each plate’s direction and overlap region can be read.

Check overlap with simple coordinates

For a check, assign B the horizontal interval x=0 to 140 and A x=80 to 220, with the same front-to-back width. Their common interval is x=80 to 140. Put the schematic rivet center at x=110 and mark that horizontal location in both top and side views. These numbers explain position only; they can be scaled consistently for the drawing and must not be used to infer real plate or fastener dimensions.

If the side view uses a different horizontal scale, label and convert it rather than connecting corresponding positions with a vertical line. For this exercise it is easiest to share a horizontal scale so both ends of the overlap and rivet center align. Vertical plate thickness may be exaggerated for readability, but say so in the legend. Hide guides when finished and check that labels still make the views understandable.

Place the rivet in the overlap

Identify the region covered by both plates and place a small circle inside it for the visible rivet head. The circle should lie fully in the common region. Add “Top view: circle is the visible rivet head,” not a hole-diameter dimension. Use one rivet so its axis is easy to match in side view. Distance from the center to an edge is an illustration choice, not a design specification.

Draw upper and lower plates in side view

Below, draw two thin long rectangles: B underneath and A above. Let their horizontal overlap represent the same region as the top view, with each extending beyond it. Use different light fills and dark outlines, but preserve upper-lower position as a distinction in grayscale. Plate thickness is schematic and can be enlarged for legibility; do not treat it as actual material thickness. Before adding a rivet, hide it and check that the bottom of A meets the top of B through the overlap.

Pass the side-view rivet through both layers

In the overlap draw a narrow vertical rectangle as a schematic rivet shank and distinguish heads above and below. Its vertical axis should correspond to the top-view circle center. In side view the shank crosses the thicknesses of both A and B rather than resting only on the upper surface. Do not present the schematic heads as a particular installation method or finished dimension. Adjust object stacking so fastener and plate boundaries remain clear; do not invent hidden details.

Connect corresponding views

Draw a light dashed guide down from the top-view circle center through the center of the side-view shank. It is an alignment aid, not a cut line, hidden edge or physical part. Label A, B and the overlap in both views, ensuring matching labels identify the same plates. If the top circle is inside overlap but the side shank falls outside it, move the shank or recheck alignment instead of slanting the guide merely to hide the error.

Make an object checklist

Beside the draft write four checks: A is upper, B is lower; they share an overlap; the top-view circle lies in it; the side-view shank crosses both layers. Hide labels first and see whether stacking, occlusion and passage remain understandable, then restore labels to verify identities. If identical gray fills make plates hard to distinguish, use line styles or spacing without inventing transparency or internal structure. This is a drawing checklist, not a structural safety inspection.

Label scope and inspect output

Label the work “schematic overlap only; not a fabrication or repair drawing” so example dimensions are not used for a real joint. Save the editable original, then use a share or export entry actually visible in the current version to save an image separately. Reopen to check both views, marks, legend and corners, and return to identify the objects in the original. If lines or labels are missing, check visibility, crop and file location before overwriting the only original. Mark device or export steps pending if unperformed.

Exclude an overhanging circle by its extent

Using the paper example’s overlap x=80 to 140, suppose the diagram circle has radius 12. A center at x=90 spans 78 to 102, extending beyond the overlap on the left; a center at x=110 spans 98 to 122 and fits inside the common range. Mark this on paper before drawing. These are composition numbers, not real rivet dimensions or edge-distance design.
