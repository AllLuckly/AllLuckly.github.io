Use public wildlife information and a side-view reference to connect horns, facial markings, legs and body color shapes.

Confirm species and reference state

Yellowstone National Park describes pronghorn horns as having a bony core and a sheath shed and regrown annually. Adult males generally have pronged horns and a black cheek patch, with reddish-tan backs and white underside and rump areas. Choose a permitted adult male side-view photograph and record its source and viewpoint. Horn-sheath growth varies, so draw what is visible rather than forcing every documented feature; do not approach or disturb wildlife.

Compose and save the original

Create an approximately 1800 by 1400 pixel landscape canvas in the current Aquarell interface and save an editable original; dimensions are compositional suggestions. Position the subject above the grass with room around horn tips and visible feet. Draw a light horizon that does not cross the eye or key horn contour. Separate sketch, background, body and details using available layers. If labels differ on the device, use identifiable functions and do not assume an automatic animal-drawing tool.

Block in torso and neck

Start with an elongated rounded torso, use a direction line for the head, and connect the neck back to the shoulder. Do not begin with tiny fur marks. Compare the height of shoulder and rump; the belly need not run exactly parallel to the horizon. Shrink the image to check that head, neck and body connect. If the head reads as a separate ball, revise the transition; if the torso reads as a rectangle, adjust chest, belly and back before drawing limbs.

Trace horns from their roots

Mark the visible roots of the two horns at the top of the head. Draw the main direction first, then add prongs and curved tips according to the chosen reference. In side view the far horn may overlap or be hidden; draw only identifiable contours, not a row of deer-antler branches. The source says the outer sheath is shed and regrown annually; do not describe the whole bony core as shedding. At small scale the horn bases should remain connected to the head.

Separate ear, eye and dark markings

Let the ears emerge from the head and distinguish their contours from horn bases before placing eye and muzzle. Use the photograph to locate the male’s dark cheek patch, suggesting it with one darker shape without covering the entire eye. Check head direction before adding the muzzle and a little inner-ear shadow. If the profile reads as frontal, revise muzzle and eye position instead of piling on lines. Leave a far ear or horn absent when it is not visible.

Give the limbs support

Locate leg origins beneath shoulder and rump, draw their main directions, then connect visible joints and feet according to the reference. A near leg may obscure a far leg; where they overlap, do not add extra lines merely to reach a count. Mark ground contact first and connect visible feet with subtle shadows. If grass hides a foot, draw the visible portion and record the occlusion rather than inventing the hidden pose.

Place back color and white areas

Block the back and torso in reddish tan or sandy brown, reserving the visible white underside and rump from the photograph. White markings follow the body surface; the amount of rump visible depends on viewpoint, so retain only the part seen in profile. Use a slightly warm or cool light tone to separate white fur from blank paper while retaining the body contour. Do not surround the whole animal with a uniform white border to represent the underside.

Compare horns and antlers

Place the sketch beside the existing moose tutorial and compare only the tops of the heads: use the photograph for the pronghorn’s main horn and prong, and the moose reference for the visible palmate antler contour. Make a small silhouette of each and compare roots and outline rather than applying one animal’s shape to the other. These exercises are not identification keys; when occlusion is severe or growth stage unknown, return to the sources and permitted photograph rather than completing it by guesswork.

Arrange the background and review

Use muted earth colors, short grass and a distant band for open ground; avoid placing dark grass behind horn tips and eye. Hide texture temporarily, leaving body shapes, horns and major color areas. Check horn roots, leg connections, foot contact and visible rump patch. Restore details, then record what was visible or hidden and the photograph’s usage rights. The illustration pose is not a wildlife observation or behavior record.

Keep before-and-after thumbnails and note one specific improvement to a horn root or foot contact. If changing photographs, record the viewpoint change and do not combine two angles into a single forced body pose.

Save and reopen

Save the editable original, then use a share or export entry visible in the current version to save a separate image. Reopen it and inspect horn tips, feet, canvas edges and white areas. Return to the original to identify background, animal and sketch. If a horn is missing, check layer visibility and cropping before overwriting the only original. Include wildlife and photographic sources; mark device, export or reopening steps as pending if unperformed.

Map overlap between horn, ear and neck

Beside the thumbnail, list three markers for horn, ear and neck in the overlap order actually visible in the photograph; mark the order unknown where it is unclear. Use three flat shapes to check whether they can be distinguished without inventing hidden parts from another photograph. If an ear looks like a horn prong, correct each connection to the head before restoring coat and face details.
