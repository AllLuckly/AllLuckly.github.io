A room diagram can explain furniture positions and the route from an entrance. Practise top-view shapes, stacking and open space in a fictional studio. Example dimensions establish proportions only; actual fitting or construction requires measurements and relevant checks.

Choose a view and proportion reference

List the entrance, window, desk, cabinet and chair, then establish which wall is at the top. Use a 1000 by 800 pixel canvas with room for a title and legend. Define grid cells as relative exercise units. Keep real measurements in a consistent unit list rather than treating pixels as metres. Label the result as a top-view layout illustration.

Draw the boundary and entrance first

Draw walls with a consistent stroke and leave a clear entrance gap. Show the door leaf and a curved swing path to explain its motion, avoiding a diagonal that resembles a permanent partition. Represent windows simply and define them in the legend. Lock the boundary layer while placing furniture so alignment operations do not move the walls.

Differentiate furniture through simple outlines

Use a rectangle and small keyboard block for a desk, a divided rectangle for a cabinet, and a seat/back pair for a chair. Keep every symbol in top view. Group each item independently. Position large furniture before small objects and retain a list of dimensions needing confirmation. Correct the footprint before adding detail when an item’s proportions look wrong.

Inspect circulation through empty space

Trace the intended route from the door to desk and cabinet, temporarily highlighting its area. Check the illustrated door swing and chair movement for overlaps. These are diagram relationships, not a certification of circulation widths. Reduce clutter or reposition large items before shrinking symbols, which would conceal a proportion problem rather than solve it.

Keep the legend and labels clear

Place symbol definitions outside the plan and use short leader lines for essential labels. Avoid crossing the entrance route or other text. Keep naming consistent and make the diagram understandable without colour. If showing measured dimensions, compare every value against the measurement list and identify its scope rather than relabelling decorative grid spacing as real measurements.

Compare two layouts at the same scale

Save the first layout and make an independent second version by repositioning the desk and cabinet. Keep room dimensions and the proportion reference unchanged. Compare equally sized exports for entrance, window side and chair clearance. Label both versions and attach a list of unresolved measurements for any discussion involving a real space.

Test a wider furniture footprint

Replace the cabinet with a wider footprint in layout B while keeping the scale and other furniture unchanged. Inspect the entrance, chair area and door direction, recording measurements needed before trying moves. Compare equal-size exports to distinguish layout changes from symbol scaling. Hide temporary route highlights before delivery and reserve real dimensional conclusions for measured evidence.

Recheck the reference when furniture seems to fit

If everything fits yet circulation seems implausible, check that furniture shares one proportion reference, especially independently scaled copies. Missing walls may be covered or hidden. Move clipped labels inside the export area. For accurate construction communication, obtain confirmed measurements and professional drawing requirements; adding dimension lines does not turn this illustration into a construction document.
