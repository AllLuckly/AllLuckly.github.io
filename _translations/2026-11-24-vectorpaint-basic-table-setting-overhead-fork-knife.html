Build a one-person place setting with independent vector objects and check utensil direction, alignment and top-view layers.

Choose one reference arrangement

One basic place setting described by Emily Post puts a napkin and fork left of the plate, a knife on the right with its cutting edge facing the plate, and a water glass above or above-right of the knife. Meals and cultures use other arrangements; this exercise depicts one example rather than a universal rule. A spoon may sit to the knife’s right if needed; omit it initially but leave room to add one.

Create a canvas and arrange objects

Create a landscape canvas in VectorPaint and save an editable original. Use available layers for sketch, plate, utensils, glass and labels. The viewpoint is directly overhead, so do not mix in a side-view glass wall. Mark the plate center and leave room around it for utensils and glass. This is an exercise layout: arbitrary document units do not automatically equal centimeters on a table.

Build the plate circles

Use the ellipse tool for a near-circle with a light fill and thin outline. Place a smaller concentric circle inside to distinguish the center from the rim. Check that both centers align and rim width looks even. A schematic need not simulate thickness or reflections. Two independent objects are easier to revise than a filled black center; after scaling, check that the outline has not become too heavy.

Place napkin and fork on the left

Draw a rectangle or lightly folded-corner napkin to the plate’s left in a color distinct from the plate. Build the fork from a narrow handle, neck and several parallel tines above the napkin. Choose the tine count for the drawing without claiming it applies to all forks. Align the handle roughly vertically and leave space between tines and plate. Hide the napkin to ensure the fork silhouette remains clear.

Draw knife and cutting edge

Use an elongated path for handle and blade to the plate’s right, with the cutting edge facing the plate. The edge may be hard to distinguish in an overhead diagram, so use a thin inner line or short label without making it look like two blades. Compare the bottom of the knife handle with the fork handle. If they differ, consult the chosen arrangement before moving everything to an identical coordinate.

Draw the glass rim above the knife

Draw a smaller circle for the top-view water-glass rim and an inner circle to suggest its wall. Place it above or above-right of the knife, clear of the knife tip and plate. The two circles represent one rim, not two glasses. Avoid mixing in a rectangular side-view glass. If a pale inner circle suggests water, explain that it is a top-view illustration rather than a capacity scale.

Check stacking and clearance

Inspect the order from background to foreground: table, napkin, fork, plate, knife and glass. The fork should appear above the napkin, whose edge should not run visibly through it. Keep objects independent; group napkin and fork for movement if useful without deleting the originals. Shrink the view to check separation. If objects merge, increase clearance before adding decorative outlines.

Add names and orientation

Use the text tool to label plate, fork, knife and water glass. Short leader lines can point to each object but should not obscure the knife edge or fork tines. State the inward-facing knife edge in the caption rather than asking readers to infer it from color. Check every leader endpoint, especially that the glass label does not point to the plate rim. Judge type at the intended output size, not only while zoomed in.

Compare the setting with and without a spoon

Duplicate the setting as a second variant and add a simplified top-view spoon to the right of the knife, checking space around the glass. Keep the first without a spoon. Label whether each version includes one and explain that utensils depend on the meal. Compare both at the same scale so the effect on spacing is clear, then keep the version appropriate for the intended use.

Troubleshoot and export

If the picture resembles several concentric plates, check glass size and position. If knife orientation is unclear, zoom in and compare the inner edge line with its label. Save the editable original, hide the sketch in a copy, and export SVG, PDF or PNG through a control available in the current version. Reopen it to inspect direction, text, crop and layering. A PNG preserves appearance, not editable objects. Record the reference arrangement and unperformed print steps without claiming a real table was set.

Try a label-free recognition check

Temporarily hide the title and labels. Identify napkin, fork, plate and knife from left to right, then find the glass. This is a repeatable self-check, not a claim that another person tested it. If an object is recognizable only by its label, revise the fork tines, knife edge or glass rim, then restore the labels. Check that the caption does not present one optional arrangement as a mandatory rule for every region and meal, and save the final copy. Also inspect landscape and portrait copies: the caption may sit below in the landscape version or beside the setting in the portrait version, but retain the fork-left, knife-right and glass-above-knife relationships. After changing layout, check leader lines separately for overlap before exporting either version, and keep the editable original.
