A mirror in a comic can hide a character, reveal an action behind someone or simply decorate a room. When reflection and subject appear differently oriented, establish the mirror boundary, viewpoint and object identity before interpreting the story. Use ordinary page navigation, zoom and external notes. This does not assume mirror measurement, automatic spatial reconstruction or character recognition in ManYue, and does not treat stylized art as an engineering drawing.

Choose a short passage showing subject and mirror

Choose two or three pages you may read, preferably showing a mirror frame, the person and a stable marker such as a one-sided pocket or wall clock. Record work, edition, chapter, file position and panel location, rather than keeping only a crop of the reflected face. Without a suitable work, draw three panels: a person faces a mirror, raises a star-marked sheet, then turns away. Number the panels. The drawing practices description; it cannot prove the structure of an author’s real scene. Retain neighbouring panels to determine whether the viewpoint changes or the same action simply continues.

Identify the mirror area before guessing reversal

On the first pass, describe visible boundaries only: which line resembles a mirror frame, how the reflected area differs from the wall, whether a person blocks the frame and whether a second mirror appears. If unclear, write possible mirror rather than treating a reflective window, photograph or screen as established. Record which objects are clearly inside, outside or crossing the boundary. If a reflective surface has no drawn frame, use adjacent panels and actions while retaining uncertainty. Establishing these layers provides the basis for comparing a subject with its reflection.

Name the reference for every left or right

Keep page-left, the character’s own left and scene-left distinct in your notes; do not use scene-left when the scene direction has not been defined. A star appearing on the page’s left does not establish that the person holds it in the left hand: the person may face the reader, face away or appear in reflection. Attach a reference to each description, such as the hand near page-right inside the mirror frame, rather than simply right hand. Reflection is not adequately described as swapping the whole page left-to-right. Do not horizontally flip the original and call it the correct version, disrupting dialogue and panel order.

Use distinctive markers to match subject and reflection

On the second pass, track a distinctive marker: the star-marked sheet’s shape, a one-sided pocket, hair clip or tear in clothing. Establish that it belongs to the same object before recording its position in the subject and reflection. If an extra figure appears in the mirror, do not immediately call it an art error. Check whether an earlier panel places someone behind the subject or a later one reveals another person. With no match, record unidentified object. Zoom helps inspect visible markers but cannot recover occluded content. Preserve features too blurred to identify as unknown instead of substituting the plot you expect.

Separate viewpoint changes from the subject turning

List mirror frame, doorway and character positions by panel number, checking whether the background changes orientation as a whole. A cut from behind the person to beside the mirror can change page directions without the person turning. Record a turn only when body orientation, action or dialogue supports it; viewpoint movement and turning can also occur together. Use three columns—event, evidence and remaining uncertainty—and record viewpoint movement separately from bodily action. Do not compress every change into a single arrow: on review, that would conceal which part came from visible evidence and which part you inferred.

Retain visual evidence when discussing narrative purpose

Only after the spatial record is complete should you describe what extra information the reflection supplies: a person behind the subject, an otherwise hidden expression or a recurring act of self-examination. Tie interpretation to specific panels and markers. A mirror alone does not establish a double identity. If dialogue and reflection suggest different things, record their scopes separately rather than forcing one to be the truth. You may offer two candidate readings and specify later evidence that supports or rules them out. Not every mirror needs a concealed answer; ordinary spatial orientation can be a complete finding.

Expand context instead of filling unseen details

If frame or marker remains unclear after zooming, return to the whole page to check whether an adjacent panel edge or decorative line was mistaken for a frame, then inspect neighbouring pages. Restore a suitable whole-page view without treating a layout change as a change in the work. If evidence remains insufficient, stop and record resolution, cropping or occlusion limits. Do not alter the original, draw missing fingers or use a generated picture as evidence. A suspected mirrored scan requires combined checks of text direction, page numbers and a reliable original, not merely an assumption about a character’s preferred hand.

Leave and relocate from the record

Leave the reader and return, locating the same mirror from edition, position and panel number. Read the observation record without its interpretation first, then review your analysis. Confirm that every left/right description names its reference, every identity judgement has a distinctive marker and no unknown quietly becomes fact in the summary. Another reader may review a description you are allowed to share and identify positions they cannot locate; sending a restricted full page is unnecessary. The result is a traceable spatial-evidence note, not a physics score for the comic or proof of automatic mirror analysis in the app.
