Declare dimensions and one unit scale

Declare an 84-wide, 30-high rectangle with lower-left origin. On an 1800×1200 canvas, scale 15 gives displayed width 1260 and height 450, leaving dimension space. Take two size-thirty squares first

84÷30 gives quotient 2 and remainder 24. Place square A at x=0..30 and B at x=30..60, both y=0..30. The right remainder spans x=60..84 and y=0..30: width 24 and height 30. Take a size-twenty-four square from the remainder

In the 24-wide, 30-high remainder, 30÷24 gives quotient 1 and remainder 6. Place square C at x=60..84,y=0..24. The remaining upper strip spans x=60..84,y=24..30, width 24 and height 6. The remainder changes from vertical strip to horizontal band, while algorithm inputs change from 84/30 to 30/24. Its horizontal boundary belongs only in the right remainder; extending it across A/B would silently split them and change the seven-piece count.

Divide the height-six strip into four squares

24÷6 gives quotient 4, remainder 0. Divide the band into D,E,F,G with x ranges 60..66,66..72,72..78,78..84 and y=24..30. Each is 6×6; four widths total 24 and close the remainder. Zero means the division is exact, not a reason to draw a zero-width square or seek another nonzero strip. Label each size once if four-edge repetition crowds the diagram; a clear dimension ledger is easier to audit.

Match quotients two, one, four to seven pieces

Write 84=2×30+24, 30=1×24+6 and 24=4×6+0. Quotients 2,1,4 mean two size-30, one size-24 and four size-6 squares, summing to seven. They do not mean seven size-six squares, and multiplying quotients does not give eight pieces. Last nonzero remainder 6 is gcd(84,30): 84/6=14 and 30/6=5. Those fourteen and five are rectangle dimensions in units of six, not this greedy tiling’s piece counts.
