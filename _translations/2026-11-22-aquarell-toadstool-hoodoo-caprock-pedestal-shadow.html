Use public geology information and a permitted photograph to practice a caprock, supporting pedestal and rock-layer values.

Choose the landform and reference

The US National Park Service describes a toadstool-like hoodoo as resistant rock above a more readily eroded pedestal. Choose a permitted photograph with a clear contact between caprock and column, and record location, source and usage rights. Shapes vary with location and weathering; this illustration is not a geological measurement or timeline. Do not climb, touch or move fragile formations.

Plan the composition and save

Create an approximately 1600 by 2000 pixel portrait canvas in the current Aquarell interface and save an editable original. This size is a compositional choice. Lightly mark caprock top, pedestal base and ground, leaving room on both sides of the overhanging cap. Separate sketch, sky, rock and shadow using available layers. If labels vary by device, use identifiable functions and do not assume an automatic landform tool.

Block the pedestal first

Use a slightly irregular center axis for the pedestal and draw its side contours from the photograph. It may widen or narrow toward either end or contain local bulges; do not force a perfect cylinder. Reserve a contact boundary at the ground so the column appears supported. Ignore texture initially and reduce the view to check the main direction and top position.

Place the cap on the pedestal

Draw the caprock’s overall outline, observing its thickness and how far it projects on each side. Mark where its underside actually meets the pedestal. The cap can be asymmetric but should not float with an unintended gap. Where the reference exposes its underside, use a slightly darker shape to show thickness; omit unseen areas. Establish contact before cracks rather than hiding the boundary under dense lines.

Distinguish layers and rock faces

Observe visible horizontal or sloping layers in the photograph. Block the pedestal in a few bands and bend their boundaries with the rock surface rather than drawing ruler-straight lines through empty space. The cap and pedestal may differ in color or value, but color alone does not establish a specific rock type. If the source does not identify it, use an observable description such as “darker cap” rather than naming minerals yourself.

Arrange lit and shaded faces

Use the reference’s main light direction to block lit faces in a lighter warm value and shaded faces in a darker cooler value. The underside of the cap may be darker than the pedestal top, but follow the photograph. Large shapes should convey volume before outlines and cracks; a single brown fill with hard outlines may look flat. If so, hide cracks temporarily and adjust the areas and boundary of light and shadow before restoring them.

Anchor the ground shadow

Start the cast shadow at the pedestal’s contact with the ground, with direction consistent with the reference light and other shadows. Use a simple shape for the darker contact area, then decide whether the far end is lighter; every rock fragment need not be described. If sunlight direction cannot be determined, treat the shadow as a compositional choice rather than a field observation. Keep shadow, pedestal and ground in contact so the formation does not float.

Compare contact in two thumbnails

Duplicate the sketch into two studies: one with cap underside meeting the pedestal and one deliberately leaving a gap. Keep pedestal and background the same. Compare which matches the supporting relationship in the reference and continue with it. This teaches overlap; it does not suggest the cap actually floated or moved. If an overly dark underside hides contact, lighten that local area rather than drawing a bright separation line through the cap.

Add restrained surface detail

Add a few short marks or shapes along cracks and layers visible in the reference. Avoid perfectly identical spacing or direction across the whole surface. Distant background spires can use lighter, lower-contrast outlines rather than repeating the exact cap silhouette. Hide details temporarily and inspect cap, pedestal and ground shapes. Restore restrained texture only if contact and shadow still read; do not invent the hidden back surface.

Check sky and cap edges

Choose a sky value distinct from the caprock near its outline so both overhanging edges remain legible. Preserve some background around the narrow pedestal instead of filling every gap with an equally colored distant wall. Compare the photograph to distinguish a real gap from a contact area with similar colors. If the background must be dark, lighten the cap edge locally rather than drawing a uniform white border around every rock.

Save and inspect the output

Save the editable original, then use a share or export entry actually visible in the current version to save a separate image. Reopen and inspect cap edges, pedestal base, shadow and canvas corners; return to identify rock, background and sketch layers. If the cap is cropped or shadow missing, check visibility and file location before overwriting the only original. Include geology and photographic sources, and mark device, export or reopening steps pending if unperformed.

Compare caprock and pedestal with a proportion bar

Draw a horizontal proportion bar beside the reference and thumbnail. Treat the widest visible caprock span as one unit and estimate how much of that unit the narrowest pedestal occupies. Compare the two and adjust only the mistaken thumbnail contour. If the reference crops an edge, do not estimate its hidden width. The bar describes this view’s picture relationships, not a field measurement of the landform.
