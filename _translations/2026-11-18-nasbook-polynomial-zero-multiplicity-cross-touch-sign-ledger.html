A curve meeting the horizontal axis need not cross, and a repeated factor does not add a distinct location. Fix a nonzero real polynomial on the whole real line

Externally define f(x)=x(x−2)² on all real numbers. It is written as a polynomial with no denominator restriction and is not identically zero. Make P1 factored/expanded forms, P2 signs/sketch and P3 controls, exporting a readable PDF and retaining editable work. Check that the square applies to the whole x−2 and bookmark P2. Highlight factors if text controls permit; otherwise record the entire expression externally. Do not delete the square to force a crossing or confuse the lesson number with polynomial degree.

Separate distinct zeros from multiplicity at each

The product vanishes at x=0 or x=2. Factor x occurs once, giving multiplicity one at zero; x−2 occurs twice, giving multiplicity two at two. There are two distinct real zeros but total multiplicity three, matching this cubic because its complete factorization is real here. This does not say every cubic has three distinct real zeros. Give location, multiplicity and total degree separate columns, not two overlapping markers at x=2 portrayed as independent intercepts.

Use nonzero samples to check interval signs

Below zero, x is negative and its squared factor positive, giving negative f. Between zero and two and above two, f is positive. Samples −1,1,3 give −9,1,3 as substitutions, not a proof from three isolated samples. Whole-interval signs follow fixed nonzero factor signs. Put f(0)=f(2)=0 at boundaries rather than inside positive intervals. A sketch illustrates the algebra without replacing exact factor evidence.

Cross at zero but retain the same sign near two

Near zero the square stays positive and x changes negative to positive, so the graph crosses. Near two, choose 0<δ<2: f(2−δ)=(2−δ)δ²>0 and f(2+δ)=(2+δ)δ²>0, with only the middle zero. It touches and returns to the same side. Both locations yield zero; their nearby signs distinguish them. Mark both intercepts but label negative-to-positive versus positive-to-positive. An intersection with the axis need not mean crossing it.

For δ=0.1, f(1.9)=0.019 and f(2.1)=0.021, both positive but unequal. Even-multiplicity contact does not require global mirror symmetry about x=2. A convenient U-like sketch only illustrates local touching; its symmetry cannot create a nonexistent global axis for the polynomial.

Retain the nonzero remaining factor in the parity rule

At a real-polynomial zero r write f(x)=(x−r)^m h(x), with h(r)≠0. Continuity keeps h’s sign fixed sufficiently nearby, so odd m changes sign and even m does not. If h(r)=0, multiplicity was not fully extracted. Control g(x)=x³ has multiplicity three and still crosses, though flatter than a simple root; at ±0.1 it gives ±0.001. A flat-looking tangent alone cannot prove even multiplicity. Parity comes from sign behaviour; exact multiplicity still needs algebraic information.

Changing the overall sign preserves zeros and multiplicities

Multiplying all of f by −1 preserves both zeros and multiplicities, but reverses negative-to-positive crossing and moves the touch near two below the axis. Odd still crosses and even still touches; touching need not occur from above. Multiplying by five rescales vertically without making a double root multiplicity ten. Keep controls separate from the original formula/sample values: −f(1)=−1 and 5f(1)=5, not the original positive one.

Unlike multiplying by a constant, squaring the whole function gives q=f²=x²(x−2)^4, with the same zeros but multiplicities two/four and degree six. Neither crosses; q is positive elsewhere and zero at those points. Unchanged location does not imply unchanged multiplicity, and squaring is not a constant vertical rescaling like multiplying by five.

Troubleshoot factor scope and domain

Two different markers for zero two confuse location and multiplicity. A flat-looking graph calls for the x³ control. If the material becomes x(x−2)²/(x−2), the original domain excludes two; cancellation to x(x−2) cannot restore that prohibited point as a zero. This is no longer the original all-real polynomial.
