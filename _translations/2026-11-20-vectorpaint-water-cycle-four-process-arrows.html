Make a simplified water-cycle graphic with sea, cloud and slope as references and arrows for evaporation, condensation, precipitation and surface runoff. The U.S. Geological Survey describes atmospheric moisture, surface water, precipitation, runoff and other processes. This graphic selects only a few paths; it does not claim each drop must follow four fixed steps or that other processes do not exist.

List four processes and the diagram's scope

Write “ocean evaporation”, “atmospheric condensation”, “precipitation onto land” and “surface runoff toward lower ground” on paper to check what each label refers to. Create an approximately 2000 × 1400 landscape canvas, reserving sea at lower left, slope at lower right and cloud above. This is not a map or specific scale. Group background, sea, slope, cloud, arrows and labels and save. The four processes are an exercise scope, not the whole cycle; arrow length is neither water volume nor rate.

Build sea and hillside outlines

Use a relatively level blue plane for sea and an earth-green shape descending from the right toward water. Make their contact clear so surface runoff does not point into an unexplained white gap. Keep the upper background light enough for cloud and labels. Use closed shapes or paths with nodes adjusted through controls actually available in the installed version. If a hillside curve crossing sea level makes accidental fragments, fix contour and stacking rather than cover breaks with text.

Place cloud and a condensation note

Place a simplified cloud above sea and slope, building a soft outline from two or three arcs or combined shapes. Attach the “condensation” label to a nearby explanation region: atmospheric vapor can condense into tiny liquid droplets. The visible cloud is not a blue pipe extending directly from the sea. The cloud is symbolic, not a required real cloud type or forecast. Leave room around its label for the precipitation arrow; do not depict all gaseous water vapor as visible blue droplets.

Give evaporation and precipitation different directions

Draw a thin upward arrow above the sea labeled “evaporation” and a separate downward arrow from below the cloud toward land labeled “precipitation”. Leave space at starts and arrowheads so they remain legible against cloud and ground outlines. Different line styles or symbols can supplement labels, without relying solely on color. If the rising arrow looks like a physical water pipe, shorten it and leave a small gap above the sea to communicate process direction rather than a literal connection.

Place surface runoff along the slope

Above the slope draw an arrow from higher ground toward lower ground and ultimately toward water, labeled “surface runoff”. Follow the slope rather than passing through the mountain and rising from the sea. If space requires a bent line, preserve the direction of each segment. This schematic does not claim all precipitation becomes surface runoff or that groundwater has been drawn. Keep arrow and terrain layers separate so one can be adjusted without redrawing the hill.

Use a terrain copy without arrows to inspect sea and slope outlines, then restore arrows and identify each start region and arrowhead. If runoff crosses the descending precipitation arrow, separate them or change the bend; the crossing does not automatically become a water confluence. Keep a monochrome copy for direction and label checks so the meanings remain understandable without color.

Keep line weight, labels and space consistent

Unify main arrow widths and arrowhead sizes. A more distant process line may be slightly thinner if it remains readable. Use consistent label hierarchy so “condensation” does not exceed the title. Cloud, sea and slope planes serve the content; realistic texture is unnecessary. Match each of four names to its arrow or position: condensation is explained near the cloud, not an unexplained arrowhead for evaporation. Reduce the preview and rearrange crowded labels and whitespace rather than deleting labels and leaving only colored arrows.

Use a process list to prevent false conclusions

Beside the graphic make four lines: evaporation transfers liquid water to the atmosphere; condensation forms tiny liquid droplets; precipitation falls from atmosphere to surface; surface runoff travels over land toward lower ground. The drawing shows one simplified combination. Transpiration, groundwater, ice and snow are omitted here, not absent from nature. Do not add percentages without data or estimate stored volume from colored areas. In teaching, retain the simplified-scope note instead of using closed arrows to imply a single mandatory route.

Save the source and inspect an output

Save editable groups, hide temporary guides and export SVG or PNG through an entry actually available in the installed version; open it elsewhere. Check four labels, arrow directions, crop and monochrome legibility. PNG is raster, while an SVG containing bitmaps is not automatically all vector. Reopen the source and move one arrow to check independent editability. Mark unperformed export, reopening or device steps pending. The graphic is not a forecast of local weather, flooding or water supply.
