Use public bird information and a permitted side-view photograph to practice the heron’s neck connection, blue-gray body and waterline.

Choose a reference and viewpoint

The US National Park Service describes great blue herons as having long necks and legs, blue-gray bodies and yellow bills; adults can have white heads and dark plumes. Choose a permitted side-view wading photograph with body and waterline visible, and record source, usage rights and viewpoint. Neck pose and lighting vary, so draw what the photograph shows. Do not approach nests, chase birds or enter unsafe water.

Reserve vertical space and save

Create an approximately 1400 by 2000 pixel portrait canvas using the current Aquarell interface and save an editable original; the ratio is a suggestion. Leave space in front of the long bill, above the head and below the water. Lightly mark waterline and body position. Separate sketch, sky and water, bird and detail with available layers. If labels differ, use the actual interface and do not assume an automatic feather tool.

Locate the neck from the body

Block the torso as a slightly tilted oval and mark the shoulder and neck emerging from its front. If the photograph shows a curved neck, plan an S-shaped center line and draw thickness on both sides. Another pose may be straighter or more curved; do not force every reference into one shape. At small size, check that neck flows into chest rather than appearing as a detached line stuck to the body.

Connect head, pointed bill and eye

Place the head at the top of the neck and block the yellow pointed bill as a long narrow triangle, adjusting its attachment and direction. Place the visible eye on the head in the direction suggested by the reference. Use small value shapes for the adult’s light head and dark plume where visible; do not invent detailed patterns in a distant image. Keep space before the tip so cropping does not make the bill appear short.

Carry legs from the body through the waterline

Locate both leg origins beneath the torso from the reference, draw visible long portions and decide how the waterline occludes them. A near leg may hide the far leg, and submerged portions may be unclear. Use a lighter color, broken line or omit them according to what is seen; do not force both legs to the bottom. If legs appear to float in front of the body, trace their connection before adding ripples.

Separate body and folded wing

Use blue-gray and darker gray to block the torso and the visible wing against its side; observe large overlapping shapes before individual feathers. In a standing pose the wing is generally folded, so do not attach an outstretched wing from an earlier flight study just to show span. Transition between the lighter neck and blue-gray body, using the reference for visible wing-tip, tail and torso boundaries.

Arrange water and foot contact

Use muted horizontal blue-gray shapes for the surface, keeping the waterline continuous in front of the legs. If a foot touches a visible shallow bottom, suggest that contact with a small shadow. Reflections can be a few short roughly horizontal highlights, not lines crossing the exposed legs. If the photograph shows a reflection, suggest it beneath the bird as a paler broken shape without copying every feather; omit it when not visible.

Compare two waterline heights

Duplicate the sketch into two thumbnails, one with a lower waterline on the legs and one slightly higher, keeping head, body and leg direction unchanged. Compare visible leg sections, occlusion and reflections, then choose the one closest to the reference. These are compositional variants, not evidence of a real water-level change. If the waterline rises, a hidden foot should not remain equally clear. Save the comparison before revising.

Review neck and negative space at small size

Hide fine feathers, grass and ripples, leaving the bird, bill, long neck, legs and water shapes. Check that bill, eye, neck and torso agree in direction, legs enter the water from the right places, and there is space above the head and before the bill. Restore a few feather marks and ensure they do not break the neck into disconnected pieces. Mark plant-obscured contours as not visible in your notes.

Check background and bird layering

Reeds, shore or dark water in a wading photograph can meet the bird’s legs. Pick one or two clear boundaries and adjust background value slightly so the visible legs can be followed from the body to the waterline, retaining natural occlusion elsewhere. Do not turn the whole background white or outline the entire bird in the same thick black. Compare before-and-after thumbnails at the same size and record whether the change was to the background or bird.

Save and inspect the output

Save the editable original, then use a share or export entry actually visible in the current version to save a separate image. Reopen it to inspect bill tip, head, legs, waterline and corners; return to identify bird, background and sketch layers. If a leg or waterline is missing, check visibility, crop and location before overwriting the only original. Keep public and photographic sources, and mark device, export and reopening steps pending if unperformed.

Use negative space inside the neck to check the pose

In a duplicate thumbnail, temporarily fill the visible background gap between the curved neck and chest with a single color. Compare its height and opening direction with the reference. If the gap disappears, check whether the neck was accidentally joined to the body or is genuinely obscured in the photograph; do not carve out an identical shape in every pose. Remove the temporary fill after checking and retain the actually visible contour.
