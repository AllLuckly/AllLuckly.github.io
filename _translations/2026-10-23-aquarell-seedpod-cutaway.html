Observational illustration should make structure understandable, not merely create attractive surface texture. An open pod contains a shell, inner wall, seeds, and attachment points, making it a good exercise in deciding which edges must be clear and which details can be omitted. This article draws a simplified bean-pod-like structure, focusing on seed arrangement rather than treating the diagram as a strict botanical identification plate.

Decide whether you are drawing from observation or imagination

Use a pod photo you took yourself or observe a familiar food item. First look at the shell's length, the shape of its ends, the opening direction, and the number of seeds. Without a real specimen, explicitly treat it as a structural exercise rather than casually assigning a species name. Recording three features you actually observe is more useful than drawing a row of identical spheres from memory.

Create a 1600×1000-pixel landscape canvas with a pale beige background. Place the pod diagonally from lower left to upper right, about 1200 pixels long and 350 pixels at its widest. Draw its outline and centerline on a sketch layer, then separate the shell, inner wall, and seeds in the finished painting. Add fine lines last.

Establish shell thickness and the opening first

Block in the complete shell with gray-green, then draw a narrow strip opening outward along the upper side to reveal the lighter interior. Taper both ends instead of making two perfectly parallel rectangles. Make the opening widest in the middle and narrower at the ends so the seeds appear enclosed. Suggest shell thickness with a slightly darker narrow edge, without a black outline all the way around.

Make the rear shell slightly darker and cooler than the inner wall, which can be pale yellow-green or beige. Add a short shadow near the fold to show the shell turning outward. Hide the sketch and check whether the opening reads clearly. If it looks like two leaves side by side, the inner wall may be insufficiently distinct or the folded edge may lack thickness. Correct those relationships first.

Arrange seeds along a curve rather than a straight line

Start with five to seven seeds of slightly different sizes, larger in the middle and smaller at the ends. Their centers should follow the curved pod centerline, with spacing that need not be identical. Each seed can be a slightly flattened ellipse rather than a repeated perfect circle. Block in the shapes with medium green, then check that they all remain within the inner wall.

Nearby seeds overlap, so a rear seed may show only most of its outline. Do not draw every outline in full, making the seeds appear to pass through one another. Leave small gaps between seeds and the inner wall, or add small contact shadows to show their separate volumes. Establish order through arrangement and overlap before considering surface texture.

Each seed needs only a little light and shadow

With light from the upper left, each seed is lighter at the upper left and darker at the lower right. Add a broad light-green area and a small dark-green area, concentrating the deepest shadow where the seed touches the inner wall. Do not give every seed an identical white highlight; plant surfaces can be softer. Add slightly more detail to the central seed and simplify those at the ends.

Shadows between seeds should resemble a darker version of the inner-wall color rather than pure black. Their width changes with the gap: narrow and dark at close contact, fading farther away. For organizing spherical values, see Hatched sphere exercise. Although this exercise uses color shapes, the relationships among light, shadow, and contact shadow are the same.

Connections help explain the growth structure

Beside visible seeds, add very short connecting lines or small stalks leading to the same side of the inner wall. Keep them thin, short, and subdued rather than making thick roots that entangle all the seeds. If the reference does not clearly show the connection, retain only small indicative marks rather than inventing a complex structure. Credible observational illustration depends on knowing which information has a basis.

Add two or three pale lengthwise markings on the outer shell while keeping the inner wall smoother. Different brushwork helps distinguish outside from inside. Use detail lines about 5 to 10 pixels wide with reduced opacity. Do not texture every area; preserve clean regions so the seed arrangement remains clear.

Support readability with space and small marks

Leave enough space around the pod so background plants do not compete with its structure. If explanation is needed, include an exported image in the article and describe the shell, inner wall, and seeds in text; do not force an in-app text tool into this exercise. If writing on the canvas, keep labels away from important edges and use thin lines pointing to specific locations.

Zoom out to check whether the opening direction reads immediately, then zoom in to ensure the seeds are not floating outside the shell. Hide sketch and color-test layers and export a PNG with the light background. For judging space around edges, see Negative-space branch exercise; the gaps here help separate the seeds.

Common problems and a second observation

Why does it look like grapes resting on a leaf? Common causes are overly round or numerous seeds and a shell that does not enclose them. Arrange the seeds in one row along the centerline, reduce overlap, and strengthen the thickness on both sides of the opening. Connect the shell and seeds structurally before adding surface color.

Must a diagram be perfectly symmetrical? No. Real plants often differ naturally, but differences should come from observation or an explicit simplification rather than random distortion. For the next drawing, place a closed pod beside an open pod at the same length and observe how the exterior silhouette relates to the interior arrangement.

If you plan to label the illustration, record observed features separately from your simplifications. For example, if the seed count comes from the specimen but the colors are adjusted, describe the arrangement and opening without presenting the artistic palette as a species characteristic. Readers can then understand the image's purpose, and you can compare it with a new reference next time.
