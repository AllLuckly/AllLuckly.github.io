A large number alone does not establish hyperbole: inspect the story world, speaker and surrounding actions. Read an original short passage in Book Isle and keep three evidence columns. Emotional wording must not rewrite the event timeline, and the reading app is not an automatic classifier of rhetorical figures.

Preserve the speaker and a recoverable location

Create an editable exercise passage: “Xiaolin entered the station at nine. The train arrived at three minutes past nine. He complained, I have waited here for three hundred years. Then he picked up his bag and boarded.” Open it through an existing import workflow, retain the original and identify a chapter or opening-word anchor. The clock times belong to narration; three hundred years belongs to the complaint. Keep them out of the same objective-duration field. If selection is unavailable, use external notes rather than repeatedly converting the only file or inventing a hidden annotation control.

Identify the quantity being exaggerated

The Poetry Foundation hyperbole entry describes conspicuous exaggeration. Circle “three hundred years”, identify waiting duration as the enlarged quantity, and record impatience or difficulty waiting as possible effects. The source supports terminology, not a proven inner state or a rule classifying every sentence containing three hundred. Keep “possible” in the interpretation column. Do not infer an exact emotion, cause or physical condition absent from the passage; this exercise concerns reading language.

Build an independent event timeline

Extract only arrival-at-station and train-arrival times from passage A. Nine to three minutes past nine is three minutes; calculate before recording. Picking up the bag and boarding has no exact timestamp, so three minutes cannot describe the entire journey through taking a seat. Keep the complaint beside the timeline with quotation marks and speaker identity. A compressed excerpt must not become the factual summary “Xiaolin waited three hundred years.” If another version only says “after a while”, record unknown duration instead of inventing three minutes.

Add a potentially literal counterexample

Write passage B separately: “The hibernation chamber closed in 2000 and opened in 2300. Its occupant’s record read: I have waited here for three hundred years.” This is an invented setting; unusual real-world experience does not automatically make its claim hyperbolic. The stated years span three hundred years within that world. Distinguish elapsed external time from subjective experience: continuous wakefulness is unstated. The final sentence matches A, but its evidence differs. Keep separate passage identities and never merge both worlds into one experience.

Compare a deliberately neutral paraphrase

In a note copy, paraphrase A’s complaint as “It feels as if I have waited a long time”, clearly marking it as your wording and retaining the original. This preserves felt length but loses the extreme three-hundred-year scale and rhetorical force; it does not supply three minutes. “He waited three minutes” preserves a clock fact while removing expression. Both compressions lose a different layer and neither is the complete quotation. Label factual summary, tonal interpretation and direct excerpt so identical typography in an export does not erase their roles.

Keep rhetorical reading separate from deception claims

Ask whether the speaker is deceiving anyone. The hyperbolic line alone cannot settle this: both parties may recognise a complaint, and the passage gives no listener response. Record “strong exaggeration cues; deceptive intent unstated” instead of automatically calling the character a liar. A separate passage explicitly describing forged dates for eligibility would supply different behavioural evidence. It would not make every hyperbole a forgery. Add conclusions supported by a recoverable passage rather than turning a rhetorical label into an unsupported moral judgement.

Troubleshoot missing qualifications in excerpts

If a note retains only “three hundred years”, restore the speaker, clock times and boarding action from A. For an incorrect reading of B, revisit dates and world assumptions rather than deleting the number. When search finds both identical sentences, distinguish chapter and opening words; reaching B does not mean A was corrected. Check whether your quotation marks accidentally absorb the narrator’s clock statements. A failed return remains a locating failure, not verification from memory. Repair file navigation separately from semantic interpretation. Label units too: minutes belong to the event record and years to the complaint. A conversion-based exaggeration factor is not a measured strength of emotion.

Reopen and retain three evidence columns

Leave reading, reopen the same exercise file and recover both passages by their anchors. Before viewing old judgements, fill in literal events, original speech, interpretation and unknowns. A’s three minutes and three hundred years can coexist in separate fields; retain B’s dated span too. Attach your neutral paraphrase labelled as non-original and ensure originals and earlier excerpts survive. Success means another reader can reconstruct what was exaggerated and why A’s conclusion cannot simply be applied to B, rather than permanently assigning an automatic rhetorical label.
