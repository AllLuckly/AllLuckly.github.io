Use grids, snapping and guides to control margins and positions while retaining necessary optical adjustments, and keep guides out of the finished artwork.

Decide which reference you need

Grids support repeated spacing, guides mark specific centres or boundaries, and snapping draws objects towards references. Related, they are not interchangeable. On a 640×640 px canvas, prepare a main circle and two informational colour blocks. Aim for order, not forcing every detail onto one grid line.

Showing a grid does not enable snapping

Show the grid and move the subject, then enable grid snapping separately. Display gives visual guidance; snapping affects placement. With display alone, objects may stop between lines. Invisible snapping can make movement feel jumpy. Knowing both states speeds diagnosis.

Define the content area with margins

Begin with about 64 canvas units around the edges, one-tenth of the width. Use fixed guides if available, or estimate through grid cells. References support composition, not the sacrifice of readable titles. Distinguish canvas edges from content edges.

Stabilise the subject with a centre reference

Place the main image near centre and compare side and vertical spaces. Text also carries visual weight, so the whole subject need not sit at geometric centre. Move the image slightly up if needed. A centre reference distinguishes intentional offset from an accidental drag.

Refine the grid only for necessary details

Coarse grids build structure; fine grids refine spacing, but density is not professionalism. On a 640-wide canvas, try 32-unit modules and refine only where needed, within available controls. Forced snapping may worsen curves or typography; temporarily disable it for optical adjustments.

Distinguish guides from real line objects

A line-tool stroke is artwork and normally exports; editor guides are references. Do not forget to remove a bright line used temporarily as a guide. Hide references or inspect previews for grids, centre marks and test rectangles. Label explanatory diagrams as such rather than presenting editor aids as design elements.

Build spacing from eighth-width modules

Imagine eight equal columns, use the middle six and reserve one on each side. On a 640-wide canvas, one column is about 80. This is a quick comparison framework, not a fixed standard. Adjust margins for the image and check shared text–image boundaries.

A shared module does not require integer-column dimensions. An image can be four-and-a-half columns wide and text can span more. Know which edges relate. Unjustified near-misses look loose; forcing every curved detail onto a line makes shapes rigid.

Keep an aid-free preview and inspect it separately from the gridded editor. The former tests the finished work; the latter explains placement. Readers ultimately see shapes and text, not how the grid helped arrange them.

Final checks and reference cleanup

Hide grids and guides and judge balance at actual display size. If the design remains crowded, object scale and space are the issue, not missing alignment lines. Save the original and an aid-free preview. Check corners and edges for test lines, especially ones close to the background colour.

Further exercise: a four-cell composition draft

  1. Place four simple shapes in two rows and columns. Use the grid for consistent margins, then hide it and check whether order remains.

  2. Replace one triangle with a similarly bounded circle and compare whether a tiny optical offset is needed.

  3. Deliberately leave a normal reference line in a copy, find it after export and remove it from the original to practise pre-export checking.

Frequently asked questions

Why do dragged objects jump at certain positions?

Grid, node or other snapping may be enabled. Check switches separately, disable an unnecessary type and repeat the same move. Turning everything off at once loses diagnostic information.

Must every icon follow the grid exactly?

Grids provide rhythm, while curves and visual centres may need correction. Establish geometry first, then adjust by actual appearance, especially the differing visual weights of circles, triangles and text.

What if reference lines appear in the PNG?

Check whether it is a normal line-tool object. Remove or hide test objects before exporting and inspect the preview; changing editor background appearance alone is insufficient.
