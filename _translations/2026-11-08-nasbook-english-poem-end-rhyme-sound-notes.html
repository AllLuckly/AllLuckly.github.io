Similar spelling at line ends does not guarantee rhyme, and different spelling may share a rhyme sound. Read a short English poem in Book Isle, separating text lines, end words, pronunciation evidence and scheme in your notes. Analyse end rhyme only, without mixing in enjambment, pronouns or emotional interpretation. The four-line example is original. The app supports reading and recording; do not assume an integrated phonetic dictionary, automatic rhyme detection or a spoken rendition that replaces pronunciation checking.

Establish four source lines rather than screen wraps

Open a lawfully obtained poem and record title, edition, poem name and chapter, distinguishing authorial lines from automatic screen wraps. Use an available bookmark and manual line numbers in notes. A narrow screen wrapping one verse line into two must not create another line number. Check a reliable edition if source line breaks are lost instead of guessing from punctuation. The exercise supplies explicit line numbers and can simply be copied into notes; Book Isle need not generate a poetry file. Layout checking identifies end words, not a formatting-repair task. Mark uncertain boundaries unresolved rather than forcing a neat scheme.

Copy the original lines and extract end words

Line 1 is “I fold a boat today.” Line 2 is “I let its colours play.” Line 3 is “I lift my eyes to see” and line 4 is “A bird beside the tree.” Save all four with their numbers, then separately record today, play, see and tree, keeping punctuation distinct. Full stops are not rhyme sounds, and line 3 still has an end word without one. Retain at least exercise title and line references with the words. Do not split today into to/day or mistake line 3’s to for its end word. Correct extraction from the source rather than forcing an expected AABB pattern.

Give pronunciation a traceable source

Use the CMU US English Pronouncing Dictionary: today and play have stressed rhyme portion EY1; see and tree have IY1. These are dictionary phoneme codes, with 1 marking primary stress, not letter names or syllable numbers. You may note familiar IPA approximations /eɪ/ and /iː/, identifying notation and reference accent rather than mixing systems in one column. For multiple pronunciations, check poetic context and reliable spoken evidence, listing candidates instead of selecting whichever rhymes most conveniently. App read-aloud may help listening but cannot automatically establish authorial pronunciation intent.

Compare from the stressed vowel onward

For these simple monosyllabic end words and today, compare the last stressed vowel and following sounds, not whole-word identity. Today and play begin differently but share /eɪ/; see and tree have different consonant beginnings but share /iː/. Matching spelling is unnecessary. Today’s earlier unstressed syllable need not match play. For complex multisyllabic rhyme, record the complete sequence from the relevant stress with its source rather than generalising these examples into a rule of listening only to the final vowel. This exercise covers clear end-rhyme examples of full rhyme.

Assign AABB from matching rhyme sounds

Label line 1 A and line 2 A because its rhyme matches. Line 3 introduces /iː/, labelled B, and line 4 also B: AABB. Letters are local group names, not a universal assignment of A to /eɪ/; another poem’s A may represent another sound. Retain line number, end word, evidence and letter together instead of an unexplained AABB. An ABAB arrangement must follow actual endings rather than moving labels to fit expected poetic form. Scheme records rhyme positions, not mood, meaning or quality. Line 3 still receives a label without a full stop.

Test different spellings and similar spellings

Add a separate comparison: blue and through share /uː/ in the reference US pronunciation despite different endings in spelling; love /ʌv/ and move /uːv/ share ove but do not form full rhyme here. Do not insert these four comparison words into the original stanza. Readers using another accent should retain their pronunciation source rather than treating one hearing as universal. Nor is imperfect rhyme automatically an authorial mistake; it may be another poetic choice. Record whether the selected pronunciations meet this exercise’s full-rhyme criterion, stating that scope in the notes.

Separate text errors, uncertain sounds and rhyme choices

If an ending sounds unmatched, first check word extraction, possible file errors and line boundaries, then dictionary candidates, retaining a near-rhyme interpretation only after those checks. Do not alter the author’s word to make it rhyme or treat app pronunciation as the sole correct answer. Preserve original word, line, candidate sounds and sources in unresolved notes, adding a later conclusion without erasing the question. A search hit may be the same word elsewhere; verify poem title and neighbouring lines. Manually transcribe image-only PDF words when needed rather than promising identical excerpt controls in every format. Use the installed version’s actual capabilities.

Return and explain every scheme letter

Save poem title, edition, four line numbers, end words, pronunciation source, AABB and comparison words. Exit reading and return to verify no word was lost through screen wrapping. Set the explanation aside and say why line 2 is A, line 4 B and why love/move cannot be judged by spelling alone. If only AABB can be recited, repair the pronunciation evidence rather than adding terminology. Start new line references and letter groups for each new poem. Finish when another reader can verify every judgement from lines and sources, not an unexplained automatic scheme label.
