All satisfy, at least one satisfies and not all satisfy are different claims. One counterexample can refute a universal claim without proving its opposite for everyone. Revisit source wording in Book Isle and check quantifiers and negation using four fictional cards. This practises textual logic within an explicit domain, not automatic evaluation of a whole book’s argument or the invention of an unlimited domain absent from its wording.

Define the domain of the quantifier

Select a short statement containing all, every, some, at least one or not all. Record book, edition, chapter and location, checking whether scope is a sample, period or category. The teaching domain is four cards on a table: A and B red, C and D blue, each exactly one colour, and the domain nonempty. Put that finite scope at the top of an external note. Do not generalize to every card in the world or confuse all red cards with all cards. A changed domain creates a changed statement and must be recorded, not switched silently halfway through reasoning.

List objects and properties separately

Before evaluating claims, make three columns: card name, whether red and observational basis. A/B are red and C/D not red by the supplied facts, not automatic colour detection. A property in real reading may require a definition, such as whether available for loan includes reserved items. Consult the text rather than ordinary intuition. Hidden or undocumented objects are unknown, not automatically nonqualifying. Red and not red are negations; red and blue exhaust this example only because of its explicit two-colour premise. With a third colour, not red cannot automatically become blue.

A universal claim requires every object to qualify

For all cards on the table are red, every A–D card must qualify. C not being red is sufficient to refute that universal claim; D need not also fail. Confirm that C belongs to the original domain. A green card outside it is not a counterexample to these four being red. Record counterexample object, failed property and domain evidence, not merely counterexample exists. Almost all and usually in a real book are not strict all. Do not remove those qualifications and use the same example to claim the author’s actual statement has been refuted.

An existential claim needs at least one witness

For at least one card is red, A supplies a witness, so the claim holds. B also being red does not make at least one false. At least one is not exactly one, so it does not establish that only A is red. A witness must be inside the domain and have the relevant property; an unfinished search does not prove existence. For some in a book, check whether it means at least one or colloquially only part of a group. This standard logic exercise uses at least one; actual wording still requires context. Preserve witness and supporting fact without expanding existence into a majority claim.

Not all does not mean none

The negation of all cards are red is at least one card is not red, witnessed by C or D. It is not all cards are not red, since A and B remain red. Conversely, negating at least one card is red gives no card is red, meaning every card is not red. Keep the four statements together and mark whether negation applies to quantification or the property. Merely changing all are to all are not does not negate the whole statement correctly. The nonempty four-card domain avoids empty-set details; if the textbook discusses empty domains, read its definitions separately rather than omitting that premise.

Check scope when an unknown card is added

In an external copy, add E with unknown colour and explicitly define a new domain. A still witnesses at least one red, and C still witnesses at least one nonred. Unknown E establishes neither its redness nor nonredness. C already refutes all red; adding an unknown does not restore it. If only A/B were known red and the rest unexamined, all red would remain unconfirmed, not proved. Separate refuted, supported and unconfirmed states rather than forcing every unknown into a true/false observation field. This better reflects actual reading evidence.

Return to the actual quantified wording when claims clash

If your summary says none are red while the source says not all are red, correct the summary; these are not merely tonal variants. Resolve domain from limiting passages, property from definitions and unknown facts without treating search-hit counts as evidence. For unreadable scanned wording, keep a manual page locator and unread status, without assuming logical-symbol recognition in Book Isle. Separate original, normalized wording, witness or counterexample, and unresolved conditions. Identify your normalization as your own, not the author’s quotation. Defer judgement rather than choosing the smoother-sounding quantifier when the issue remains unresolved.

Reconstruct four statements after leaving and returning

Leave the reader and return to the statement and domain definition. Without consulting the old table, evaluate all four red, at least one red, not all red and none red: false, true, true, false. For each, supply the witness or counterexample rather than memorizing a sequence. Check that your summary retains actual qualifications such as almost or usually. Preserve domain, property, quantifier, negation scope and evidence together, with the book separately retained. The record shows how far one sentence has been checked; a few card examples do not establish success or failure of the author’s entire theory.
