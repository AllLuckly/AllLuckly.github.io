The value of the square root of nine and the numbers whose squares equal nine are related but different questions. Read original radical cards in Book Isle, separating expression values, equation solutions and variable conditions. Use negative substitution to check absolute value. Calculations are manual, without assuming formula recognition, symbolic simplification or automatic grading.

1. Fix the real domain and symbolic objects

Prepare an original short exercise explicitly restricted to real numbers. Record file, edition, chapter/page and nearby phrase. Keep radical expressions, equations and variable conditions in three columns. Preserve radical scope, exponents, minus signs and parentheses when copying. Mark unclear symbols unresolved instead of guessing from an answer. Use available reading or note controls, or an external location-linked record where selection is unreliable. No automatic mathematical parsing is assumed, and display wrapping must not decide the operation’s scope.

2. Preserve three distinct targets

The cards ask for an expression value, an equation’s solutions, and an expression valid for all declared inputs. Classify the task before answering. Shared squares or numbers do not make them identical. Here sqrt and ^ are explicitly declared plain-text notation for principal square root and exponentiation, not Book Isle commands. Retain the original typography separately when studying an actual source.

Domain: real numbers
Card A: sqrt(9) = 3
Card B: x^2 = 9 has solutions x = 3 and x = -3
Card C: sqrt(x^2) = abs(x)

3. A principal square root has one nonnegative value

The principal-root definition in OpenStax radicals and rational exponents gives a nonnegative value. Thus the radical of nine is three, not plus-or-minus three. Both three and minus three square to nine, but that does not make the radical expression two-valued. Keep the two-number statement for the equation question. Check zero as well: its principal root is zero, so “nonnegative” must not become “always positive.” Explain that an expression value is being evaluated rather than merely memorizing a forbidden symbol.

4. Find all values satisfying the square equation

For card B, substitute three and minus three separately; both squares equal nine, so both belong to the solution set. Zero and two fail. If the problem additionally restricts the unknown to nonnegative values, retain only three while preserving that restriction beside the equation. Do not silently remove the negative solution. A compact plus-or-minus notation represents two candidates, not one sign-changing number or an instruction to add them into zero. Two substitution records help distinguish omitted solutions from confusing an expression value with a solution set.

5. Check the absolute value using minus three

For card C substitute minus three: square to nine, then take the principal root to obtain three. Replacing the result directly with the original input, minus three, contradicts that calculation. Across real inputs the result is absolute value: the input itself when nonnegative and its opposite when negative. Check positive three and zero too. These cases follow input conditions, not a preferred sign. Preserve the substitution chain rather than writing an unconditional claim that squaring and square-rooting cancel.

6. Check the domain and operation order separately

A separate square-root-of-minus-nine card has no real value in this lesson; do not fill minus three or introduce complex numbers while pretending the domain stayed unchanged. In a reverse-order control, taking a principal root and then squaring returns a nonnegative radicand, but a negative input is outside that real-valued chain. Draw both operation orders with intermediate values. Complex-number or branch discussions in another source need a separate definition-based record. Domain conditions are part of an equality’s scope rather than optional annotations.

7. Troubleshoot minus signs and radical scope

For disagreement, check whether a minus is inside or outside a radical, whether squaring applies to a parenthesized negative number, and whether copying lost parentheses. Squaring minus three differs from negating the square of three. Resolve unclear exponents from a clear source rather than letting one OCR line decide operation order. Open exported notes and inspect the real domain, both equation candidates and absolute-value explanation. Use declared plain text when no formula editor exists, without claiming a repaired native math layout. Keep wrong controls and correction reasons instead of merely replacing the final number.

8. Finish by classifying the three questions again

Exit and return to the cards. Cover the answers and classify value, solutions or all-input expression before writing three, two candidates or absolute value. Recheck card C at minus three, positive three and zero, identifying real-domain and nonnegative-definition dependencies. Save edition/location, symbols, question type, domain, intermediate steps and substitutions. Completion means another reader can explain why the three similar-looking questions have different answer forms, not automatically adding plus-or-minus or deleting negatives whenever a radical appears. Recheck changed variable conditions in later chapters.
