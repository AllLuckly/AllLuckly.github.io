An audience knowing a key is in a box hears a character’s “it is empty” differently. Read an original three-scene exercise in Book Isle, tracking who knows what, on what evidence and when the gap ends. This is not automatic plot inference, and not every error or sarcastic tone becomes dramatic irony.

Prepare three original scenes with directions

Scene one: A puts a blue key in an empty box on the table, closes it and leaves. Scene two: B enters and, without opening it, says it must be empty and goes to search elsewhere. Scene three: A returns, opens the box and shows B the key. Number and label these original, not quotations from a real play. Save the external source, import a genuinely readable format and record title/scene. Lancaster’s drama-discourse course explains audience/character knowledge differences. No course dialogue is copied or automatic detection implied.

Record only information supplied in scene one

In scene one, directions inform the audience/reader that the blue key enters the box; A knows the placement. B has not entered, and no peeking is stated. Record knower, proposition, scene/sentence evidence and unconfirmed parties rather than giving B knowledge from your completed reading. Empty describes the box before placement, not afterward. A’s departure adds no locking, removal or disappearance. Save short notes where supported or use external scene references, without inventing a plot-tracking button.

Compare propositions rather than tone in scene two

B’s certainty that the box is empty conflicts with the audience’s knowledge that the key remains inside. B has not opened it or acquired new evidence. Record audience knows key present, B believes empty, and A knows the placement; absence does not erase A’s knowledge. Dramatic irony is supported by the consequential knowledge gap and search elsewhere, not a sarcastic tone in “must.” Readers may feel amusement, anxiety or something else; separate your reaction from any unsupported claim that all audiences laugh.

Do not assume the character is lying

B may be mistaken; the text does not establish knowing deception. If later wording adds prior peeking and pretended ignorance, update to a concealment candidate from that new evidence rather than guessing it initially. State the exact audience advantage—key in box—not omniscience about the plot. A’s purpose, box ownership and B’s reason for searching are unstated. Keeping knowledge, belief and reader inference distinct prevents a false all-knowing column and makes revisions traceable to new sentences.

Update scene three without backfilling earlier scenes

When A opens the box and shows B the key, B receives visible evidence and this specific knowledge gap ends. Update scene three without deleting scene two’s mistaken belief or saying B knew all along. Earlier audience knowledge and later character knowledge coexist. If B were absent or did not see, opening alone would not justify the update; showing B is explicit here. One fact does not establish knowledge of all motives/consequences. The table remains limited to key location.

Separate verbal irony and aside evidence

B can sincerely mean “it must be empty” under a mistaken belief; no opposite-to-literal intention is required, unlike verbal irony’s contextual contrast. Nor does this example specify an aside directed to the audience but unheard by others onstage. An aside may create a knowledge gap, but is not the only route: scene-one action already informs readers. Give each term its evidence rather than interchanging them because all involve dialogue. A real passage may support several devices, recorded separately without forcing one exclusive label.

Delete one scene as a controlled comparison

Save a rewrite deleting only scene one’s key-placement information. A first-time reader in scene two may no longer know the box contains anything, so the original audience-knowledge row cannot carry over. Scene three may now reveal rather than confirm. Suspense can remain, but later disclosure does not establish earlier audience certainty. Name rewrite and original separately without overwriting the sole book. For script/performance comparison, verify whether the production actually presents scene one; reading directions does not establish what every live audience saw.

Revisit scenes to accept the knowledge record

Leave reading, then return to all scenes and check four qualifiers: empty before placement, occupied afterward, B did not open it, and A showed B. Open exported notes to verify negatives and scene numbers survive. Correct knowledge rows from actual acquisition, chronology from scenes and overgeneralised effects from personal response. Retain proposition table, mistake/lie distinction, deleted-scene control and sources. Search failures require text-layer checks, not deleting/reimporting books. The result is located dramatic reading evidence, not automatic spoilers, authenticity assessment or proof of an actor’s performance.
