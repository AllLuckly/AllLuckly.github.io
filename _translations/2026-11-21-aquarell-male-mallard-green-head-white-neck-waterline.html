Paint a male mallard swimming in side view, using head and neck bands, breast and waterline to clarify structure.

Read the source and choose a pose

The US Fish and Wildlife Service describes the male mallard’s iridescent green head, white neck ring and chestnut breast, while the female is mottled brown; both have a blue-purple wing patch. This exercise chooses one still male swimming in side view and does not use the picture to prove season, mate or identity. When observing references, distinguish a true side view from an oblique one; do not approach or feed wild birds.

Set a canvas and waterline baseline

Create an approximately 1800 × 1400 pixel landscape canvas in Aquarell. Using controls actually visible in the installed version, create sketch, water, body, head-and-neck and detail layers, then save the editable work. Mark the approximate water surface with a light horizontal line and sketch a slightly flattened body oval near the center. Let water obscure a small part of the lower body rather than placing the entire duck above the line. Canvas dimensions are a composition aid, not real scale.

Connect head, bill and neck to the body

Draw a rounded head above the left side of the body and connect it to the front breast with a neck. Extend a flattened bill forward from the head, keeping its base continuous with the outline and avoiding an exaggerated needle tip. Place the eye high on the side of the head with space to the crown, not inside the neck ring. Curve the breast down into the front body. Shrink the sketch: if the head looks like a floating ball, repair the neck connection before coloring.

Place the green head and narrow white neck ring

Block the upper head and neck in dark green, using small translucent blue- or yellow-leaning areas to suggest the iridescence seen in a reference rather than making the whole head fluorescent green. Reserve a narrow white neck ring between head and breast, curved with the neck. Show only the portion visible from this angle. Keep the white ring distinct from bright water reflections and do not run it through the bill or eye. Use a yellow-leaning midtone for the bill with a clear boundary against the green head.

Separate breast, body and folded wing

Block the breast below the ring in chestnut brown and let it follow the round breast shape. Use lighter gray for the body and a separate mid-gray folded wing overlapping its upper side. Establish the breast-wing relationship before adding a few feathers. If they merge, add a modest value boundary. Avoid rendering the torso as a straight stripe; feather details should follow the curve of the body.

Treat the visible wing patch carefully

The source notes a blue-purple speculum in both sexes, but visibility and shape depend on pose, occlusion and the reference. If your folded-wing pose shows it, place a small blue-purple area toward the rear of the wing and use the chosen reference for neighboring light and dark boundaries. If it is hidden, leave it out instead of coloring the entire wing blue from text alone. At small scale it should still belong to the wing, not look like a separate mark on the water.

Let the waterline overlap the lower body

Wash the water in muted blue-gray or green-gray, making a slightly varied boundary where it meets the lower body. Avoid tracing the whole duck with an equally thick black line. A few light reflections near the water should extend horizontally and stay clearly separate from the white neck ring. If underwater legs are not visible in the reference, do not invent a conspicuous orange pair. The scene can show floating without relying on unseen underwater anatomy.

Check markings and silhouette in order

If water highlights and feather texture were drawn on separate layers, temporarily hide those layers; otherwise inspect a sketch without the details. Check in order that head and bill connect, the white ring lies between green head and chestnut breast, the wing overlaps the body and the lower edge meets water. If head and wing are unclear, adjust value or the neck-breast boundary before adding highlights. At phone-thumbnail size, confirm the duck does not appear as a complete oval floating above the water. Restore details and check that the wing patch, if drawn, has not been covered by water color.

Compare waterline placement in two thumbnails

Save small sketch variants with the waterline near the lower body and noticeably higher, without changing head, bill or wing. Compare their floating appearance. A line too high may obscure the breast-wing connection; too low may make the body look as though it stands on water. Choose a line that preserves the main silhouette and clearly shows contact, then close the comparison and return to the main work. This is a composition experiment, not a claim that every duck exposes the same amount in every water condition.

Before final coloring, save a grayscale-only preview and check that head, breast, wing and water remain distinguishable in value. If the white ring disappears at small size, see whether it is too narrow or merges into a light background, then adjust against the reference; do not hide the boundary problem by increasing saturation. The preview is a composition check, while final color remains an artistic choice informed by the source.

Keep the two files separate for later comparison.

Save and independently inspect the export

Save the editable original, then use the share or export entry visible in the actual version to save an image separately. Reopen the exported file and inspect the white neck ring, bill tip, lower body and four canvas corners. Return to the original and hide water and head-neck layers to verify their identities. If the ring is missing from the export, check visibility and file location before overwriting anything. Deliver the self-drawn image and sources; mark unperformed device, export or reopening steps pending.
