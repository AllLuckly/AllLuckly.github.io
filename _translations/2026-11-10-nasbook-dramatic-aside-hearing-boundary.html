A line available to a play’s audience is not necessarily shared information for every onstage character. Read a short passage with aside directions in Book Isle and establish knowledge boundaries between public speech and asides. Beyond identifying speakers, check who is conventionally meant to hear. The exercise requires no automatic dramatic-device detection and does not infer fictional hearing from recorded volume.

Choose a continuous scene retaining directions

Choose a lawful play and record title, author, translator, edition, act/scene and a phrase anchor. Read several turns before and after the aside, preserving character labels, parenthetical directions and entrances/exits rather than excerpting only the apparent secret. Manually transcribe necessary directions from unselectable scans and compare with the page without claiming recognition. Use five fields: speaker, words, direction, intended hearers and subsequent evidence. The following two-person scene is invented, not attributed to a famous play. Keep presence separate from knowledge; standing nearby alone does not establish hearing.

Use convention and this edition’s directions

The Open University’s lesson on asides explains the convention that audiences hear words other onstage characters do not, while also allowing an aside between two characters. Thus not every aside is exclusively addressed to the audience. Check whether this edition specifies the audience, a particular character or another addressee, and retain both the term and exact direction. Mark unclear cases unresolved rather than supplying a definite hearer from a general definition. Identify editorial additions in parentheses as such.

Separate a public promise from an aside

In an invented scene A tells B, “I will come as agreed,” then says, with the direction “aside to the audience,” “Actually, I have not decided yet.” For the first line record A as speaker, B as an intended hearer and the audience as having access. For the second record A and audience access; that direction does not give B the undecided information. The common speaker does not make both lines a contradictory confession heard by B. Not yet deciding is not deciding not to come. Keep that distinction and separate quotation from interpretation rather than rewriting the line as a cancelled agreement.

Check later responses without inventing thoughts

Read B’s next response. “Then I will wait for you” can answer the public promise and does not prove B has uncovered the aside. If later directions establish overhearing or A explicitly confesses, update when B gains that information rather than backdating later knowledge. For ambiguous responses retain alternatives with evidence; do not invent B secretly understanding everything. The record tracks information access, not a verdict that A certainly lies or B necessarily believes naively. Motive requires additional text rather than a formal direction supplying every psychological explanation.

Compare an aside to one selected character

For a separate three-person control, A says “Do not tell C yet” with a direction indicating an aside to B. B is the specified addressee; C is outside the intended hearing scope. Replace the audience-only addressee of the previous example with B instead of reusing its marks. The audience still reads the words, but information distribution among characters differs. List named roles for more complex texts rather than hiding presence under “the others.” Keep the control in your own exercise without editing the book. Not every parenthetical action is an aside: exits and pauses serve other functions.

Separate script convention from performance sound

If also watching a lawful production, separately record its identity and clip time; a reading location is not a video timestamp. Audible delivery for the audience does not prove B hears within the fiction. Position, orientation and camerawork may inform that production’s interpretation but do not erase script directions. If a production changes the treatment, identify it as a production choice rather than universal practice. Without viewing a performance, complete only the textual task and invent no sound observations or device tests. Do not distinguish asides and soliloquies mechanically by word count alone; inspect context and addressees.

Reread once on each side of the aside

Save the location with available bookmarks or notes, leave and return to the same act/scene. Read the public line before, the aside itself and the public line after it, verifying the return to dialogue. If exported notes lose parentheses or speaker labels, consult the source and restore necessary explanation rather than assigning every line the same hearers. Reflowed page changes do not change act/scene identity. Retrieve by scene and phrase before resetting progress. Retain only necessary excerpts and clear locations, not an entire act for continuity. Inspect names and negatives as well as the excerpt title.

Review addressee, timing and source

For inconsistent knowledge records, first check whether audience access was mistaken for character access, then the aside’s addressee and later acquisition time. For missing directions inspect editions rather than inserting dramatic assumptions into quoted text. Preserve the previous judgment and new evidence, specifying which role and time change. Retain scene directions, both hearing scopes, the distinction between undecided and not coming, later responses and performance boundaries. This produces a traceable reading record, not proof about characters’ hearing, automatic deception judgments or exceptionless claims about all dramatic conventions.
