An adjective attached to a noun need not assign that noun a literal living state. Study the transferred-modifier use of hypallage through original short passages in Book Isle, tracing grammar and contextual bearer. This is interpretive reading, not automatic tagging of every unusual phrase.

Preserve both context sentences and four cards

A on P1 contains the two sentences below, thirteen whitespace tokens ignoring periods. P2 records the grammatical target and bearer of sleeplessness; P3 contains B, C, D. Export PDF/EPUB and retain source work. Check sleepless was not split by reflow and bookmark P1. Select adjective/Nora where possible or record sentences externally. Cards are original exercises, not automatically one continuous story about the named character.

Identify the full noun phrase before interpreting it

In a sleepless night, sleepless grammatically modifies night, not an adjacent noun phrase with Nora. Preserve that attachment instead of rewriting the source as a sleepless Nora. After relates the period to her experience, while lay awake until dawn supplies the human state. Grammar locates the modifier; context supports its bearer. An isolated adjective cannot complete both columns.

In this short card She refers back to Nora through the adjacent context. Removing the first sentence leaves a familiar sleepless-night expression but removes the direct named-bearer evidence. Context deletion weakens this particular identification; do not claim the remaining words alone still establish Nora.

A: Nora lay awake until dawn. She crossed the room after a sleepless night.
B: Nora lay awake until dawn. She crossed the room after a noisy night.
C: The sleepless guard crossed the room at dawn.
D: The night argued with Nora.
A: grammatical target=night; contextual bearer of wakefulness=Nora

Keep the transferred-modifier interpretation narrowly grounded

Night is a period, not literally a sleeping organism; Nora supplies the wakeful experience. Transferring her state to the night she experienced is therefore a context-supported transferred-modifier reading, not a new conscious character suffering insomnia. The cited hypallage reference includes other shifts of correspondence too. State whose state modifies what before using the term, rather than inventing psychology to fit a label.

Retain a literal human attachment in C

In C, sleepless directly modifies guard, a person who can be awake, so this evidence does not require transfer from another bearer. Similar states do not create identical grammatical relations. C does not identify the guard as Nora or establish her occupation. No cause such as anxiety, illness or a task is supplied; rhetoric analysis is not a diagnosis or a new plot fact.

Separate ordinary period description and personifying action

B replaces sleepless with noisy: events during a night can make it ordinarily noisy, so a nonhuman noun does not make every adjective a transferred human state. D assigns arguing to night, supplying personifying agency rather than A’s sleepless modifier. Categories may overlap in complex writing; this exercise distinguishes visible evidence, not universally disjoint labels. Diagnose words, grammar and context rather than a generic “nonliteral” switch.

Keep interpretation separate from known intention

A can spread the character’s wakeful experience across the period, colouring the night through her perspective. That is a supported proposed effect, not proof of deliberate use of a classical label. Alternative readings such as conventional phrasing still need textual comparison. Separate source facts, grammar, interpretation and inferred intention; the final column may remain unknown without an author statement.

Replacing night with guard without revising the rest can produce an awkward “after a sleepless guard.” Such an uncontrolled rewrite cannot prove the source has a particular figure. C is a separately complete control, not a word-for-word substitution into A. Preserve task identities rather than merge events or use a damaged sentence to discredit the original reading.

Diagnose shifted bearers and overextended categories

If night itself becomes the wakeful person, revisit Nora in the first sentence. Replacing A with the guard sentence changes the source, not merely its interpretation. Automatically classifying B ignores whether noisy requires a sleeping bearer. Search words and return manually to context, without claiming semantic search. For scans use P1.
