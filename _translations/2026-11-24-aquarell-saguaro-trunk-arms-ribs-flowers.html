Use a permitted reference to distinguish the trunk and arms of a saguaro, then describe volume with ribs and flowers.

Choose a reference and growth state

Choose a permitted saguaro photograph and record source, season and view. The National Park Service describes an initially unbranched trunk and arms that can appear as plants age; flowers are normally at trunk and arm tips but can also occur along the sides. Individuals, environments and seasons vary, so do not treat one arm count or flowering state as universal. This exercise uses a branched reference.

Set up canvas and placement

Create an approximately 1400 by 1800 portrait canvas in Aquarell and save an editable original; dimensions are exercise choices. Separate sketch, plant, ground and sky with the layer controls visible in the installed version. Place the subject near the center with room above the trunk and beside the arms. Mark a light axis and horizon, checking the reference ground slope before committing to a thick outline.

Compare trunk width and height

Lightly block the trunk from ground contact to top, comparing widths at base, middle and upper portion. It is not a rectangle pasted on a horizon: establish where it meets the ground and follow the photo for the top curve. Use trunk height as a reference when judging the highest arm. Shrink the canvas to check that the subject is neither cropped nor flattened.

Draw arms and their connections

Follow the arms visible in the reference. Mark each attachment at the trunk before drawing the outline that bends outward and turns upward. Arms need not begin at the same height or mirror each other. A near arm can obscure the trunk edge and a distant arm can be hidden by it. Check each connection, then remove construction lines hidden behind overlaps so the arms do not float as green tubes.

Use ribs to describe the cylinder

Observe the vertical ribs running down the trunk. Their apparent spacing changes across a curved surface: the front-facing region can appear wider and lines nearer an edge closer together. Begin with a few slightly curved lines, rechecking arm junctions rather than drawing trunk lines through a near arm. Rib count and spacing follow the photo; the number or thickness in the illustration does not measure a plant’s age.

Separate light and green values

Block trunk and arms in a mid green, then use two or three broad values for lit and shaded sides according to the reference light. Leave spines and flowers until later. At a small scale the trunk should have volume and the arms remain distinct. If identical black rib lines make it look like striped cardboard, reduce their contrast and use larger value planes for roundness. Screen colors are not environmental measurements.

Add visible spines and flowers

Use small, varied strokes for spines only where they remain clear when enlarged; distant arms do not need every spine drawn. If the photograph shows flowers, add a few pale flower shapes at the visible trunk or arm tips. Omit them for a nonflowering reference rather than repeating one flower on every arm. The park source describes creamy white blossoms; a single drawn flower does not establish season or variety.

Handle sky and ground contact

Use muted sky and ground colors behind the plant. Check where the trunk meets the ground and add a shadow consistent with the chosen light direction. Background hills or clouds are compositional choices, not a claim they come from the same place. If an arm merges with the sky, adjust local values or simplify texture before surrounding the whole plant with a thick black outline.

Inspect arm-rib junctions

Zoom in on every trunk-arm junction. A near arm should hide the trunk ribs behind it; a far arm’s visible edge should stop at the trunk boundary. If ribs make an arm appear to pass through the body, restore the sketch and inspect the connection instead of changing color alone. Shrink the view to compare trunk, arms and flowers, and hide details to ensure the large form still reads without spine marks.

Troubleshoot, save and export

If the trunk looks flat, inspect rib spacing and the broad side values. If an arm floats, check its origin, overlap and outline connection. Save the editable original first, then use a share or export control visible in the installed version to save a separate PNG or JPEG. Reopen it to inspect flowers, arm tips and shadow for cropping, then return to the original to verify editable layers. Record the photo source and one connection to improve; mark device steps not performed as pending.

Compare flowered and unflowered versions

Duplicate the original. Keep the flowers visible in one copy and hide their layer or remove drawn flowers in the other, preserving trunk, arms and background. Compare them small to see whether flowers obscure arm tips and whether the unflowered silhouette remains readable. These are composition exercises, not a timeline of a particular plant. Name the copies separately so a practice drawing is not mistaken for the original reference photo. If flowers become a blur at small scale, reduce their number or increase their value contrast with the background, then check that the arms are not obscured.
