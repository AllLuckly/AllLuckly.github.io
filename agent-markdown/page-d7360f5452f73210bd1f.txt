## Derive the finite formula before taking a limit

Shifting rS\_N with r=−1/2 gives S\_N=(1−r^N)/(1−r)=(2/3)\[1−(−1/2)^N\]. The denominator is 3/2, not 1/2. Since |r|\<1, r^N→0; the limit of partial sums defines S=2/3. There is no finite “last term” set to zero, and every finite N still has a nonzero remainder. Viewing several cards is not completing infinitely many additions.

```text
a_k=(-1/2)^k, k=0,1,...
S_N=sum k=0..N-1; S_N=(2/3)*(1-(-1/2)^N)
S_1=1; S_2=1/2; S_3=3/4; S_4=5/8
S=2/3; R_N=S-S_N=(2/3)*(-1/2)^N
S_6=21/32; abs(R_6)=1/96
S_7=43/64; abs(R_7)=1/192
```

## Separate signed remainder from absolute error

R\_N=S−S\_N=(2/3)(−1/2)^N, so |R\_N|=2/(3·2^N). Even N gives positive remainder and an underestimate; odd N gives an overestimate. N=6 yields 21/32 with remainder 1/96; N=7 gives 43/64 with remainder −1/192. Neither equals 2/3. An overestimate need not be farther away; compare absolute errors rather than signs alone.

Six terms give 0.65625, seven give 0.671875 and the limit is approximately 0.666666…. One lies below and the other above, neither equal to the sum. R\_N here means limit minus approximation; reversing subtraction preserves magnitude but reverses sign. Record the adopted definition before checking parity rather than assume opposite sign conventions are an arithmetic contradiction.

## Use a strict inequality for the one-hundredth target

For |R\_N|\<1/100, six terms fail because 1/96 is larger; seven pass with 1/192. Halving each successive error makes seven minimal. At two decimal places six terms display 0.66, seven display 0.67, and the limit also displays 0.67. Matching displays for seven terms and the limit do not make the remainder zero; compare both approximation errors exactly. Use exact fractions for the threshold and decimals only for reading. Changed first term, ratio or tolerance requires a new count; seven is not a universal geometric-series requirement.

## Do not extend the infinite formula past boundary ratios

With first term one, r=1 gives S\_N=N; r=−1 alternates partial sums 1, 0 with no limit. For r=2 positive partial sums grow, so calling 1/(1−2)=−1 their infinite sum violates convergence conditions. The finite identity remains available when its denominator 1−r is nonzero; taking an infinite limit is a separate step. Restore the |r|\<1 scope in notes, without altering values or making claims about other generalized summation conventions.

## Group a counterexample to terms-tending-to-zero sufficiency

The harmonic series 1+1/2+1/3+… has terms tending to zero but diverges. After 1, group 1/2, then 1/3 through 1/4, then 1/5 through 1/8, and so on. Each block doubles its count while every term is at least its last term, making every block sum at least 1/2. Arbitrarily many blocks give unbounded partial sums. This refutes sufficiency of vanishing terms, not the proved alternating geometric limit. Distinguish term and partial-sum limits.

Check harmonic block boundaries: the two-term block is 1/3+1/4, not a block omitting 1/3; the four-term block runs from 1/5 to 1/8. Block m has 2^(m−1) terms, each at least 1/2^m, for lower bound 1/2. Arbitrarily many such blocks exceed any finite bound; slow growth in a short plot is not convergence evidence.
