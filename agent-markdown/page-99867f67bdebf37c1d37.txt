A pun requires more than attaching two dictionary senses to a word in a note: examine how both readings are activated by the phrase and context. Read original Chinese dialogue and a separate English same-word example in Book Isle, preserving language and translation identity rather than claiming the app automatically finds humour.

## Separate sign wording from character speech

Passage A reads: “A clock-repair shop displayed a sign: We have time for repairs. A customer asked: Are you busy today? Pointing to clocks awaiting repair, the owner laughed: What we never lack here is time.” Retain all four sentences in an owned exercise file, noting chapter and opening words. The sign is displayed text and the later lines are character dialogue, not narration claiming unlimited lifespan. Locate occurrences of “time” before assigning senses, without adding prices or opening hours absent from the passage.

## Keep evidence for both readings of wordplay

The [Academy of American Poets teaching glossary](<https://poets.org/resources-teachers>) includes pun as wordplay involving multiple meanings of wording or sound. The Chinese word for time can suggest available leisure in the question about busyness, while the clock-shop setting evokes time measured or displayed by clocks. The latter is an associative semantic echo, not a physical stock of a substance named time. Record the supporting sentence for each reading; if the second seems weak, explain why rather than requiring every reader to find the example funny.

## Test whether a context replacement removes a reading

Keep the owner’s final sentence but replace setting and gesture with “an ordinary warehouse; the owner pointed to a sack of potatoes”. Control B can still imply available time, but the clock-based cue disappears. Record the contextual change: identical words do not ensure identical wordplay. A claim that potatoes save cooking time adds an association the passage does not supply. This comparison tests where evidence resides rather than using limitless invention to prove that every sentence has two meanings. Label both versions and the exact changes.

## Read an English homograph without projecting it onto Chinese

Write a separate C: “A financial bank stood beside a river. The narrator wrote: The bank stood by the river bank.” The two occurrences of English “bank” invoke a financial institution and a river’s edge. Label it an original English exercise with Chinese explanation, not proof that A’s Chinese time-word has exactly the same lexical relationship. A Chinese translation using separate words for bank and riverbank preserves facts but loses English same-form repetition. Record source language, source word and translations rather than promising identical wordplay in every translation.

## Assign senses occurrence by occurrence

For C, label the first “bank” as financial institution and “river bank” as river edge, checking syntax and reference. Both as institution creates a mistaken river-bank institution; both as river edge removes the financial institution. Keep these as external reading errors, not edits to the source. For A retain available time and the clock-related echo without assuming that two columns imply identical lexical structures. The same analysis sheet can hold different kinds of evidence without proving them to be the same linguistic phenomenon.

## Separate proposed effect from textual evidence

A explicitly says the owner laughed, supporting that narrated action but not laughter from the customer or every reader. C has no reaction; its same-word arrangement can still be analysed without inventing laughter to satisfy a template. Mark possible lightness, attention-getting or verbal ingenuity as proposed effects. Wordplay alone does not prove deception. Nor does a translation’s loss of repeated form necessarily mean its factual content is wrong: preservation of effect and preservation of facts are separate comparison tasks.

## Troubleshoot matching strings and translation backflow

When search returns many occurrences of time, inspect the clock or busyness cue at each result rather than classifying every match as verified wordplay. If notes show two banks while the source uses distinct Chinese words, check whether your English explanation flowed back into the supposed original. For changing italics or quotation marks, verify textual identity against the original rather than using appearance to infer a second sense. Missing context remains unverified; two dictionary definitions cannot replace it. Name rewrites so an incorrect control does not become the next round’s source.

## Reopen and check wording, context and translation

Leave reading, reopen A, B and C, locate the original keywords and independently record readings with their supporting sentences. A and B need not have the same effect despite matching final words; C’s repeated English word does not require one repeated Chinese translation. Retain original wording, context anchors, interpretations, rewrites and unknowns, ensuring source files survive and your translation is not attributed to the author. Acceptance requires traceable evidence for each reading and what disappeared in rewriting, not a pun label for every repeated form or similar sound.
