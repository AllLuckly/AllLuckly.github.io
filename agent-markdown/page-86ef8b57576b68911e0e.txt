## Give the handle and shank a shared centre

Create an approximately 1500×1800 px portrait canvas with warm-grey background. Tilt the T-handle slightly up to the right while keeping the shank nearly vertical. Block the handle and central socket, then extend an axis downward instead of placing coils first. Separate sketch, wood, metal and shadow. Let the handle span about half the image with space below for the helix. Shorten an overly cross-like shank and show handle thickness; repair an off-centre attachment first. ## Treat three turns as one path rather than three objects

On a checking layer, trace one line from the shank around the axis downward, planning three complete turns before the tip. Each revolution descends; do not draw three individually closed rings. Distinguish near and far arcs with a single line and follow it continuously. Repair a dead end before adding thickness. Use similar axial spacing without mechanically copying projected curves. Three turns are an invented simplification, so an actual extra turn is not an assembly defect. Do not export the checking axis as a physical central core.

## Preserve near and far ordering without making knots

Thicken the path moderately, with lighter near arcs and darker far arcs. At projected overlaps, let the near segment occlude the far one without welding them into an X or erasing a whole turn. Check transitions in two greys and retain the onward path as it emerges. Add far arcs and visible inner surfaces if flat and wavy; reduce local thickness and separate projected segments if knotted. White spots at crossings cannot substitute for depth. Continuity should survive hiding highlights, and the outline must not bulge into beads.

## Keep the shank-to-helix transition on one axis

Trace the metal from below the handle into the first turn with a natural bend, not a separately glued spring. The whole axis may tilt with the viewpoint but must stay coherent. If the first turn sits left and later turns right, repair the shared axis instead of inventing extra bends. Show the handle socket with a small dark recess and narrow metal connection, not an oversized opening exposing invented gears. This is a simple single-handle design. Do not add wings, gears or a bottle frame from other models and assemble an unsupported hybrid.

## Separate wood from metal with end faces and grain

Use soft browns on the handle, with end faces aligned to its length and sparse longitudinal grain rather than screw threads. Keep cooler metal greys and a few narrow glints; wood highlights should be broader and softer. Hide texture and establish handle volume first. If it resembles a brown-painted metal pipe, soften mirror-like white streaks and strengthen the end faces. Reduce grain contrast where it hides the socket instead of moving the attachment. ## Give the tip and cast shadow separate ends

Taper the final metal segment to a clear tip in empty space instead of closing another ring. Place a shadow down-right, softer under elevated sections and firmer near the table, without making it a second wire. Start with a simple dark silhouette and separate tip from shadow if they merge. ## Trace from handle to tip at reduced size

Reduce to roughly quarter size, hide grain, axis and all metal highlights, then trace shank, first, second and third turns to the tip. Repair connections and occlusion wherever labels would be needed to guess the route. Recover a lost far arc through local background contrast rather than thick black outlines.
