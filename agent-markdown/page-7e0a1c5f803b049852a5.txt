Observe one Yoshino cherry flower, its stalk and branch overlaps to paint a small cluster of three or four blooms.

## Choose a cultivar and reference

Harvard’s Arnold Arboretum describes Yoshino cherry as having white-to-pink five-petaled flowers, often appearing before leaves, in clusters of three or four. Choose a permitted photo clearly showing the cultivar and single flowers; record source and viewpoint. Cherry cultivars vary and double flowers should not be forced into five petals. This exercise follows the selected Yoshino reference and does not identify a tree from an illustration.

## Compose the branch and canvas

Create an approximately 1600 by 1600 square canvas in the current Aquarell interface and save an editable original; dimensions are suggestions. Let a branch cross diagonally with three or four flowers nearby, leaving space around outer petals and corners. Separate sketch, branch, petals, centers and background using available layers and actual interface labels. Mark the main branch direction and each flower center first.

## Give each flower a direction

Avoid treating the cluster as repeated stamps on one plane. Choose one near-front flower as the subject and place one or two slightly angled or partially hidden blooms according to the photo; simplify others. A front flower’s envelope may be roughly circular, while a side flower’s can appear compressed according to viewpoint. Use light circles or ellipses as placeholders and check that recognizable spaces remain between flowers.

## Draw five petals on the main flower

Mark five directions radiating from the main center and draw broad soft petal contours. Adjacent petal bases converge toward the center and edges may differ; do not begin with five disconnected circles. Stop an edge where another petal obscures it. Temporarily number one through five to check the count, then hide numbers and shrink the image to confirm the petals still surround one center rather than scattering across the branch.

## Handle angled flowers and overlaps

For an angled flower first identify the visible center and petal plane; near petals may hide far portions. Observe the five-petal structure in the reference but mark hidden portions unknown rather than spreading every petal onto one layer to reach the count. Decide front-to-back order between overlapping flowers and stop rear contours at the front edge. If petals look as though they pass through each other, hide color and recheck the sketch.

## Connect stalks to the branch

Draw a slender stalk from each flower base to the branch according to the photograph. Do not run the stalk through the petal center when it is hidden behind the bloom. Length and curve can vary with the reference, but visible connections should be traceable. Establish the thicker branch first and let each slender stalk meet it naturally so flowers do not float nearby. Do not invent junctions hidden by overlapping blooms.

## Build pale petal values

Block petals in warm white or pale pink and use a slightly deeper muted pink-gray for shaded or overlapped areas. Color depends on light and white cultivars need not be made uniformly vivid pink. Preserve large petal shapes first, then add restrained soft edge variation without outlining every petal in equally dark strokes. If pale blooms merge with a light background, adjust background or local edge contrast.

## Simplify centers and stamens

From the photograph, suggest centers and visible stamens with small yellow-gray or pale dots and lines; individual indistinct filaments need not all be drawn. The center belongs where the petals converge and can appear off-center in an angled flower. Start with few short marks and inspect at thumbnail size. If the center becomes a heavy dark knot, reduce marks or contrast rather than adding more. Do not complete a center hidden by petals.

## Compare front and side thumbnails

Duplicate one flower sketch into a near-circular frontal envelope and an ellipse compressed like the photographed side view. Keep the center and petal-count logic, then compare which edges become shorter because of occlusion. Uniformly flattening the whole flower does not automatically explain front-to-back relationships. Record one visible overlap, choose the closer version and mark unclear portions unknown.

## Check branch, flowers and edges

Zoom in on stalk-to-branch connections, overlapping petal contours and whether each center sits where visible petals converge. Hide petal color to inspect the sketch, then restore it. If a branch crosses a foreground flower, adjust stacking or hide the occluded segment. At phone reading size ensure the main and neighboring blossoms remain distinct and key petals are not cropped. Use restrained background shapes without inventing leaves absent from the reference.

## Save and recheck output

Save the editable original and use a share or export entry actually shown in the current version to save a separate image. Reopen it and inspect the five-petal subject, stalk connections, overlaps and crop; return to identify sketch, branch, flowers and background layers. If a center or branch disappears, check visibility, color and scale first. A flattened image without layers does not establish original damage. Record photo source and permission and mark unperformed device or export steps pending.

## Distinguish buds from open flowers

If the reference includes closed buds and open flowers, record their positions separately beside the sketch. A compact outline can suggest a bud without forcing five spread petals; retain the visible five petals and center on the open subject. Mark unclear stalk junctions unknown rather than adding flowers merely to reach a cluster of three or four.
