Draw a curved-claw hammer resting on a pale-grey table, choosing a one-piece forged steel head and handle with a separate grip covering. Connect the striking face and two claws through one head, then continue its neck into the grip. This is not an impact test; proportions and lighting are original drawing choices.

## Lock the pose with head and handle axes

Create an approximately 1600×1800 px canvas. On the sketch layer put the head centre near (780,500) and the grip end near (900,1520). The head axis runs roughly across: striking end left, curved claw right; the handle tilts slightly down-right. Mark their junction faintly without demanding a perfect right angle or treating pixels as physical dimensions. Separate base colour and metal detail, save native work, and check the whole pose at reduced size. Leave room beyond the right claw tips rather than crop away a shape problem.

## Give the striking end a substantial face

Draw a slightly compressed elliptical striking face at the left and connect it through a short narrowing neck to the head. It must not resemble a coin pasted onto a rod. The pose reveals a little upper surface, so use a compatible face ellipse rather than a fully frontal circle on a strongly turned head. Block the face in grey with a firmer near rim and softer far contour. With highlights hidden, face, neck and middle should remain distinct. If only a bright dot survives, restore volume boundaries before adding more white.

## Separate the curved claw into two prongs

Extend a downward-curving metal mass from the right of the head, dividing into two prongs near its end. Choose an oblique view where both tips read, with a tapering V-shaped gap between them. The gap is empty space, not a third black prong. Both prongs share a rear root and each has inner and outer edges; they are not two disconnected wires. Correct the gap with background colour before judging near/far tip lengths. The far claw may be partly concealed, but do not make the near one transparent to force symmetry. The slot root need not become a sharp through-hole.

## Keep the forged neck continuous with the handle

Narrow the middle of the head into its steel neck and continue it toward the grip. This chosen one-piece construction must not acquire the wooden wedge or handle socket of a wooden-handled hammer, nor a background gap cutting across its neck. Mark the grip covering’s upper edge with a short arc: the steel continues inside it, and the material boundary is not a movable hinge. First connect the steel in one mid-grey, then shade it. If head and handle look loosely detached, repair the silhouette instead of using a black halo as a connection.

## Shape the grip covering separately from steel

Make the grip slightly thicker than the neck, with gently swelling sides and a rounded end rather than a knife point. Use dark blue-grey or another chosen dark colour and a narrow light-facing band. Group a few shallow texture marks along it instead of filling it with equally black stripes. This distinguishes a covering visually; colour does not establish vibration-reduction performance. Inspect the steel-to-grip boundary enlarged so the covering follows the same axis, with no twisted edge or unexplained metal spike at the end.

## Break reflections at claw gaps and contacts

Light the subject from upper left. Give the head a broad, uneven pale-grey reflection narrowing toward the curved claw. Each prong has its own light edge; no white bridge crosses the slot. Retain a middle tone on the striking face instead of filling it white. Reserve darkest grey for inner slot edges, small contact areas near the grip and the table shadow. Keep that shadow close to actual contacts before softening down-right, rather than outline every edge with an equal black ring. In a tonal check, steel, covering and background should still separate.

## Troubleshoot face, slot and neck separately

Hide texture and highlights. Trace from the left face through the head to the right claw root, then down the neck. If white closes the slot, repair only the crossing highlight. If prongs cross like scissors, restore their common-root fork. A rope-like grip calls for checking edge thickness and axis. Keep a faulty copy and change one contour category at a time, checking both enlarged detail and the full image at fit-to-window size afterward. No nail or hand is included to conceal errors, and a static pose proves no load capacity.

First identify a left face, two right prongs with one gap and a continuous neck; enlarge it to check that bright edges do not cross empty space. Reopen the native work and confirm grip colour and steel reflections remain separately editable, recording version and omitted details. Leave export/reopening pending if unperformed and deliver only expected checks.
