## First success on trial three permits only FFS

T=3 requires FFS, with probability (3/4)²(1/4)=9/64. SFF stops at one; FSF stops at two, so neither belongs. Generally P(T=n)=(3/4)^(n−1)(1/4). Do not multiply by three as in a fixed-three-trials exactly-one-success question: the first-success location already fixes the order. The incorrect 27/64 combines different stopping positions.

Here p is per-trial success probability, not a probability that accumulates after failure. After two failures the next trial still succeeds with probability 1/4 in the independent constant-p model, not 1/2 because success is “due.” Separate a one-trial conditional probability from cumulative success-by-time probability, which increases without changing per-trial p.

## Use the complement for success by trial three

T≤3 includes S, FS, FFS with probabilities 1/4, 3/16, 9/64, totaling 37/64. The complement FFF has probability 27/64, giving the same 1−27/64. T\>3 means still no success, not success on three. ## Failure count differs from T by one

Let Y=T−1 count failures before first success, allowing zero. Y=2 and T=3 are the same event, probability 9/64. Y=0 means success on trial one, probability 1/4, not success without a trial. P(Y=k)=(3/4)^k/4 for nonnegative integer k differs by one exponent because it counts another object. ## Conditional waiting counts three further failures

Given T\>2, asking T\>5 requires failures on trials three through five. Independence and fixed probability give (3/4)³=27/64, also obtained from P(T\>5)/P(T\>2). Previous failures do not make success due. ## A nonreplacement control changes the assumptions

A uniformly shuffled four-card set with one S and three F, read without replacement until S, has T=1,…, 4 with probability 1/4 each. In particular P(T=3)=(3/4)(2/3)(1/2)=1/4, not 9/64. In the without-replacement control P(T=1)=1/4, but after an initial F the next success probability becomes 1/3.
