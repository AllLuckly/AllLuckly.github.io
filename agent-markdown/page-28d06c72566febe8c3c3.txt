Draw a cyclic isosceles trapezoid with unequal bases, checking Ptolemy through length products rather than apparent angles. Name vertices in circular order and distinguish diagonals. The equality requires a cyclic convex quadrilateral; visually similar lengths are not proof, and forty-one is not its area.

## Fix vertex order and scale

Take A=(−3,0), B=(3,0), C=(2,4), D=(−2,4), closing ABCD as a non-self-crossing isosceles trapezoid. On about 1800 × 1500 pixels use u=900+150x, v=1050−150y, giving (450,1050), (1350,1050), (1200,450), (600,450). Group points, sides, diagonals, circle and calculations, then save. Keep equal scales and move labels without moving points. Separate abstract units from pixels; nonuniformly stretching the diagram invalidates its old length labels.

## Verify all four points from one circle centre

Use O=(0,11/8). Squared distances from A and B are 9+121/64=697/64; from C and D they are 4+441/64=697/64. Equal positive values establish one circle of radius √697/8. Draw an approximation from this centre and radius rather than the trapezoid’s bounding-box centre. Check screen O’s y=843.75, without centring it halfway between bases for appearance. If the circle is dragged approximately, label it illustrative; the four squared distances remain the cyclicity evidence.

## Compute sides six, four and two square roots of seventeen

AB is six and CD four. BC has horizontal difference −1 and vertical difference four, hence length √17; DA is also √17. List sides before labelling, without mistaking a horizontal difference of one for a sloping length or merging both √17 labels. Pair opposite sides AB with CD and BC with DA, not adjacent AB with BC. Distinct line styles may help, but names must preserve the pairing in monochrome so every factor remains traceable.

## Separate squared diagonal length from diagonal product

AC has differences five and four, giving √41, as does BD. Neither diagonal is forty-one; their product is. Equal diagonals follow this configuration, not every cyclic quadrilateral. Ptolemy gives AB×CD+BC×DA=6×4+√17×√17=24+17=41, matching AC×BD=√41×√41=41. Products have squared-length units but do not automatically equal area. The trapezoid’s area is (6+4)×4/2=20, a useful note against that confusion. In an independent copy scale all geometry by two. Sides become 12,8 and two lengths 2√17; diagonals each become 2√41. Opposite-side products become 96+68=164, matching the diagonal product. Area twenty becomes eighty, still different. This requires one scale factor for every geometric dimension; enlarging text or stretching width alone does not justify fourfold products. Restore all six lengths and area with the original geometry, not just its size while leaving 164. This checks quadratic scaling of products, not cyclicity of arbitrary shapes.

```text
A=(-3,0); B=(3,0); C=(2,4); D=(-2,4)
O=(0,11/8); R^2=697/64
AB=6; CD=4; BC=DA=sqrt(17)

AB*CD+BC*DA=24+17=41=AC*BD

noncyclic side-2 rhombus: side products=8
diagonal product=4*sqrt(3) != 8
```

## Use a noncyclic rhombus that breaks the equality

In a separate frame draw a side-two rhombus with angles sixty and one hundred twenty degrees, using (0,0), (2,0), (3,√3), (1,√3) in order. All sides are two, giving opposite-side product sum eight. Diagonals are 2√3 and two, whose product 4√3≈6.928 differs from eight. Convexity alone is insufficient because this rhombus is not cyclic. Retain the original trapezoid and save the counterexample separately instead of shrinking the discrepancy or moving labels to imply equality.

## Diagnose inequality through order and factor identity

If a diagonal becomes √65, check for an erroneous horizontal difference of seven. If 24+17 fails, check both sloping-side radicals. Connecting ACBD instead of ABCD crosses the boundary and changes opposite-side identities; restore order before checking six, four and two √17 lengths. For a misplaced circle, restore O and radius rather than moving points to it. Keep a pre-edit copy and change one value, connection or label at a time, retaining the cause of failure. Visual resemblance is not mathematical proof.
