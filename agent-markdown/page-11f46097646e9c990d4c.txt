A moth’s spread wings share structure without machine-perfect duplication. Use an axis and paired points to draw manually, then main patches, a band and a few edge dots. The focus is correspondence and hierarchy, not required mirroring or symmetry tools.

## Use a centre axis for body and wingspan

Create a 1400×1200 pixel pale gray-beige canvas. Sketch a short central axis with a body around 550 long and 100 wide. Put each wingtip about 500 from the axis, upper tips just above the head and lower edges near the mid-to-lower body.

Mark corresponding upper tips, widest points, lowest lower-wing points and wing-root connections before joining with curves. Do not finish one wing then guess the other. Slight height differences are fine if spans and main areas correspond. Separate background, wings, body and patterns for overlap adjustment.

## Separate upper and lower wings with broad colour areas

Limit colours to gray-brown, pale ochre, dark brown and a little blue-gray. Use an approximately 80-pixel brush for darker upper wings and lighter ochre below. Darker roots and lighter outer areas give slight volume. Share colour roles across sides while allowing natural mark variation.

Place the body above the roots, with a broader thorax and tapering abdomen. Avoid an overlong thick stick splitting the wings. Add antennae after shape and patterns. At reduced size, it should read as one moth, not disconnected leaves.

## Place the main patches before comparing their sizes

Position one main patch on each upper wing, slightly outward of the midpoint from root to tip. Paint an irregular 100–140 pixel oval on one side, then locate its counterpart from the axis. Exact pixels need not match, but distance, size and tilt should correspond.

A main patch can be a dark outline with a pale interior, not necessarily an eye. Keep both eye-like patches simple rather than rings on one side and a dot on the other. Their contrast should exceed later edge dots without overpowering the full wing.

## Let bands follow the wings

Use a medium brush for a curved upper-wing band about 40–70 pixels wide. Follow the outer wing at varying distance, not as an equal-width outline. Main patches may interrupt it rather than every pattern crossing through. Mark start, middle and end opposite, then connect a similar rhythm.

Give lower wings only a weak band near the base colour, keeping upper wings informative and lower wings quiet. Strong patterns everywhere bury body and wing shape. For palette roles, see [Five-color palette exercise](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/aquarell-limited-palette/>), giving each colour only one or two roles.

## Small patterns add rhythm, not fill every gap

Add three to five edge dots or short bars around 15–30 pixels. Counts may match with varied spacing. Do not fill gaps merely because they are empty; marks should guide attention along the wing. Keep roots simple and any vein lines sparse, not a dense grid.

Use a fine brush for antennae and short body hairs. Curve antennae outward with a few short terminal branches. Keep furry edges mainly beside the thorax and the abdomen more solid. Reduce detail-layer opacity or use dark brown rather than black if lines dominate.

## Compare pairs so one side does not become heavier

Compare corresponding tips, patches and band turns pair by pair, not only the whole moth. If one side is heavier, reduce its detail before adding more opposite. Balance means similar main rhythm, not unlimited equal information.

Hide the axis and sketch and zoom out. Check continuous wing outlines, body between roots and corresponding patches. A pale background circle may support it, but is not a strict alignment template. For edge cleanup, see [Cat silhouette exercise](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/aquarell-cat-silhouette/>).

## Common questions and a second version

**Can I draw it without a symmetry tool?** Yes. An axis and paired points establish proportions, while hand differences retain a painted feel. For greater regularity, reduce patterns so each pair is easy to compare.

**Why does it look like a decorative butterfly rather than a moth?** Mute colours, thicken the body slightly, broaden wings and reduce long pointed tails. This is stylisation, not species identification. Next change only patch shape while retaining wings and bands to compare pattern possibilities.

In a second version, remove edge dots and retain main patches plus one band. If clearer, keep that as the finished version. Pattern design is not scored by element count; major shapes, space and repeated rhythm should support one another.
