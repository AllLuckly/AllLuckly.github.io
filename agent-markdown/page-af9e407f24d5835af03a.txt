A flower-like sea anemone still needs an animal body organised into oral disc, column and attachment base. Draw an invented expanded anemone with tentacles surrounding the mouth rather than growing everywhere. Use public exterior references without touching, feeding or collecting animals. Tentacle count and colours are illustrative, not species identification.

## Reserve column and expanded crown

Use an approximately 1600×1700 px canvas, reserving the upper half for tentacles and lower half for a short column and low attachment rock. Sketch axis, shallow oral ellipse and base before fine strands. A modestly bent column still supports the disc and meets the rock instead of suspending the crown. Choose an elevated view showing both mouth and column side. Pixels are compositional and the rock supplies no collection-site evidence. Establish these regions before coral, fish or a complicated seabed.

## Separate oral-disc surface and central mouth

Make the oral disc a shallow visible surface at the column top, retaining tissue around a small distinct mouth. Do not turn the entire ellipse into a dark flowerpot cavity. Keep the mouth on that surface in perspective without moving the column to satisfy a screen centre. A short slit or small opening is enough for this exterior view; no digestive section is needed. Rejoin a floating disc to the column and remove unsupported stamens or pollen from a flower-like centre.

## Gather tentacle roots around the disc

Temporarily mark twelve representative tentacle roots around near and far disc margins, keeping the central mouth legible. Twelve is the resolved drawing count, not a fixed biological total or exact angular division. Attach roots around the disc rather than the rock, column waist or mouth slit. Test curved directions before thickness. Allow far roots to be concealed instead of forcing twelve fully exposed roots into a black star. The ledger organises the illustration rather than counting every natural tentacle.

## Give tentacles taper and occlusion without suckers

Thicken roots and taper tips along one or two gentle bends, avoiding identical rigid spikes. Near tentacles may cross part of the disc with explicit front/back stops. Add neither rows of suckers nor petal veins; microscopic stinging cells need no invented large bumps. Restore background gaps in a fused fan, or give selected near strands volume before subduing far ones. A larger count does not make the drawing anatomically better.

## Join the column continuously to its pedal disc

Continue the column down from the oral disc into a slightly spreading pedal attachment on the rock. It is neither feet nor plant roots; add no claws. A few longitudinal tones suggest surface without internal pipes. Reduce hard container-like edges if it resembles a flowerpot. A narrow contact shadow can settle a floating base without a black glue blob. Match attachment to the rock plane. This establishes no calculated adhesion or observed residency on a real stone.

## Use colour to clarify roots and central space

Use invented grey-green column, warmer disc and muted pale-violet tentacles, firmer near and lighter far. This palette is no species rule or universal three-colour exterior. Keep a small dark mouth distinct from root shadows. Break highlights along soft volume instead of identical white borders and subdue rock colours. Reduce palette complexity before deleting required connections. No light or flow experiment occurred, so shading establishes no glow, feeding or contraction behaviour.

## Trace base to disc before each tentacle

Hide materials and trace rock contact–pedal disc–column–oral disc continuously, then assign all twelve representative tentacles to roots. Check occlusion before adding a supposedly missing root on the column; a surplus may be a highlight mistaken for tissue. Adjust a few curves to expose a closed-off disc rather than move the mouth into ground. Repair crossings locally instead of changing global layers and breaking others. Guide numbers are not automatic organ counts; document omissions and uncertain areas.
