Hand-draw a simplified fern frond, using branch origins, a length gradient and overlap to establish hierarchy instead of scattering tiny leaves.

English translation of the [Chinese original](<https://allluckly.cn/guides/aquarell-fern-frond-branch-hierarchy/>), translated on 2026-11-05. Original publication dates, attribution, code and version notes are retained.

A fern frond appears intricate, but the drawing first needs a main axis, then side branches growing from it, and only then smaller leaf shapes. Use an invented, simplified pinnate form without claiming to represent every fern type. The task is to clarify three levels: small leaflets should not float beside the axis, and branches should not all share the same length and width. Place their origins and directions manually in Aquarell, using repeated simplification checks to decide which details matter.

## Begin with one gestural main axis

Use an approximately 1400×1900 px portrait canvas with a pale warm-grey background. Curve the main axis gently from lower left toward upper right, leaving space above its tip. Prepare lighter and darker greens, but keep the sketch monochrome. Taper the axis from a slightly thicker lower portion to a fine upper end where its boundaries meet, rather than ending it with an abruptly thick flat cut. Separate sketch, main axis, side branches and leaflets. Reduce the view and inspect the gesture. Correct an unintended angular bend in the axis rather than covering it with side leaves. Draw only this framework initially, so premature leaflet density cannot obscure the overall direction.

## Attach each side branch to the axis

Mark four to six pairs or near-pairs of side-branch origins along the axis, allowing slight left/right offsets instead of strict mirror symmetry. Angle them upward. For this exercise, shorten them toward the tip to create a broader lower and narrower upper silhouette. Do not position every origin outside the axis with an invisible gap. Trace each branch tip back to its named attachment point. If neighbouring branches cross into a bundle, adjust origin spacing or angle rather than fading them into mist. This is a checkable simplified structure: its length gradient serves the chosen shape and is not a fixed rule for every fern.

## Check overall density with an envelope

Lightly draw an envelope around the branch tips to inspect how width changes around the axis and tapers toward the top. Leave some background gaps inside rather than filling a solid green feather. Compare lower, middle and upper sections. If the middle becomes much wider than the bottom, decide whether this is an intentional turn or a length-distribution error. The envelope is only a guide and need not appear in the final image. Temporarily thicken the side branches into a simplified silhouette, confirming one layered frond before returning to fine lines. This preserves clear branching logic more easily than changing the outline after drawing dozens of leaflets.

## Grow leaflets from their own side branches

Choose a middle side branch and place a few narrow leaf shapes on both sides, pointing generally toward its tip and touching the branch at their bases. Complete this sample before applying the same connection logic elsewhere without copying an identical leaflet count. Shorten leaflets toward each branch tip instead of ending it with the largest one. If a leaflet floats in a gap, connect it clearly or remove it; botanical complexity does not excuse a missing attachment. At normal size, the small forms should organise around their side branch rather than each reaching independently from the main axis. Keep these two hierarchical levels distinct.

## Choose the front element at overlaps

Neighbouring branches may overlap locally, but choose one clear front/rear relationship at each crossing: retain the front leaflet contour and interrupt the rear one behind it. Drawing both complete boundaries would create a transparent crossing mesh. Use lighter green for a lit group and darker green for selected rear leaves, not black for every lower layer. Inspect the three most crowded places, identify front and rear leaves aloud or in notes, then refine their edges. If all leaves crowd one boundary, remove one or two or change a branch angle, retaining background gaps. Omission is a structural choice; every blank cell need not be filled.

## Vary colour by groups, not leaf by leaf

With upper-left light, keep the left/front somewhat lighter and branches turning toward the right/rear slightly darker. Organise two or three broad groups rather than alternating bright and dark on every leaflet like a string of lamps. The main axis may lean a little yellow-green. Add only small shadows at selected branch junctions, not a black dot at every base. Hide fine veins and check whether colour still connects one plant. If the leaflets resemble separate stickers, reduce excessive contrasts and bring similarly lit groups into related colours. Texture can remain sparse; attachment points, length and angle matter more than decorative colour spots.

## Inspect the three levels separately

Show the main axis alone, then add side branches and finally leaflets. Each added level should preserve the readability of what preceded it. If the axis disappears once leaflets are shown, reduce density or detail contrast rather than simply thickening the axis into a black stick. Save a simplified version with only three lower branches and check that leaflets still surround their own branch. Restore the envelope guide for comparison: the final outline can vary naturally but should not acquire a large unexplained foliage mass. This inspection uses existing layer visibility, not automatic plant recognition or an application feature that repairs branching order.

## Prioritise connections in reduced output

Inspect the exported PNG at its intended size, checking the axis tip, middle branch origins and narrowest background gaps. If leaflets merge into a blurred sawtooth edge, reduce their count and slightly widen gaps between groups rather than adding heavy black outlines to every leaflet. Keep the layered original and a skeleton-only preview. A future study can change the axis curve and redistribute side branches instead of copying the old frond and forcibly stretching it. Record a particular hierarchical error, such as a leaflet on the third upper branch touching the main axis directly. Identifying the wrong level is more useful than saying the foliage looks unnatural. Add only a few veins once the connections are sound.
