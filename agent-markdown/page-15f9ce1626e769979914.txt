## Locate both crowns on one ground plane

Use an approximately1900×1300 px landscape canvas with pale grey-beige background, mother left and smaller daughter right, leaving space for the link. Block two crown regions on one soil surface, mother taller/denser and daughter quieter. Separate crowns, runner, stalks, blades and ground shadow; use medium green. Remove separate pots and establish shared ground if unrelated plants. Reduce daughter foliage if competing rather than breaking the connection. Pixels and spacing are composition, not planting distance, age or growth rate. Do not invent unobserved underground parts.

## Extend the runner from the mother crown

Run a slender stem from the mother’s near-ground crown, bending gently right near the soil and entering the daughter node. Trace it completely, not from a leaf tip or fruit. Width may resemble a stalk or be slightly finer for illustration; distinguish organs through attachment and direction. Restore ground-level route if root-like, organising leaves/roots from crowns. Add local contact and height changes if a suspended rope. Portions can lift slightly without a perfectly horizontal line. Curvature establishes no wind, tension or actual growth stage.

## Organise daughter leaves and roots at the node

Place a small crown at the daughter node, leaf stalks rising outward. If roots need indication, show only a few short downward lines at soil contact. It is not one large leaf dangling from the runner, nor roots drawn as horizontal branches. Label runner, crown, stalks and visible roots on a checking layer, then remove labels. Restore soil relation and upward foliage if fruit-on-branch-like; reconnect an unexplained sprout to the node. Root count/length are simplified, not actual counts or a claim of excavated specimen observation.

## Give each leaf group three leaflets on one stalk

Choose two clear mother leaf groups and one daughter group, each with three leaflets: a central leaflet and one on either side at a shared stalk top. Nine visible leaflets represent these three illustrated groups, not a whole-plant total. Count three groups of three in pale guides, then apply occlusion. Reconnect shared stalks and crowns if nine independently planted leaves; restore three-leaflet grouping if a five-petal flower. Far leaflets may narrow or be partly hidden, but checking geometry retains three rather than silently calling two a complete group.

## Resolve crossings without merging stalk and runner

A mother stalk may hide a short runner base, but the rightward crown-to-daughter path should remain traceable. Separate depth with edges rather than black knots or a stalk attached to a daughter leaflet. Temporarily use blue-grey stalks and brown-green runner, then restore natural colours. Reduce stalk count or move blades if a confusing net, not labels on every line. A visual crossing is not necessarily branching. Only the selected node organises this daughter; do not add a new crown at every crossing for decorative abundance.

## Keep leaflet and soil texture distinct

Use broad oval leaflets with restrained serrations, brighter centres and cooler far surfaces, varying all nine rather than stamping copies. Keep soil broad pale grey-brown with sparse grains that do not hide the runner. Add crown contact and blade turning if floating cutouts; vary regularly spaced groups if hanging ornaments. ## Inspect the complete crown-to-crown route

At roughly quarter size, trace left crown along one runner to right crown, then upward to three daughter leaflets and downward to soil contact. Keep the link visible against background and foliage. Open a clearer middle segment and adjust stem colour if two grass clumps, not a woody branch. Restore crown, stalk and soil relation if a fallen leaf.
