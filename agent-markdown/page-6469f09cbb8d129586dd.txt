Pliers are not arbitrary bent rods crossing in the middle: each half continues from a handle through the pivot to its corresponding jaw. Draw an invented slightly open pair, tracing both halves and their common pivot before metal and coated grips. This static object study checks connections rather than using real tool functions or mechanical specifications as its visual criteria.

## Establish the overall jaw-and-handle pose

Use an approximately 1500×1700 px portrait canvas with jaws above and slightly separated handles below, sharing a pivot in the upper-middle area. Start with two principal paths through it, identifying each half before a large circular joint. Separate background, far half, near half, grips and light accents. Check balanced proportions small; oversized jaws should not reduce short handles to ornaments. Trace each handle-to-jaw path before adding width. The crossing is not the endpoint of every line.

## Use different greys to trace the halves

Temporarily paint one half pale grey and the other darker, clarifying front/back order at their crossing. Each continues through the pivot to the correct jaw, rather than suddenly joining the wrong one. If confusing, sketch the two half silhouettes separately and imagine overlaying them around one centre. Restore a shared metal palette once the structure works, so the finish does not depend on diagnostic colours. This visual separation is not presented as real disassembly instructions. Retain the native halves independently for subsequent corrections.

## Keep one definite pivot with restrained thickness

Use a small circular or elliptical pivot face aligned with the crossing metal planes. It may hide nearby contours but should not become a giant disc concealing every path. Mark its centre in mid-grey before a narrow side plane. Repair a pasted-button appearance through surrounding junctions and thickness; reduce a tyre-like joint’s size. Reserve deepest shade for local contacts and keep the pivot centre readable. Attractive reflections cannot compensate when the viewer cannot tell where the halves turn.

## Relate the jaw opening to both halves

Extend the two jaws above the pivot with inner faces toward each other and a clear negative space between. Establish broad contours before sparse inner teeth. If one jaw faces outward, retrace its half rather than asking texture to explain it. The gap need not be perfectly symmetrical but should belong to two faces that could approach. Start with a modest opening rather than an extreme pose; understandable jaw/pivot/handle relations are more useful than exaggeration. Stabilise proportions first.

## Keep coherent width through curved handles

Curve the handles apart below the pivot, tapering along their lengths to slightly rounded ends. Coated grips fit over metal with definite starting edges rather than suddenly becoming disconnected thick columns. Clarify the near handle and interrupt the hidden far section. Restore rigid turns to a rope-like result, or trace pivot continuity if they resemble unrelated spoons. Preserve broad and side planes instead of drawing every part as a thick line. Visible surfaces, not repeated outlining, create volume.

## Suggest teeth in a limited jaw area

Add only three or four short changes along a jaw’s inner surface, compressed with that plane and echoed more faintly opposite. Do not turn every tooth into a front-facing zigzag. Lower spike-like teeth and restore the shared inner surface; reduce count rather than repeatedly sharpen a blurred dark strip. Begin in grey and reserve the deepest gaps for one or two near places. An illustration need not reproduce real tooth pitch, but its texture must remain attached to a coherent mass.

## Use different contrast for metal and grips

Give the metal head selected narrow lights and firmer value transitions, while grip shading stays broader and quieter under the same upper-left light. Do not light one part from upper right and another from lower left. Check in grey before choosing a muted grip colour. Restore a local metallic jump in a plastic-like tool, or soften sharp white grip lines in a chrome-like grip. Edge and surface rules can distinguish materials before different bright colours; unify the final palette afterward.

## Separate grounding from crossing shadows

Here the tool lies on the table, with narrow contacts and a softening lower-right cast shadow; the pivot crossing has its own small overlap shadow. Do not combine those purposes into a black outline. A slightly raised handle may cast softer shade rather than every edge being equally grounded. Review opening small, pivot paths normally and erroneous crossings close up. Repair floating through ground contact. Hide shadows before fixing a confused joint instead of darkening all greys to supposedly solve every problem at once.

## Check paths with the pivot cap hidden

In a copy, hide the pivot cap and trace both halves from handle to jaw, then restore the cover to decide which edges it hides. A cap enlarged to conceal errors signals a path needing repair. Temporary colour can expose the correct jaw connection; do not skip this because the crossing alone looks plausible. Restore colour after the test and retain before/after previews. Change one half while holding the other and shared centre fixed, making improvement identifiable and protecting correct connections.

## Change only the opening angle for a second study

Keep diagnostic paths, unaccented structure and a PNG with separate halves. Next, rotate one half slightly around the same centre and rebuild jaw gap, crossing occlusion and handle angle with material colours unchanged. Moving only jaws while retaining old handle positions breaks continuity. Check the export: fine teeth may be omitted at use size, but main paths and the small pivot must survive. Record how a change at one end affects the other, testing a connected object rather than an arbitrary crossing pattern.
