If someone had left earlier, they could have caught the bus may imagine an unrealized past, not add an actual bus journey. ## Preserve the complete conditional and context

Choose a short conditional passage and record book, edition, translator, chapter and location. Preserve both condition and result rather than only a memorable outcome. Read surrounding facts about departure and arrival before judging. Create five fields: wording, hypothetical condition, imagined result, actual evidence and unknowns. ## Fix both layers with an explicitly framed example

Invent a bus departing at seven, Lin arriving at seven ten and missing that bus. Lin then says, If I had arrived ten minutes earlier, I might have caught it. Facts are departure seven, arrival seven ten and no journey on that bus. Hypothetical content is earlier arrival and possible boarding. Preserve might, not certainly. These times teach scope, not a guarantee that arriving exactly at seven permits real boarding. Label all sentences invented. Explicit facts establish the distinction from imagined events.

## Check unrealized condition and actual result separately

The example explicitly supports both no earlier arrival and no ride on that bus; do not derive them solely from each other through sentence form. If a work gives counterfactual wording without an established outcome, record contextual implication separately from missing factual evidence rather than supplying every opposite event. Might have caught differs from certainly would have caught. Separate grammatical cues and explicit narration so a construction cannot replace plot evidence. This is bounded reading, not an exhaustive theory of counterfactual semantics.

## Do not treat every future condition as already unrealized

Compare If we arrive early tomorrow, we will watch the opening. Its future reference does not establish lateness or a missed opening merely from if. Contrast time and context, not just keywords. Distinguish a stated plan, wish or conjecture from execution and an unconfirmed future event. Translated would, might or equivalent wording also needs sentence scope rather than word-by-word labels. This comparison checks your summary, not a reason to rewrite the author into tidier grammar.

## Attribute the imagined result to its source

Identify whether a character regrets, someone consoles, narration explores or another voice is quoted. Lin’s might is not narrator-verified knowledge of an alternative world. Preserve competing conditions and wording rather than selecting the most confident voice. If later information says the bus left early, append its effect on the hypothesis without erasing the earlier statement. Regret does not establish author attitude. Label personal interpretation and scope instead of turning psychological speculation into time evidence.

## Preserve actual and imagined actions in the summary

Write two summaries: Lin actually arrived late and missed this bus; Lin imagines earlier arrival might have allowed boarding. Lin arrived early and caught it changes event status, not merely brevity by omitting if. Preserve negation, modality, time and object scope in real passages too: this bus is not every bus. Keep conditional framing at the start of a short excerpt card rather than removing it for a catchy title. Separate source and analysis so export still distinguishes established events from character hypotheses.

## Check quotation and time reference when claims conflict

For caught and missed together, check hypothetical result, mistaken recollection or a different bus before alleging contradiction. Recover a conditional spanning paragraphs. Consult reliable grammar or retain uncertainty about unfamiliar tense rather than assigning past time from one auxiliary. Check missing scan words against the original without personal additions changing the condition. Search locates wording, not whether an alternative life occurred. Preserve both locators and attributions as questions rather than resetting progress or editing source events into agreement.
