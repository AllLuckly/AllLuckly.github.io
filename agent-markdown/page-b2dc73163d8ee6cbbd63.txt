Two raptorial forelegs belong to a mantis’s six legs, not a human pair of hands added to four others. Draw a resting individual from an oblique front-side view in Aquarell, checking thoracic roots, stout forefemora and folded tibiae. ## Locate head, thorax, abdomen and three leg pairs

Use an approximately1700×1500 px canvas with pale grey-beige background, head upper left and abdomen lower right. Block triangular head, elongated prothorax and broader abdomen, marking three leg-pair roots on the thorax, not eyes or abdominal tip. Separate torso, far/near legs, head and detail; use grey-green. Establish head and foreleg envelope if stick-insect-like; reduce upright human-like stance if humanoid. ## Attach forelegs to the prothorax, not the head

Place the near foreleg root on the prothorax, extending a basal segment forward to a stout femur, with a separate corresponding far root. Head proximity does not permit roots in the jaw as giant mouthparts. Trace both unspined forelegs to thorax, retaining hidden checking paths where occluded. ## Make femur and tibia form a traceable fold

Make the main forefemur stout, tibia folding inward at its distal joint with a clear angle/gap, then a finer tarsus. Locate three segments in different pale greys, then unify colour. Simplify small joints without reducing the whole leg to one hook. Remove an invented central screw if scissor-like; avoid two symmetric claw valves if crustacean-like. No exact angle is prescribed, nor future strike direction, speed or force inferred. Joint relationships matter more than exaggerated tips.

## Place spines along opposing inner leg edges

Suggest a few visible spines on opposing femoral/tibial edges, rooted in their segments rather than floating. Establish folding first; do not cover the body in spikes for ferocity. Reduce exactly matched teeth if meshing gears, or shorten and vary spacing if brushes. Depicted counts are omissions, not diagnostic spine formulas, species or sex. Do not show hidden far spines through the body or interpret every dark dot as a sharp structure. Opposing segment direction, not maximal specimen detail, is the task.

## Return four walking legs to middle and rear thorax

Draw one middle and one hind pair as slender supports, each returning to its thoracic region and ending on the chosen twig. Make near legs clearer, far legs quieter, with hidden parts only in guides. Raised forelegs mean four walking legs touch the twig here and two raptorial legs do not; not eight legs or antennae counted as legs. Count roots if spider-like. Reposition twig or segments if floating rather than adding a fifth walking leg. This pose does not claim a real mantis always moves or stands only on those four.

## Keep antennae and folded wings from becoming extra limbs

Add two fine head-rooted antennae, distinctly weaker than walking legs and not touching the twig as supports. For a winged reference, suggest folded wings above the abdomen without displaying hidden hindwings. Source wing presence/length rather than inventing four spread fans. Soften foot-like wingtip splits if extra-legged; reconnect thorax/abdomen if a floating leaf. Align eyes with head turn rather than suspended eyeballs. Use the twig only as support, adding no prey to turn morphology into an unobserved behavioural story.

## Check six legs and foreleg folding in silhouette

Hide spines and texture, count two front, two middle and two hind legs in silhouette, and keep the main femur/tibia angle from filling solid. Distinguish antennae and legs at small size and keep twig outside the thorax. Separate overlapping forelegs through pose/spacing, not a third raptorial leg. Recover far walking-leg roots locally rather than equalising every line weight. Layers help editing but do not establish anatomy; trace every root, then recheck after restoring colour/detail.
