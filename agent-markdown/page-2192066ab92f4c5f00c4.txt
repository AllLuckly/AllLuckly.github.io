Reviewing a lecture can become repeated seeking without remembering which step matters. Combine a permitted course video with ordinary notes, mapping questions to timestamps and recognizable content. iCouldPlay provides playback and seeking; keep the index on paper or in your existing notes rather than assuming automatic chapters, transcription or summarization. Start with roughly twenty minutes and validate the method before indexing a complete course or recording every sentence.

## Start with three questions

Write three concrete questions before playback: where a setting changes, why preparation comes first or which step to revisit after failure, rather than simply “lesson three.” Each should point to an explanation or action with context. Preview the course outline and choose what you tend to forget. Three dependable locations are more useful initially than dozens of untested times. Mark an unanswered question as not covered rather than inventing a tidy answer or timestamp.

## Fix the edition and time basis

Record filename, course title, duration and spoken language. Use displayed media time rather than wall-clock time or minutes actually spent at increased speed. Opening edits can move content, so matching duration alone does not prove edition identity. Share an index across quality copies only after checking identical content; otherwise keep them separate. Do not copy old-course times into a new edition merely because the title and poster match.

## Leave a short contextual lead-in

After locating an answer, move a few seconds before the action or explanation and record a useful start. Choose the lead-in from context, not a fixed ten-second rule; include the preceding sentence if it defines a term. Starting only at the final button press loses prerequisites. Distinguish the suggested starting time from the core visual event so introductory context is not mistaken for the wrong location. Watch once at normal speed before choosing a review speed.

## Pair every time with a content anchor

Add a recognizable clue to each time, such as a three-column table, a short keyword or an object changing colour. Summarize in your own words rather than copying long captions. An anchor need not identify a single frame, but should be recognizable nearby. A bare number can hide a minute-entry error; an anchor also helps relocate content after an edition change. For a static picture, use a spoken example or phrase and identify it as an audio clue.

## Separate the action result from its rationale

An answer may require both an action and its rationale at different times; record both rather than compressing them into one segment. The completed demonstration checks appearance, while the conditions explain applicability. Separate observed results from what you still do not understand and from actions actually performed on your device. If the demonstrated software version differs, index its logic and check your current interface before following it. The map retrieves evidence rather than deciding applicability for you.

## Test returning after leaving the passage

After three rows, play elsewhere and return using each suggested time, checking both anchor and context. Ordinary seeking may be approximate; start earlier and play forward without assuming direct time entry or frame stepping. Distinguish original and corrected times and record why they changed. If a location repeatedly lands in a transition, move to the complete explanation rather than favouring tidy whole minutes. Test each row after leaving it, not only while writing it.

## Record review speed and caption conditions

Only after return locations work should you try a review speed and note whether captions help with terminology. Speed changes elapsed viewing time, not the media positions in the index; keep them distinct. Slow down or pause when an operation becomes unclear rather than blaming the index. Pause before practising elsewhere so the course does not run past the result. If captions and speech disagree, record the question and review context instead of treating displayed text as infallible.

## Maintain a portable review table

Keep questions, edition identity, suggested starts, anchors, conclusions and open issues in ordinary portable text. For a new edition, relocate content by anchor before updating times rather than adding one guessed offset everywhere. Remove unnecessary detail after review. Personal summaries and location information suffice; do not redistribute the course transcript. Next time, answer without notes first, then retrieve evidence through the map to check that it supports understanding rather than becoming another organizational burden.
