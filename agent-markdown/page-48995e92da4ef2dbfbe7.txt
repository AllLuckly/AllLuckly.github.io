Remembering a sentence but forgetting its book is a common private-library task. Book Isle searches books, body text, and notes, but these are different result scopes. Narrowing keywords and checking context is more effective than repeatedly entering a whole remembered sentence.

## Prepare an example you know exists

Choose an EPUB, TXT, or text-layer PDF already downloaded and opened locally. Find a sentence with a distinctive phrase and note four to six Chinese characters, such as the phrase meaning “echoes in the valley.” Do not start from an undownloaded remote cover; a visible entry does not mean its text is locally searchable.

Also write a short personal note, such as “Compare this with the previous chapter.” This lets you test title, body, and notes separately. Verify the entry using known content before searching vague memories, avoiding confusion between wrong keywords and index preparation.

## Open unified search from the bookshelf

Use bookshelf search and check for a prompt such as “Search books, text, or notes.” Enter at least two characters and search part of the title to confirm the book appears. Then use the short phrase from its text and inspect the result category. If scope controls exist, choose Text to reduce distractions.

Do not stop at a result title. Open a result, verify its snippet and book, then open the text and check the chapter or page. PDF results may locate a page, while reflowable text locates a chapter and text position; page-number information need not match. Correct context establishes search success.

## Adjust queries gradually from short terms

Initially avoid complex punctuation and copying entire paragraphs. Files may differ in full-width spaces, line breaks, Simplified or Traditional characters, and quote styles; longer queries are more vulnerable to small mismatches. Start with a distinctive noun and judge chapter and snippet context. If results are excessive, add a word or two rather than immediately clearing everything and rebuilding the index.

Suppose you remember “Light reflected on the stone road after the rain.” Try “stone road,” then “after the rain.” Do not begin with a full sentence reconstructed from memory; the original may say street instead of stone road. Search does not prove memory correct, so allow differences between the original and your recollection.

## Use note search and full-text search separately

Switch to Notes, search a phrase from your own comment, and check its associated book and selected text. This finds personal markers such as “Reread next time” or “Check the source.” Those words can appear in note results without occurring in the book, and a word in the book does not mean you wrote a note about it.

For research, use stable topic labels in notes, such as “Quotation candidate” or “Needs verification.” Then searches cover your reading work as well as the author's text. Keep keywords concise and consistent rather than using different synonyms each time. After finding a note, reread its original context to avoid detached excerpts.

## If nothing is found, check whether the content is searchable

Import support does not mean every object enters the full-text index. Text pictured in scanned PDFs, comic images, and embedded diagrams is not automatically ordinary searchable body text. Compare a simple document with selectable text to determine whether the source lacks a text layer. For scanned material, obtain an OCR-processed copy, check recognition accuracy, and then import and test it.

After a large import, allow time for parsing and indexing before retrying the same known keyword. If settings offer Rebuild Background Index, consider it only after confirming readable content, a correct query, and persistent failure after waiting. Rebuilding cannot repair damaged files or convert unsupported formats into readable text; repeatedly triggering it merely adds waiting.

## Build reliable references from search results

After finding the passage, record book, author, chapter or page, and a short excerpt with your own explanation. Search snippets locate content but do not replace full context. With same-named books and multiple formats, confirm the exact copy before adding a quotation to notes.

If the book has EPUB and PDF versions, locate the quotation in an easily searched text edition, then verify the fixed page number in the PDF. Editions may add or remove content, so recheck the wording after switching. Treating search as a locating tool rather than taking the first result as a final answer better supports long-term study and research.

## Frequently asked questions

**If the title is searchable but the text is not, is the book corrupt?** Not necessarily. Titles are metadata, while body search needs parsable text and an index. Open the book and test a short phrase known to exist. An undownloaded remote entry and a scan without a text layer are different causes.

**Why does a one-character query return nothing?** The current entry may require at least two characters, and one character often produces excessive irrelevant matches. Meaningful two-character words or short phrases meet the requirement and make results easier to judge.

**What if the highlighted term is not visible after opening a result?** Confirm chapter or page first, then inspect nearby content. Typography changes, file editions, or unusual text structures may affect precise positioning. Retain the query and use the contents list or PDF page number to continue checking. A temporarily absent highlight does not invalidate the whole result.
