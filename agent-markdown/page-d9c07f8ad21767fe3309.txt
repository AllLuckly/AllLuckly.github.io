## Compute dot product and product of lengths separately

The dot product is 1×2+2×1=4. Both squared lengths are five, so each norm is √5 and their product five, not √10. Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the absolute dot product by that product; four is strictly below five here. Label 4,√5,√5,5 by role, not as angle degrees or a sum of lengths. In the squared version the sides are sixteen and twenty-five; square both sides consistently rather than only one.

```text
u=(1,2); v=(2,1)
u dot v=4; norm(u)^2=5; norm(v)^2=5
abs(u dot v)=4<5=norm(u)*norm(v)
5*5-4^2=9=(1*1-2*2)^2
w=3*u: dot=15, norm_product=15
w=-3*u: dot=-15, absolute_dot=15, norm_product=15
w=(0,0): both sides=0; no angle quotient
```

## Check the gap with a two-dimensional square identity

For real (a,b),(c,d), expansion gives (a²+b²)(c²+d²)−(ac+bd)²=(ad−bc)². The square is nonnegative, independently proving the two-dimensional inequality. Here ad−bc=1×1−2×2=−3, whose square nine equals twenty-five minus sixteen. Retain the minus sign inside the square rather than call −3 the inequality gap. This algebra does not depend on drawing precision or measured screen angles.

## Check equality for positive and negative multiples

With w=3u=(3,6), dot and norm product are fifteen, attaining equality. With w=−3u=(−3,−6), dot is negative fifteen but absolute dot still fifteen, again equality. Proportional nonzero real vectors include opposite directions for the absolute-value bound. In the upper-bound statement without absolute value u·w≤norm product, the opposite direction instead gives the negative lower endpoint. Separate absolute-value equality from a positive maximum; a negative dot product does not rule out equality in Cauchy–Schwarz.

## Zero vectors attain equality but cannot divide a norm

For w=(0,0), both dot and norm product are zero, so equality holds. Do not divide by its norm to form a scalar ratio or assign an angle from 0/0. State the complete condition: either vector zero gives equality; with both nonzero, equality holds exactly for proportional vectors. The square identity includes zero cases. Original u/v have equal norms but determinant −3≠0, so are not proportional. Equal length alone is insufficient; keep equal norm and a common direction line separate.

Doubling original v gives dot eight and norm product ten, still strict. Multiplication by zero yields equality at zero without proving original proportionality. This degenerate transformation loses information about the original equality case. Recheck zero status before extending a nonzero-scaling observation to a zero multiplier.

## Carry equality into a constrained maximum

For real x,y with x²+y²=5, target x+2y is u·(x,y). Both norms are √5, so absolute value is at most five and the positive maximum at most five. Candidate (1,2) satisfies the constraint and attains five, proving attainability; (−1,−2) gives minimum negative five. Original (2,1) is feasible but yields four, not the maximum. Do not stop at the bound or use (3,6) as a maximizing candidate: squared norm forty-five violates this constraint.

With x²+y²\<5 instead, the target is below five: five is an approachable bound, unattained, and (1,2) is infeasible. Along t(1,2),0\<t\<1, values 5t approach five without attaining it. With ≤5 the boundary is feasible again. Check strictness together with existence of a maximum rather than reuse maximum five merely because the bound number stayed unchanged.
