Draw a triangle with a point inside its base and connect it to the opposite vertex. Use Stewart’s identity to check the cevian length, carefully pairing squared sides with base segments and distinguishing length from its square. Calculate externally and illustrate manually.

## Fix the base point and a general cevian

Let A=(0,0), B=(10,0), C=(4,6), D=(3,0), with D inside AB. Base 10 splits into AD=3 and DB=7. CD is a cevian, with no median or right-angle symbol assumed. The triangle is nondegenerate and lengths share one dimensionless unit. Separate conditions, triangle, cevian and calculation labels, then save native work. D is not midpoint 5 or vertically below C, so visual neatness cannot make CD a median or altitude. Those extra properties are unnecessary here. Interior D makes 3 and 7 positive lengths summing to 10. Moving D outside AB requires a newly declared external/directed-segment convention, not retention of the positive 3/7 ledger. This diagram makes no such extension.

## Independently calculate three squared distances

CA²=4²+6²=52, CB²=(4−10)²+6²=72, and CD²=(4−3)²+6²=37. Record lengths√52,√72,√37 rather than place 52 or 37 in a length column. C-to-D has horizontal difference 1 and vertical difference 6, so CD is not vertical. Retain these coordinate values as an independent control before solving by the theorem. Both routes must use the same D, not silently substitute C’s perpendicular foot.

## Pair each squared side with its correct base segment

Write CA²·DB+CB²·AD=AB·(CD²+AD·DB). Thus 52×7+72×3=364+216=580. CA² pairs with the segment toward B, not AD merely because A appears in the label. Two thin ledger leaders can show pairing without becoming triangle edges. Preserve the named-segment identity before substitution; another textbook’s a,b,m,n labels do not license memorizing symbols without objects. Both sides have cubic-length units: a squared side times a segment versus a length times a sum of squared-length terms. Substituting CA instead of CA² breaks dimensional consistency even if numbers look close.

## Solve for the length and substitute back

The right side is 10(d²+21)=580, giving d²+21=58, d²=37 and positive length d=√37≈6.083. Neither the negative root nor 37 itself is the segment length. Substitution gives 10(37+21)=580, agreeing with coordinates. Incorrect pairing 52×3+72×7 gives 660 and d²=45, contradicted by coordinate differences. Keep the wrong pairing as a control instead of stretching CD to resemble√45 and calling that verification.

```text
A=(0,0); B=(10,0); C=(4,6); D=(3,0)
AD=3; DB=7; AB=10
CA^2=52; CB^2=72; CD^2=37
CA^2*DB+CB^2*AD=52*7+72*3=580
AB*(CD^2+AD*DB)=10*(37+21)=580
wrong pairing: 52*3+72*7=660 -> d^2=45 (incorrect)
```

## Explain the pairing through general coordinates

On paper let base length be c, point coordinate m, other segment n=c−m and vertex(p,h). Adjacent side squares are p²+h² and(p−c)²+h²; cevian square is(p−m)²+h². Expanding n times the first plus m times the second yields c times\[cevian square+mn\], explaining the pairing. Here c=10,m=3,p=4,h=6. Retain interior-point and nondegeneracy conditions rather than promote one numerical equality into a universal spatial theorem. The p term is−2cmp on both sides, while mc²−cm²=cm(c−m)=cmn. This explains the mn term algebraically rather than inventing it to force the numerical 580 check.
