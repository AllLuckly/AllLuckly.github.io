A numerical calculation can look plausible while mixing length and area. Read a self-made explanation or a trusted textbook in Book Isle and keep a unit ledger using fictional rectangles and toy-car data. Fill the notes yourself; this does not depend on automatic formula recognition or answer checking, and the results are not engineering measurements.

## Record the quantity, not only its number

Choose a short example containing metres, centimetres and seconds. Record the title, edition, chapter, original sentence and location. Identify what each number measures: length, width, distance or elapsed time, rather than treating four numbers as interchangeable inputs. If the width has no stated unit, mark it unspecified instead of guessing. Read definitions and captions before creating an external record or using available notes. If scan text cannot be selected, transcribe manually and check the image; finding the same number in search does not identify the same example. Retain a nearby locating phrase.

## Unify length units before addition

The original rectangle is two metres long and fifty centimetres wide. First write two metres as two hundred centimetres; the perimeter is then twice the sum of two hundred and fifty, giving five hundred centimetres, or five metres. Alternatively express the width as 0.5 metres: twice the sum of 2 and 0.5 again gives five metres. Both routes must describe the same length. Adding 2 to 50 and labelling 52 metres does not resolve units. If the textbook converts before forming the expression, retain that step in its actual position rather than silently moving it to the end.

## Square the conversion factor for area

The same rectangle has area 2 metres times 0.5 metres, giving one square metre. In centimetres, 200 times 50 gives ten thousand square centimetres. One metre equals one hundred centimetres, but one square metre does not equal one hundred square centimetres: both directions receive the factor of 100. Imagine a square with each side 100 centimetres and check that their product is 10,000. Give length conversion and area conversion separate ledger lines so the previous factor is not copied blindly. This checks a specified flat rectangle; it does not infer a real room area from a comic or screen pixels.

## Track both numerator and denominator for speed

For a second example, a toy car travels 300 metres in 150 seconds, so its average speed is 2 metres per second. To obtain kilometres per hour, convert metres to kilometres and seconds to hours, or check that 1 metre per second equals 3.6 kilometres per hour, giving 7.2 kilometres per hour. Write the direction of time conversion: an hour contains 3,600 seconds; changing the label from seconds to hours does not mean dividing the speed by 3,600. This average covers the specified whole interval and does not show that the car travelled two metres in every individual second. It is not a real transport plan.

## Use dimensions to reject incompatible expressions

Label perimeter as length, area as length squared, and average speed as length divided by time. If a line says area equals length plus width, the dimensions disagree: stop accepting the result and revisit the surrounding definitions and multiplication sign. If the text instead defines a semiperimeter, length plus width may be correct; do not rewrite a formula without its quantity name. Keep the original expression, your dimension expansion and the conditions requiring review for every doubt, rather than only a cross mark. Check unclear unit-letter case or type on the source page before treating a display uncertainty as a mathematical error.

## Recalculate coefficients even when dimensions agree

Create an explicit wrong control: 2 metres times 0.5 metres equals 10 square metres. Both sides have area dimensions, so a dimension check passes, but the multiplication is wrong; the correct result is still one square metre. Likewise, writing 500 centimetres as 50 metres retains length dimensions but uses the wrong factor. Record three separate checks: compatible dimensions, recalculated numbers and satisfied conditions. Passing one does not certify the others. Change only one quantity or unit in a control, label the original and control separately, and do not attribute your new result to the printed book.

## Troubleshoot missing exponents and unit placement

If the square exponent appears as an ordinary 2 in Book Isle, enlarge that exact position and compare the same file in an already available trusted reader; changing a font does not prove the original is repaired. Record differences between searchable text and the image separately; a scan may lack a usable text layer. For a wrapped expression, reconnect its denominator and following unit before copying it, rather than attaching a unit to the next term. If still unclear, mark it unresolved and consult author corrections or the original publication file. Do not insert a guess into a quotation or overwrite the retained original.

## Exit and return to verify the whole unit chain

Exit the reader and return, then relocate the rectangle and toy-car examples from your record rather than checking against remembered mental arithmetic. Match five metres, one square metre, ten thousand square centimetres and 7.2 kilometres per hour to their quantities, and explain both wrong controls. Save six fields: original unit, target unit, conversion factor, operation, result and conditions; explicitly leave unspecified conditions blank. Completion means another person can review the unit chain in the same edition. It does not mean every numerical expression can be checked automatically; definitions must be read again in a new chapter.
