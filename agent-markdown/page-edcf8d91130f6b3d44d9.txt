A character moving from image left to image right may reflect a changed viewpoint rather than crossing the room. Track a short sequence through doors, windows and stairs. Produce a textual route with evidence and unknowns, without assuming an automatically generated map or inferring precise architectural dimensions.

## Choose a scene with identifiable landmarks

Choose an authorised already-read sequence covering one movement, such as entry to exit or downstairs to upstairs. Inspect surrounding pages for at least two recognisable landmarks, not merely similar background colours. Record edition, chapter and boundary scenes. Keep the scope small rather than expanding to a whole city: a stable sample makes viewpoint changes and actual movement testable while leaving unshown space genuinely unknown.

## Name landmarks before drawing a map

Assign simple labels to doors, windows and passages with their first page/panel reference. Record relationships without exact directions or distances. Comic perspective serves expression and does not necessarily support a measured floor plan. Label characters consistently and record inside, outside or unknown positions. Ordinary notes suffice. Any later sketch should be identified as your personal aid, not an official map supplied by the work or app.

## Record characters relative to the landmarks

For each panel record nearby landmarks, orientation and contact with a door or rail before comparing the next. A half-body crop does not establish foot position or a completed turn. Use occlusion, door state and scale as explicit clues, marking uncertainty where needed instead of inventing an unseen corridor. Focus on spatial relationships rather than retelling the plot so each later question leads to specific panel evidence.

## Separate viewpoint from relocation

Check whether landmarks reverse sides and occlusion relationships change before inferring another viewing direction. A reverse view can place the same door on the opposite side, but the work may also cut elsewhere. Compare unique details and dialogue. Similar colour is insufficient, and a film-axis convention is not a universal requirement for comics. Retain competing explanations until later evidence distinguishes them instead of immediately calling the geography an authoring error.

## Use thresholds and floors as route nodes

Use shown crossings of doors, corners and stairs as nodes and leave unshown intervals open. “Through door A, then toward window B” survives viewpoint changes better than “far to the right.” Mark cuts as omitted movement rather than calculating real-world travel time. Arrows in a personal sketch can indicate observed sequence instead of compass direction. The result can clarify pursuit or a missed encounter without turning your reconstruction into supposedly canonical geography.

## Save the decisive transition pages

Bookmark entry, viewpoint change and exit pages and supplement each with a panel address. Inspect neighbouring panels rather than relying on similar cropped details. On tall images use scene and dialogue landmarks, not screen height. Link externally if remarks are unavailable. Recheck a new edition/source even when an old bookmark still opens; accessibility alone does not prove unchanged evidence.

## Test the route with a reverse question

Ask which nodes a return to the starting point would actually rely on and which you only inferred. Revisit the scene for missed doors or landmarks. Keep unresolved space unresolved. If you confused screen direction with building orientation, record the correction and revise the route. State conclusions for these pages rather than claiming the complete building layout or measured travel distance.

## Acceptance and uncertainties

Finish with recognisable landmarks, panel-supported movement, separate viewpoint/location reasoning and working returns. Resolve mistaken direction from source order and context rather than tap settings. Check source quality before guessing blurry detail. Retain a short route and unknowns for later evidence; this can also help with street scenes without implying automatic route tracking or background recognition.
