Manually arrange white keys C through B and two black-key groups, then verify positions and labels.

## Define the keyboard range

Draw one C-through-B section of a modern piano keyboard with seven white keys and five black keys. This is a diagram, not a complete keyboard or an instrument that produces sound. Yamaha’s keyboard guide shows white note names C, D, E, F, G, A, B and repeating groups of two and three black keys. Write the order on paper before drawing in VectorPaint. If a specific register is required, check that instrument’s labeling convention instead of automatically calling this C middle C.

## Plan canvas and layers

Create an approximately 1600 by 900 pixel landscape canvas using the current interface and save an editable original. Reserve space for a title above, keyboard in the middle, labels below and margins at both sides. Dimensions are composition choices. Separate white keys, black keys, labels and background with available layers or objects. If interface labels differ by device, use identifiable entries. Do not assume the app generates or plays music automatically.

## Make seven white keys

Draw a vertical light rectangle with a clear, restrained outline. Use an available duplication method or copy manually to make seven adjacent white keys of equal width and height. In your working sketch label them C D E F G A B from left to right. Count rectangles, not only boundaries: seven keys have six internal seams. Align lower edges before adding black keys and do not let their later placement change white-key widths.

## Place black keys in groups of two and three

Overlay five narrower, shorter dark rectangles on the upper white-key area. The first two lie near the C-D and D-E boundaries; the next three near F-G, G-A and A-B. There is no black key between E and F. This drawing ends at B and does not include the next C, so do not add a black key beyond the right edge. Black keys sit in front, covering portions of the upper white keys.

## Verify positions and space

Check the five positions from a paper table: C-D, D-E, F-G, G-A and A-B. Leave the E-F gap between groups without adding an extra white key. Align black-key lower edges approximately and leave room at the bottoms of white keys for note names. Place their centers near corresponding white-key seams for this diagram; it is not a manufacturing drawing of a particular piano.

## Check placement with illustrative coordinates

For a working sketch, make each white key 40 units wide and 180 high, giving a total width of 280. With the left edge at x=0, internal seams lie at x=40, 80, 120, 160, 200 and 240. Use black rectangles 24 wide and 110 high centered at x=40, 80, 160, 200 and 240; leave the E-F seam at x=120 without a black key. These numbers check placement, not actual piano-part dimensions, and do not claim all physical black keys are exactly centered.

This leaves 70 units visible below each black key for clear labels. If reducing the complete graphic for a phone, scale the key shapes together and adjust label size for the final reading context; do not shrink text alone. Keep the coordinate table in your working notes so a shifted duplicate can be corrected against its seam. The rightmost edge is the boundary of B, not a drawn next C.

## Label natural notes separately

Place C, D, E, F, G, A and B in the lower white-key areas or below the keyboard, centered on each key. Omit sharp and flat labels on the black keys for this basic layout so note names, key signatures and playing technique are not conflated. At small scale letters should not sit on seams or cover black keys. A title such as “Keyboard section from C to B” makes clear that the left C is this example’s start.

## Check stacking and readability

Hide text temporarily and count seven white keys with black keys above them, then restore labels and verify each column. In grayscale or low brightness, white-key outlines, dark keys and labels should remain distinct. If outlines are too faint, darken them moderately without turning adjacent keys into a single dark bar. Check that black keys are not hidden by background or white-key objects and do not enter the title area.

## Check again in reverse

Read C, D, E, F, G, A, B from the left, then verify B, A, G, F, E, D, C from the right. Next count only black keys: two, a gap, then three. If you see two and four, check for an extra object at E-F or beyond B; if only four, inspect object visibility and overlap one by one. Record the result rather than assuming that snapping or duplication guarantees the count.

## Make the endpoint explicit

This illustration includes twelve keys from C through B but does not draw the next higher C. To show the octave interval from one C to the next, add that next white key in a separate copy and relabel the range rather than calling the existing B a C. When extending the diagram, recheck the repeating two-and-three black-key groups, title and total count.

## Save and inspect output

Save the editable original and use a share or export entry actually visible in the current version to save a separate image. Reopen and check seven white keys, five black keys in the two-and-three grouping, seven letters and canvas corners. Return to identify text and key shapes separately. If keys or labels are missing, check visibility, crop and location before overwriting the only original. Include the keyboard-layout source and mark unperformed device, export and reopening steps pending.

## Check the gap with an incorrect sample

Copy the keyboard sketch on paper and deliberately add an extra black key between the E and F white keys. Compare it with the original and count the two-key and three-key groups. The incorrect sample has six black keys and breaks the gap between groups; the final diagram keeps five. Discard the practice error after finding it and do not retain the extra key in the finished illustration.
