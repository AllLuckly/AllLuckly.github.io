Use public plant information and a permitted reference to arrange American lotus flower stalks, rounded leaves and waterline occlusion.

## Identify the plant and reference

The US National Park Service describes American lotus leaves as either floating or supported above the water by long stalks, with solitary yellow flowers on long stalks often rising above leaves. Choose a permitted photograph showing flower, leaves and water and record source and usage rights. Do not infer submerged roots if they are not shown. Do not pick, trample or move wild plants for a composition. This exercise studies the open flower and leaves rather than the existing seedhead study.

## Plan the canvas and water level

Create an approximately 1800 by 1400 pixel landscape canvas in the current Aquarell interface and save an editable original. The size is a composition choice. Lightly mark the water surface and positions of flower and main leaves, leaving room for the highest petal and any reflection. Separate sketch, water, leaves, flower and details using available functions. If interface labels differ, use identifiable entries. Decide which leaves float and which are raised before painting occlusion.

## Establish spatial relationships

Block the flower as an upward-opening shallow bowl at the top of a separate flower stalk. Add one or two main leaves, observing their heights, elliptical projection and distance from the flower. Flower stalk and leaf stalk separately support their structures; do not draw the flower emerging directly from the middle of a leaf. Where a near leaf hides a stalk, draw only visible portions on either side and suggest continuity behind it rather than a line through the leaf.

## Arrange yellow petals outward from the center

Mark the flower center first, then layer the many yellow petals from inside outward according to the photograph. Near petals may obscure part of the center; far petals can appear narrower or higher. Do not place all petals as equal-length sun rays. Start with two or three light and middle yellow shapes for front and back layers, then add a few visible edges. If the center is hidden, do not force a complete disk or copy seedhead holes into the open flower.

## Draw leaf projection and stalks

Block each leaf as a large round or elliptical shape. A leaf facing the viewer can look rounder, while a tilted one projects flatter. Place the stalk’s connection beneath the leaf as the reference shows, using a few light lines outward from near the center to suggest veins. Do not make every edge petal-like or serrated. If an edge curls, change only the visible portion; a floating and raised leaf need not share the same perspective.

## Separate above-water and submerged portions

Where a floating leaf touches water, use water color and local occlusion to describe the leaf edge. For a raised leaf retain the visible stalk and distinguish above-water and underwater portions at the waterline. Do not extend a flower stalk unchanged as a dark line to the bottom of the canvas. Use the reference to decide whether to include reflection: it can extend generally downward as lighter broken shapes; do not force mirror effects absent from the photograph.

## Separate values for leaves, flower and water

Block calm blue-gray or green-gray water first, using middle and dark greens for leaf planes and edges. Paint the flower in light and middle yellows so petals remain readable. Where leaves overlap, distinguish the near edge from the far leaf by color or value rather than outlining every leaf in equally thick black. If petals merge with a light sky, adjust local background value before deciding whether a fine outline is needed.

## Compare floating and raised leaves

Make two thumbnails: in the first a leaf rests close to the waterline; in the second its stalk supports it above the water. Keep flower position and viewpoint fixed. Compare visible stalk lengths, leaf-edge overlap and waterline, then choose the combination closest to the reference. These thumbnails show different leaf states, not a leaf automatically rising at one moment. The drawing reflects visible structure and does not claim measured heights.

## Review stalks and background

Temporarily hide fine veins, ripples and petal edges, leaving flower, major leaves and water shapes. Trace each visible stalk to its flower or leaf, avoiding a sudden connection to a different organ. Restore restrained details and check value separation between petals and leaves. Record visible and hidden regions and photographic usage rights. There is no need to invent submerged roots, rhizomes or unseen stalks.

When comparing before-and-after thumbnails, keep the flower and major leaves at similar displayed sizes. Enlargement alone can make stalk connections look clearer without showing whether occlusion actually improved.

## Save and inspect the output

Save the editable original, then use a share or export entry actually visible in the current version to save a separate image. Reopen it and inspect the top petal, leaf edges, waterline and canvas corners. Return to identify water, flower and leaf layers in the original. If a leaf or stalk is missing, check visibility, crop and file location before overwriting the only original. Include public plant and photographic sources; mark device, export or reopening steps pending if unperformed.

## Compare two leaf projections with bounding boxes

On the sketch draw a tight rectangle around one floating leaf and one raised leaf. Note each approximate width and height and compare whether perspective makes their oval projections differ. If both look like identical perfect circles, consult the reference and adjust only the visible contours, then hide the rectangles and check again. These boxes compare projections in the image and do not establish equal real leaf sizes.
