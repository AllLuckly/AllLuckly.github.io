Use a dorsal green sea turtle reference to observe carapace scutes, head and the attachment of two pairs of flippers.

## Define the reference and species scope

NOAA Fisheries describes a central row of scutes on a green turtle’s carapace and four lateral scutes on each side; the US National Park Service notes that green turtle scutes meet like tiles rather than overlapping. Choose a permitted green turtle photo showing carapace and flippers, recording source and species. Color and visible structure differ with individual, age and view; do not identify a turtle from the painting or approach or touch one.

## Compose the dorsal pose

Create an approximately 1800 by 1400 landscape canvas in the current Aquarell interface and save an editable original; the size is a suggestion. Choose a slightly elevated swimming reference with head to one side and space around both pairs of flippers, following the photo for which are visible. Separate sketch, carapace, head and flippers, water and background using available layers. Lightly mark the head-to-tail axis.

## Block the carapace

Use the reference to draw a somewhat elongated outer shell, comparing front and rear width and overall proportions. Perspective may widen the near side, so do not treat a dorsal ellipse as fixed from every angle. Mark head and tail directions, then shrink the image to ensure the shell does not crowd the canvas. Test the shape as one color mass before adding scutes; if it resembles a flat coin, revise contour and values from the photo.

## Arrange central and lateral scutes

Sketch a longitudinal central row, then observe visible lateral and marginal scutes. NOAA’s typical description is five central scutes and four lateral scutes on each side; if occlusion or perspective compresses them, draw only what can be seen instead of squeezing overlapping tiles into the view to reach a count. Number lightly for a check, then hide numbers. Adjacent carapace plates should meet like tiles, not be drawn as overlapping scales.

## Connect head and front flippers

Attach the head at the front of the carapace and observe the neck transition rather than sticking an isolated circle onto the shell. Locate the two front-flipper roots and directions from the photo, blocking their long broad shapes before refining edges. In an oblique dorsal view a near flipper may look larger or hide the shell edge. Mark hidden roots unknown and do not draw flipper lines through the carapace.

## Place rear flippers and tail

Observe the rear pair of flippers relative to the carapace’s back edge. Sketch visible directions and compare their lengths and widths with front flippers rather than copying four identical shapes. The tail may be hidden by shell, flippers or viewpoint; draw only confirmed parts. At thumbnail size check that head-to-tail direction and limb pose agree, then revise occlusion.

## Model the shell with color

Choose a middle brown, olive or gray-green from the photo; the name green turtle does not require a bright green shell. Lighten lit areas and deepen unlit edges, allowing restrained variation inside scutes without giving each an identical pure-white highlight. Use related but distinguishable values for head and flippers and keep junctions clear. With scute lines hidden, the shell should still read as one volume.

## Handle water and occlusion

Suggest water with low-contrast blue-green, then decide from the reference whether the turtle is underwater, near the surface or ashore; these settings do not share one automatic waterline. In a swimming picture, stop surface marks and reflections around the body rather than drawing uninterrupted white lines through it. If the photo shows no reflection, omit one or identify it as a design choice. Decorative ripples do not establish actual depth.

## Compare scute connections

Duplicate the shell sketch into two thumbnails: meeting adjacent scute edges in one and deliberately overlapping them in the other, keeping silhouette and color fixed. Compare with NPS’s green turtle description and choose the meeting version, then check which boundaries the photo actually shows. Mark a plate hidden by reflection or flipper for later observation; do not transfer another sea-turtle species’ shell pattern to a green turtle.

## Inspect flipper roots and silhouette

Zoom into the head connection, front and rear flipper roots and shell edge, checking for outlines passing through the shell or accidental white gaps. If shell texture covers a flipper, revise stacking or reduce texture; if the far flipper is unclear, retain occlusion instead of copying the near one. At phone reading size confirm head, shell and front and rear flippers remain recognizable and check corners for cropped tips.

## Save and reopen

Save the editable original, then use a share or export entry actually shown in the current version to save an image separately. Reopen it to inspect scutes, head and flippers, water and crop, then return to identify sketch and subject layers. If a flipper is missing, check visibility, crop and file location first; a flattened image lacking layers does not establish original damage. Keep source and permission notes and mark unperformed device and export steps pending.

## Record typical and visible counts

Use two columns in reference notes: NOAA’s typical central and lateral counts, and the number actually confirmed in this photo. They can differ when reflection or flippers hide the far shell edge; fewer visible scutes do not establish missing scutes. Decide whether each boundary belongs to the center, side or margin before drawing fine lines, so small marginal scutes are not counted as lateral scutes.
