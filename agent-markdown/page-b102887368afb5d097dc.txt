Hand-draw an unoccupied escalator, separating level treads, upright risers, sloping side panels and turning handrails while checking both landings.

English translation of the [Chinese original](<https://allluckly.cn/guides/aquarell-escalator-treads-handrail-route/>), translated on 2026-11-06. Original publication dates, attribution, code and version notes are retained.

Parallel diagonal lines between two handrails do not necessarily explain escalator steps. Draw an unoccupied indoor escalator rising from a near low landing toward an upper-right high landing, distinguishing treads, risers, sloping side panels and turning rails. This is a static scene illustration, not engineering dimensions, maintenance or riding instructions, and no mechanical simulation is claimed. Layers separate hand-drawn structures so each line belongs to an identifiable surface.

## Set both landings and the rising direction

Use an approximately 1900×1500 px landscape canvas with a pale warm-grey background. Mark a near lower landing and upper-right higher landing as ground planes, then connect them with sloping sides. The axis sets direction only; do not place the upper landing at the same height like another floor tile. Separate sketch, steps, side panels, handrails and shadows. Inspect one-colour masses for a continuous low-to-high route. If it resembles an unsupported bridge, clarify both platform junctions. Begin with one escalator, leaving out a neighbouring reverse unit and crowds so competing lines do not obscure its rise.

## Separate level treads from upright risers

Begin with four to six large schematic steps, each combining a visible level tread with an upright riser, like a continuous folded stair rather than plates sliding down a ramp. Cross edges follow escalator width and depth edges share perspective. Reduce visible step size with distance without abruptly omitting a level. Distinguish the two planes in greys before fine grooves. If it resembles blinds, check for missing risers between crossbars. If it resembles a ramp, restore each step turn. This is a simplified count, not the real number of machine steps; readable alternation of two surface types is the goal.

## Slope the side panel along the whole route

Run side panels smoothly along the overall incline rather than making their exterior a giant sawtooth matching every step. The near panel hides some step ends, while a narrower far panel bounds the other side, both changing width with perspective. Hide handrails and check that all steps remain between the same side boundaries. Correct endpoints and overlap if one pierces a panel instead of thickening the panel to conceal it. Use mid blue-grey against lighter treads. A panel is not another row of steps and should not protrude outward at alternating levels without a deliberately supported decorative reason.

## Give handrails width and turning ends

Draw narrow dark handrail bands along the side tops, their incline matching the panels, with ends curving toward their respective entry areas. Establish continuous centre routes before width rather than black rods stopping in midair. Near and far bands need not be screen-parallel, but must share a spatial explanation. Keep end turns smaller than playground slides and bands narrower than side panels. Hide steps and locate both ends against the landings. This depicts an exterior route without real internal return or drive mechanisms; simplified arcs are not maintenance sections.

## Join the route coherently at both landings

Let lower steps transition into a flat section meeting the near landing, and do likewise at the upper landing without leaving the last step suspended before a black gap. A simple pale-grey transition plate may suggest the join without specifying real comb-plate geometry. Trace treads from low end to high end, checking for no abrupt jump across a side panel or to another level. If the upper end attaches midway up a wall, expose its platform plane and correct overlap rather than explaining it with doors or windows. Minimal detail still needs continuity; a solid-black patch must not conceal every unclear endpoint.

## Separate tread, riser and side panel values

Assume soft upper-left indoor light, with lighter treads, somewhat darker risers and broad continuous panel midtones connecting both ends. Avoid pure-white/pure-black barcodes at every step. Keep handrails dark with restrained reflections rather than thicker and brighter than all other structure. Hide grooves and decoration to inspect the three value groups, then restore a few tread marks. Suggest grooves on only two near steps and omit them at distance to avoid dense reduction artefacts. If treads and risers merge, correct broad values and boundaries rather than adding more texture; texture cannot replace step orientation.

## Trace two route systems before combining them

In one copy, show only steps and platforms to check low-to-high continuity. In another, show panels, handrails and platforms to inspect end turns. Combine them and check that near-side overlap does not swallow every tread. Compare a small counterexample with sloping treads to see why the stair reading fails, changing orientation alone without also changing light and count. If the original still resembles a slide, repair tread/riser alternation instead of adding a standing person to insist on its purpose. Let structure read independently before later people or surroundings; finish this unoccupied scene first.

## Inspect the far end and narrow treads after export

Save the layered original and inspect the last two steps, upper landing junction and handrail ends in the PNG at normal size. If distant steps merge into a black line, reduce count or grooves and moderately separate plane values rather than thickening every edge. If a handrail turn breaks, reconnect its route instead of using a bright spot as a false join. Keep a texture-free preview. A later scene-colour change still needs spatial-route checks. Record a precise issue, such as the third upper tread disappearing along a panel edge, to target the right layer. Deliver a static illustration, not a claim of real equipment operational testing.
