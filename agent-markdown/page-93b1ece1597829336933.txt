Pea tendrils and large stipules can become a branch with springs if attached arbitrarily. Draw a stem-and-leaf study of a conventional leafy pea in Aquarell, choosing three leaflet pairs, branched terminal tendrils and a basal stipule pair. Cultivars vary; this does not represent every form, including leafless types, and pods are omitted to preserve attachment clarity.

## Distinguish the stem from one leaf axis

On an approximately 1800×1500 px canvas, draw a short upright stem left and one rachis extending upper right from a node. Temporarily colour them separately and mark their junction before blades. Retain an upward stem continuation if the subject reads as one horizontal branch; repair contact if it looks like crossed plants. Angles and lengths are compositional, not measured internodes. Study one node rather than crowding an entire plant with flowers and fruit.

## Arrange three leaflet pairs along the rachis

Choose three rachis positions with one leaflet each side, six in this illustration. Mark six short axes with varied spacing. Attach them to the compound-leaf axis, not six separate branches from the stem. Spread them along the rachis if clustered like a palmate leaf. Inspect stray colour near the terminal tendril if seven appear. Three pairs are a conventional-form choice, not grounds for rejecting real differences by leaf age or cultivar.

## Give each leaflet a blade and midrib

Build broad elliptic-to-oblong blades with narrowed basal attachments, not discs pierced by a rachis. Each midrib runs from its own base to tip, with a few oriented side veins. Permit modest near/far overlap without losing a whole pair. Repair middle-pierced “beads” at their bases. Reduce overly dark blocks while preserving contours. Abbreviate margins from the reference rather than adding mechanical teeth, and claim neither automatic venation nor exact specimen replication.

## Attach tendrils at the compound-leaf apex

Continue beyond the last leaflet pair into a thinner terminal tendril route with a few branches and curls. Do not grow springs from every leaflet tip or attach the terminal system as an independent stem branch. Establish one continuous path before two or three curves. Remove broad tissue if it resembles a seventh leaflet. Use uneven curves, tapered tips and omission to avoid a metal spring. Branch and turn counts are invented, not indicators of age or support strength.

## Place stipules at the stem–leaf junction

At the left node, draw a pair of broad stipules beside the leaf base, separate from six rachis leaflets. They may be prominent but do not enter the three-pair count or grow from tendril tips. A near stipule can occlude the junction while the far one retains a visible edge. Remove pod-like walls or seeds and keep them close to the leaf base rather than as unrelated new stem leaves. Size is schematic, not measured, and hidden edges stay behind the stem.

## Distinguish stem, rachis and tendrils by width

Use a sturdy stem, narrower rachis and finer terminal tendrils with smooth transitions. Two or three related greens distinguish faces and distance against warm white. Repair tendrils thicker than the stem and stop hidden edges if the rachis seems transparent. Shadows may be omitted to avoid extra tendril-like lines. Restrained texture keeps nodes visible; attachment precedes mottling. Layer groups enable manual local revision, not built-in plant modelling.

## Troubleshoot three distinct categories

Hide texture and identify three leaflet pairs, trace terminal branched tendrils, then return to the node for two stipules. Reposition stipules mistaken for a fourth pair and relocate a margin-attached tendril to the rachis apex. Trace continuous routes instead of concealing errors with a green knot. Retain the conventional leafy-form limit when references differ; do not mix cultivar traits. This checks the drawing, not observed climbing behaviour or a growth sequence inferred from curl direction.

## Reopen without counting stipules as leaflets

Save native work, hide temporary colours, axes and numbers, then export PNG. Reopen small, count six rachis leaflets and distinguish basal stipules from terminal tendrils. Enlarge to inspect broken paths or unattached loops. Restore lost tendrils with local contrast and simpler ground, not thick branches. Revise, export again and reopen editable groups, noting three pairs as illustrative. Success concerns organ positions, not cultivar identification, a growth record or an automatically generated scientific specimen plate.
