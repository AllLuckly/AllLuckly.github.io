A book may say a quantity grew by one quarter without readers retaining its starting amount. An increase of three items alone also does not establish relative growth. Practise both expressions with fictional paper-hat counts, locating source passages in Book Isle and keeping original value, new value, unit and arithmetic in external notes. No automatic statistics are used, and the teaching example supplies no real business or high-stakes judgement.

## Establish that the compared quantities match

Find a passage actually describing change and record book, edition, chapter, wording and table or page location, reading surrounding definitions first. Separately note object, time span, unit and measurement scope. Twelve hats and fifteen hats are comparable; twelve boxes and fifteen hats require a hats-per-box definition before subtraction. The invented example is two counts of the same stock, in hats, first twelve and then fifteen, with no real transaction. If the source compares totals from groups of different sizes, retain that scope difference rather than silently converting it into growth of one population over time.

## Keep units and direction in absolute change

Write new value fifteen minus original twelve equals three hats. This absolute increase carries a unit. Reverse the order: twelve minus fifteen equals negative three hats, a decrease from that starting point. Do not retain three without direction or call three hats three percentage points. For terms such as net increase or balance, check whether other components were deducted; the simple example has none. If the source supplies only a difference without endpoint values, mark the original or new value missing. A difference of three does not reconstruct one unique pair of quantities.

## Fix the original-value denominator for relative change

Under this example’s convention, relative change is new minus original, divided by original. Three divided by twelve is one quarter, or twenty-five percent. Thus twelve to fifteen is an increase of three hats, relatively twenty-five percent. The denominator is not fifteen, three or an arbitrary average of endpoints. Preserve parentheses and the original-value label, not a percentage sign alone. If the book explicitly defines another rate, such as one using the endpoint average, record that definition under its own name. Do not combine the methods into one result or assume different definitions necessarily contradict one another.

## Do not merely reverse the sign of a percentage

Starting anew from fifteen and returning to twelve still decreases by three hats, but relative change is negative three divided by fifteen, or negative twenty percent. An earlier twenty-five-percent increase does not imply a twenty-five-percent decrease returns it: the denominators differ. Check that fifteen times eighty percent equals twelve. This illustrates different relative proportions for the same absolute gap, not a profit-and-loss analysis. If a summary says up or down twenty-five without its baseline, restore the original value so the two directions are not mistaken for a symmetric process.

## Distinguish a quantity percentage from percentage-point difference

Use a separate teaching record: a completion proportion moves from twenty percent to thirty percent. The difference is ten percentage points; relative to the original proportion, ten divided by twenty is a fifty-percent increase. Twenty and thirty percent are already proportions, unlike the hat counts. Do not exchange the examples’ units. Keep percentage-point difference and relative change in separate columns with formulas and baselines. If a book merely says rose ten, check whether it means ten hats, ten percentage points or ten percent. Do not infer the unit from a headline; leave it unspecified when the source does.

## Handle zero baselines and rounding honestly

With an original value of zero, this divide-by-original formula cannot be used directly. Do not fill the gap with one hundred percent or infinite growth. You may record a known absolute change and mark the relative result not computable under this definition, checking whether the author supplies another convention. Rounded displayed values may yield a slightly different calculated percentage. Record precision rather than inventing hidden decimals to force agreement. Consult an authorized original table or author explanation when more data are needed; a reader cannot recover precise values never supplied.

## Check direction and scope before disputing a result

When results differ, check old/new order, unit, original denominator, population scope and rounding before disputing the result; do not immediately edit the book’s numbers. Locate passages separately if prose and table refer to different periods rather than joining them into one change. Use available viewing controls to read a wide table’s full headings so units such as per hundred people are not missed. Work on paper or in a familiar external calculator and record input, without claiming formula recognition in Book Isle. Preserve passage, arithmetic and question when unresolved, instead of replacing supplied data with an estimate.

## Recalculate both directions after leaving and returning

Leave the reader and return, locating original and new values by chapter, table and wording. Without consulting your notes, recompute twelve to fifteen as three hats and twenty-five percent, then fifteen to twelve as negative three hats and negative twenty percent. Separately verify ten percentage points and relative fifty percent for the proportion example. The summary must retain object, endpoint values, unit, denominator and definition, with missing conditions marked. Completion means explaining different percentages for the same absolute gap, not memorizing twenty-five. Preserve the book file separately from personal calculations.
