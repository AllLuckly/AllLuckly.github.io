Hands are often difficult not because of numerous wrinkles but because the directions and overlaps of five fingers are unclear. Choose a hand lightly holding a cup handle or naturally half-bent, and divide it into palm, thumb, and finger segments. Understand the pose through simple shapes before deciding which joint lines matter, rather than drawing every finger as an equal-length tube.

## Choose a pose with visible joints

Observe your other hand or use a reference photo you took. Avoid a tightly clenched fist or strong foreshortening pointing at the camera initially. Choose a view with the back of the hand slightly upward and the fingers naturally bent. Side lighting helps distinguish the planes of the palm and joints. Do not hold the hand tense for a long time to maintain the pose; take a photo and relax.

Create a 1400×1100 pixel canvas with a pale-gray base. Let the hand extend from a lower-left wrist toward the upper right, occupying roughly two-thirds of the image. Separate sketch, structural tones, and details into layers, using only three shades of brown-gray. Avoid complex skin colors initially so color does not distract from structure.

## The palm mass is not a regular rectangle

Represent the palm with a slightly tilted quadrilateral, narrower at the wrist and broader at the finger bases. Try about 420 pixels long and 330 wide, adjusting to observation. Mark the four finger bases, which usually form an arc rather than a straight line. The middle-finger base projects more and the little-finger base sits lower; assess the overall rhythm first.

The thumb extends independently from the palm's side, attached through a small angled mass, not on the same line as the index-finger base. The wrist narrows slightly from the palm before widening toward the forearm. Zoom out: the palm and thumb masses together should already show the hand's direction rather than remain unrelated geometric shapes.

## Divide fingers according to joint turns

Begin each finger with two or three directional lines marking bends, then add width. Proximal segments are usually wider than the ends, and fingertips become rounder and narrower. The four fingers differ in length and need not bend equally. Compare the visible lengths in the reference rather than forcing a fixed numerical ratio.

Understand the direction of hidden finger segments without drawing their complete outlines. If the middle finger covers the ring finger, the latter may reveal only a side and tip. Drawing invisible portions as solid contours makes the hand look transparent. Keep faint construction lines in the sketch, but draw only visible edges on the final layer.

## Check whether the pose is convincing through its gaps

Observe the gap between thumb and index finger, the small triangle between two bent fingers, and the indentation between palm and wrist. These negative shapes provide a cross-check on finger positions. If gaps are overly regular or disappear entirely, adjust joint directions before adding nails. Refine gaps with a small eraser on the sketch layer and compare before and after.

For a hand holding an object, sketch a simplified object outline first, such as an elliptical cup handle or a straight pen. Fingers should overlap around it rather than pass through it. The object is a pose reference and needs little detail. Combine negative-space observation with [Branch negative-space exercise](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/aquarell-negative-space-branches/>); the method of judging gaps still works with a different subject.

## Connect the back of the hand and joints with large shadows

With light from the upper left, the upper back of the hand is lighter, while the lower-right side and inner bends are darker. Connect these shadows into a few mid-gray areas instead of shading around every finger. Short dark marks can occur at joint turns, while lit fingertips remain pale. The darkest areas usually occur where fingers touch each other or press against an object; keep them small.

Light planes follow the back of the hand and the finger joints rather than becoming white dots on each finger. Lay in broad areas with a larger brush, then refine joint turns with a smaller one. If the hand looks like a string of sausages, every segment is probably separated by the same dark line. Remove complete divisions and connect adjacent segments naturally through tone.

## Keep only necessary nail and wrinkle information

Draw nails as simple shapes following the fingertips; a far-side nail may reveal only a small part. Avoid white borders on every nail and use a color only slightly different from its surroundings. Use short, broken wrinkle lines concentrated inside bends. One or two tendon directions on the back of the hand are enough; do not turn it into a dense map.

Lower the detail layer's opacity and see whether the pose remains recognizable. If hiding details makes the hand more natural, the wrinkles or nails are too strong. For choosing monochrome tones, see [Monochrome portrait exercise](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/aquarell-monochrome-portrait/>); both emphasize establishing the large forms before adding a little useful information.

## Check all five fingers without detailing each individually

At completion, check thumb direction, the arc of finger bases, and the wrist connection, then inspect the fingers in overlap order. All five need not be fully visible, but visible portions should explain the hidden parts. Zoom out to assess the overall gesture and in to check for extra contours creating an illusion of a sixth finger.

**Why does the hand look stiff?** Common causes include four parallel fingers, identical bends, or right angles at every joint. Arrange the finger bases along an arc, vary their bends, and soften some turns. This usually helps more than adding wrinkles.

**Where should I restart when the drawing is inaccurate?** Return to the palm and thumb masses and check their angles, then the direction of the longest finger. Do not start patching each tiny fingertip. In the next drawing, keep the same hand but change the thickness of the object it holds, observing the joint angles as a structural comparison.
