Use a folded paper boat as a reference for rim overlaps, folded-plane values and water contact.

## Prepare a model and viewpoint

Use Durham University’s paper-boat activity as a starting point and fold a model, or choose a finished-boat photo with clear permission. Its example has raised ends and a triangular interior; different folds can have different rims, so do not impose one shape on every boat. Place the model on a table and choose a slightly elevated oblique view that shows both inside and outside. Record light direction and viewpoint; if working from a photo alone, record its source and usage permission.

## Save the canvas and plan the subject

In the current Aquarell interface create an approximately 1800 by 1400 landscape canvas and save an editable original; this size is an exercise choice. Let the boat occupy roughly two thirds of the width with space on both sides. Use available layers for sketch, boat, background and water, following the labels actually displayed on your device. Draw a horizontal reference and decide whether the boat is on a table or in an illustrated water scene; the latter does not require putting the paper model in water.

## Set proportions with a bounding box

Lightly draw a box around the silhouette, marking both pointed ends and the visible lowest point. Compare the end heights: an oblique view need not place them at the same height or make them perfectly symmetrical. Use the distance between tips to judge the height of the central folded plane. Shrink the view and check whether the hull is too short or tall; revise the large box before adding paper texture or minor creases.

## Draw near and far rims

Start with the near rim and connect the ends with a continuous folded contour, then draw the far rim. The near rim can hide part of the interior, while the central plane can hide part of the far rim. Stop hidden lines at the occlusion rather than drawing through paper. The edge may vary in apparent thickness but paper itself is thin; avoid a thick wooden border. If the outline resembles a closed eye, check whether the two rims have merged and whether there is space for the visible interior.

## Build the central folded planes

Observe the raised triangular form and adjacent paper planes in the model. Mark its apex first and connect visible edges toward the rim. The triangle is not an actual sail: it and the surrounding planes are folded paper, and visible edges depend on the view. Use three slightly different off-whites for the front, side and shaded planes instead of making all paper identical white. If the lines become confusing, hide the construction sketch and keep only edges needed to explain the folds.

## Light the paper

Choose upper left as the exercise light. Use warm off-white on illuminated planes and a cooler gray on planes turned away. A narrow local dark band may appear near a fold, but use the reference to decide whether it is shadow or another paper edge. Do not trace every fold with an identical black line. Paper is not polished metal, so pure-white highlights are not needed on every point. At thumbnail size, the center triangle and near rim should still be distinguishable without texture.

## Place waterline and occlusion

For a water scene, choose an approximately horizontal waterline at the lower hull and decide how much is hidden. Show only hull above that line, covering the submerged area with water color; do not show the complete bottom while also claiming water hides it. Leave enough visible hull between the rims and waterline. If the reference model is on a table, waterline and immersion are compositional choices, not observations of actual flotation.

## Control reflection and background

Block the water in muted blue-gray while retaining the boat silhouette. If adding a reflection, place a softer and darker shape directly below the boat, with some connection near the waterline; ripples can break it, so every fold need not be mirrored. Hide the reflection layer and check whether the boat still reads. If its edge disappears against water, adjust the value difference rather than relying on a heavy black outline. Keep the background simple and leave clear space around both tips.

## Compare two rim treatments

Duplicate the sketch for two thumbnails: merge near and far rims into one line in the first, and retain the visible interior with the near rim overlapping it in the second. Keep tips, central triangle and waterline unchanged. Compare with the model and decide which better explains the space bounded by paper; record one observable overlap. Mark hidden areas as unknown rather than inferring unseen folds from the thumbnail.

## Troubleshoot breaks in the image

Zoom in on both tips, where the front rim meets the central plane, and where the waterline meets the silhouette. If a white gap appears, first decide whether it is a visible background opening or a coloring omission, then repair the appropriate layer rather than covering every junction with a thick line. If the interior seems to pass through the hull, revisit the near-rim occlusion in the sketch. Hide and restore the background to compare, without treating a single antialiased pixel as paper thickness.

## Save, export and review

Save the editable original first, then use a share or export option actually visible in the current version to save an image separately. Reopen the output and check both tips, the central folds, waterline and colors, then return to the original and identify sketch and boat layers. If an element is missing, check visibility, cropping and file location; do not expect a flattened image to retain original layers. Record the source, canvas dimensions and one overlap to improve next time. Mark device and export steps not actually performed as pending.
