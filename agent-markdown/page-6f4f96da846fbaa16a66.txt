Begin with the supporting branch and body contact, then arrange a raccoon’s face mask, rounded ears and ringed tail.

## Choose references and a picture goal

The US National Park Service describes the raccoon’s dark facial mask and black tail rings. Read the source and observe public photographs, choosing a still side-front pose on a branch. Do not approach, touch or feed wildlife for the picture. Use observable structures for your own illustration; the gray-brown palette, branch, leaves and pose are design choices, not evidence of age, sex or health.

## Create a canvas and editable layers

Create an approximately 1800 × 1400 pixel landscape canvas in Aquarell. This dimension serves the composition and does not assert real animal scale. Using layer, brush and save controls visible in the installed version, create working layers for sketch, branch, body, face and tail; record differences if the interface varies. Save an editable original first and retain source links separately. Do not publish a restricted reference photograph as your own work.

## Place the support and major body shapes

Draw a sturdy branch across the lower composition, using a gently rising upper edge as the animal’s support. Extend the branch beyond the frame rather than leaving a floating stub beneath the body. Sketch the body as a loose oval, head toward the upper left, with room for the tail on the right. Bring a lower body area close to the branch’s upper edge. Reserve room for legs and place the center of the torso over the support so the animal does not appear suspended in front of it.

## Connect the head and rounded ears

Attach a smaller rounded head on the left side of the torso, using a light centerline for gaze, then draw rounded ears on both sides of the crown. The near ear may be more complete and the far one partly hidden by perspective; both bases must reconnect to the head. Project the muzzle slightly forward and put the nose below the eyes. Avoid filling the shape with fur lines. Check that a continuous neck transition joins head to body instead of attaching a decorative face to the torso.

## Paint the mask while preserving eye boundaries

Place dark regions around the eyes and coordinate them with the turn of the face. Preserve lighter separation at the eyes and bridge of the nose instead of painting the entire head as one unstructured black patch. Near and far eyes can differ in size through perspective while remaining aligned to the face centerline. Use pale gray and warm brown for cheeks, then retain a tiny highlight in each eye. The source supports dark facial markings; this illustration simplifies their shape and hue from the chosen reference.

## Make visible limbs actually meet the branch

From the lower body sketch the major near forelimb and hindlimb outlines. First reconnect each visible limb to the torso, then arrange paw overlap against the branch. A few short curved marks can suggest a paw; do not invent a precise pose from an unclear photograph. Let the branch edge disappear briefly beneath a paw and add a small contact dark. Far limbs may remain partly hidden by the body. If leaves have been drawn on a separate layer, temporarily hide that layer; otherwise inspect a sketch without leaves and check that legs grow from the body, not from the branch.

## Attach the tail and arrange rings

Extend a broad bushy tail from the rump, curving it toward the lower right within the frame. Draw a continuous tail silhouette first, then wrap several alternating dark and light curved bands around its volume instead of making disconnected parallel bars. Leaves may obscure part of the tip, but the root must remain traceable. Temporarily fill the tail with one color and confirm it is not mistaken for a second branch before restoring the rings.

## Block color while retaining value structure

Wash the body with mid gray-brown, leaving cheeks, ear rims and underside slightly lighter. Use dark brown-gray for mask and tail rings rather than losing boundaries in pure black. Give the branch a related but slightly warmer brown and shade the body’s side away from the light. Establish structure with large color areas before adding a few short fur marks that follow body turns. If the mask and ears merge, separate them with a lighter cheek or edge gap.

## Check silhouette and contact separately

If fur marks and leaves have been drawn on separate layers, temporarily hide those layers; otherwise use a sketch without those details. Simplify body, head, tail and branch into fewer shapes, and view the whole image small. Ask in order: does the tail root meet the rump, is the mask on the face rather than the ears, and do visible paws overlap the branch? If color alone distinguishes them, first adjust silhouette or negative space. Restore details and record outline, face markings, tail rings and contact separately. If a part is heavily obscured, note the design choice rather than claiming verified anatomy.

## Make a final branch-contact check

If limbs merge with the branch at small scale, separate the lower body with a light background or negative space, then retain two clear contact darks. Do not turn every short line into a paw; simplify unclear details. If tail rings are missing in the export, first check original layer visibility and the exported target file.

## Save, export and recheck the original

Save the editable work and use the export or share entry actually offered by the installed version to save an image separately. Reopen the exported file at its destination and inspect face, tail tip and all four image corners. Reopen the original and hide the tail and branch layers in turn to check that both remain identifiable. If the entry or file identity is unclear, investigate the save location without overwriting the only original. Deliver the self-drawn image and source links; mark any unperformed export, reopening or device step pending.
