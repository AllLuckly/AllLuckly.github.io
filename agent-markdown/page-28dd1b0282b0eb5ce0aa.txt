Paint a male fiddler crab on a mudflat, focusing on two conspicuously unequal claws and their connections to the body. The U.S. National Park Service describes one large and one small male claw, while female claws are similar in size. This is a static pose, not evidence of an actual animal's behavior or environmental conditions.

## Prepare reference and draft

Read the source and study the carapace, differently sized claws, eyestalks and walking legs in photographs. Leave unclear parts unresolved rather than inventing them. Do not capture or touch animals. Make an approximately 1800 × 1400 landscape canvas and save an editable draft with separate sketch, body, claws, legs and mudflat layers. Dimensions organize the picture, not actual crab size. Follow canvas, layer and save controls visible in your installed version. Keep the reference link separate from the drawing.

## Use the carapace to set direction

Place a flattened horizontal carapace in the middle, its upper edge gently curved and front facing slightly left toward the viewer. Mark a faint center guide and the two claw attachment points; do not make it a spherical ball. Leave a narrow dark area beneath for leg connections. In a thumbnail, if the shell looks like an upright disc, adjust its flattened form and visible upper plane before adding texture. Guides are drawing aids, not measurements.

## Connect the large claw to one side

On the left, draw a short arm emerging from the body, then a larger palm joining two distinct claw fingers. Leave a narrow gap between the fingers ending at the palm's front edge; do not split the entire claw into halves. The claw may be raised a little, but its root must connect to the torso. Slightly curve the edges and use values to distinguish palm from fingers so it does not look like a machine clamp. Left and right are this composition's choices, not a claim that every male has its larger claw on the same side.

## Draw the smaller claw opposite

On the right, connect a shorter arm to a small pincer, noticeably smaller than the first. The claws need not have symmetric detail, but the small claw still needs a body connection rather than a floating triangle. Hide the mudflat and inspect the body and claws against a plain background. If only color makes the size difference clear, adjust silhouette sizes before restoring color. The source describes a sex-related size difference; a single drawing cannot identify every real individual.

## Arrange eyestalks and visible walking legs

Place two short eyestalks and small dark tips at the front of the carapace, not growing from its rear; use the chosen photograph to establish visibility. Add slender segmented legs on both sides. Near legs can obscure far parts and joints can change direction rather than all forming parallel lines. Connect visible legs to the body before checking whether their tips meet the mud. Leave occluded limbs for further observation rather than inventing a complete view. Separate large claw and leg layers so changes do not destroy each other.

## Color the claw as a volume

Block in the carapace with gray-brown, lighten the plane facing the chosen light and retain a narrow darker underside. Give the large claw a coordinating warmer color, a broad light plane on the palm and a darker downward-facing turn; use a similar color in a smaller area for the small claw. This palette is a drawing choice, not a claim about all fiddler crabs. If the large claw looks flat, add a clear side plane before piling on highlights. Leave space between eyestalk and large claw edge so they do not merge.

## Paint mud and a shallow burrow opening

Lay in low-saturation gray-brown mud and add short contact shadows beneath feet. An optional oval opening off to one side can have a light upper rim and dark interior. Keep it away from the claw and avoid making it resemble another foot. NPS says males may be near burrows and burrows provide shelter; this static illustration cannot prove the depicted crab dug that opening. A few irregular dots and shallow-water reflections suffice instead of filling the scene with texture.

## Check both claws as a single-color silhouette

Copy a simplified outline onto the sketch layer and fill carapace and both claws with one dark color, omitting eyes, pattern and mud. Reduce it to a phone-thumbnail size and see whether relative area and outline still distinguish the large and small claws. If shapes merge, clarify each connection and intervening negative space before changing color. Restore each claw's detail layer and verify the large pincer gap remains open. Label the silhouette as a working control and hide it before final export. A readable silhouette does not identify a real individual.

## Check, export and record

Hide texture, background and color details, then shrink the image to test whether large and small claws and the body remain distinct and whether legs emerge from the body rather than mud. Restore layers and save the draft. Export a separate image through a control actually offered in the installed version, open it elsewhere and inspect the pincer gap, eyestalks and frame. Reopen the draft and hide claws and background separately; if they differ, check file identity and visibility first. Deliver image, source and actual operations, marking unperformed export, reopening or device steps pending.
