“Two more pairs of hands are needed” may refer to workers, while another context might concern body parts. The word hands alone proves no figure of speech. ## Limit the working definition to part for whole

Use the part-for-whole definition in [Harvard’s poetic-terms guide](<https://poetry.harvard.edu/guide-poetic-terms>), practising only that direction rather than every historical classification. Prepare an original passage: “The captain said, ‘We still need two more pairs of hands.’ The steward replied, ‘Two new helpers will come aboard tomorrow morning.’” It is neither a real shipping record nor a classical quotation. Import a readable format and retain source, title and paragraph; reflow-dependent screen lines are insufficient. The definition supplies a concept, while this context must support its application.

## Record the part and candidate whole separately

Quote “two pairs of hands” in one field, candidate “two helpers” in another and the reply’s evidence in a third. Do not silently rewrite the original as two people or turn four literal hands into four people. The explicit reply establishes two, not a universal assumption about everyone’s anatomy. Attribute each sentence to captain or steward without inventing speech by the helpers. Save short selections where supported, otherwise paragraph/opening-phrase references externally, without a fictional one-click rhetoric panel.

## Use the reply without inventing background

The steward’s reply connects the missing hands to personnel, supporting part-for-whole reference. It establishes neither names, skills, actual arrival nor completed hiring. Coming aboard tomorrow is future arrangement, not completed action. Retain still needed, tomorrow morning and new; do not summarise as two helpers already aboard. Write that this passage supports hands referring to helpers, not that hands always means workers. Later changes require a new location/update rather than backfilling subsequent outcomes into this passage.

## Do not force literal hand actions into synecdoche

A control says, “The two helpers placed both pairs of hands on the table while the captain inspected their gloves for damage.” Hand position and glove inspection can be literal; identical wording does not carry over the labour-shortage reading. Actions involving hands still require checking whether the expression refers to people or body parts. This is no forced dictionary-choice test: actual texts may exploit both, and uncertainty should remain. Preserve original, control and interpretation separately rather than inserting your control as an author’s explanation.

## Distinguish part/whole from resemblance

This candidate depends on hands being parts of people and context referring to helpers, not helpers resembling a hand’s shape. An invented “The helper was like a key that unlocked the problem” uses a different comparison relation, not automatically synecdoche merely because a substitute image appears. State the relationship rather than classifying solely from like. Figures may overlap; one slogan excludes no complex case. A ship-name-for-crew association should be recorded for separate checking, not swept into part-for-whole without support.

## Preserve quantity and tone qualifiers

Keep two pairs/two helpers aligned without calculating total crew or days needed. Changing the line to “A few more hands would help” removes the exact count; do not retain two. Weariness or expectation may be tonal candidates, but an unstated shout cannot become a factual command or mistreatment. Part-for-person wording alone does not establish the author endorses treating workers as tools; that interpretation needs context. Record it as a question requiring evidence, not a moral verdict from a rhetorical label. Saved notes are not automatic endorsement or proof.

## Keep rewrites and translations distinct from the original

Rewrite the first line as “We still need two helpers”: personnel demand broadly remains, while direct naming replaces part-for-whole expression. Label your rewrite and retain the original hands wording. Pairs of hands is a gloss here, not a universal cross-language counting unit. If a published translation uses helpers, record the expressive change without declaring mistranslation from that difference alone. Monolingual checking is enough when no translation exists. Open saved/exported notes to check pair/person units and future-versus-arrived distinctions survive summarising or lost glyphs.

## Revisit relation and scope for acceptance

Retain both original sentences, part/whole candidate, reply evidence, literal control and direct-person rewrite. Four hands becoming four people requires unit repair; labour demand applied to every hands requires syntax/context; tomorrow turned into arrived requires tense repair. Leave reading, then return by paragraph and check the opening phrase, checking extractable text/scope before treating failed search as absence or deleting books. Limit conclusions to the evidenced passage and retain unresolved references as candidates. The result is a sentence-checkable rhetorical record, not automatic annotation, anatomical statistics or certification of a work’s values.
