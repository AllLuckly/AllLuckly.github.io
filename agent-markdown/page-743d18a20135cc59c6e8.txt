A knight chess piece suggests an animal with few carved planes while connecting securely to a round base. Draw an invented wooden piece, establishing forehead, muzzle, neck and chest before limited carving. This is neither an anatomical horse study nor a copied branded design. Add grain last, after the form is clear.

## Set head, neck and base proportions

On an approximately 1300×1700 px portrait canvas, leave space before a left-facing muzzle. Establish base ellipse and axis, then neck and head masses. A head-and-neck height two to three times the base is only a starting proportion. Separate silhouette, planes and grain. Repair a detached horse head through its chest-to-base connection before the eye.

## Keep identifying silhouette changes

Turn forehead into a short muzzle with a blunt end and small mouth cue. Vary the two ears and preserve the jaw/neck gap. Check the silhouette without internal lines. Correct a dog- or seahorse-like result through muzzle length, forehead angle and neck curve rather than extra eye or mane detail. Stylisation still needs coherent proportions.

## Group carving planes by orientation

Group forehead, side face, lower jaw and broad neck planes under upper-left light using three related wood values. Avoid random checkerboard patches or spherical shading. Restore clear cuts to a plaster-like shape, or align shared edges in a paper-collage result. Broad planes establish orientation before small identifying cuts.

## Treat the mane as a carved strip

Add a narrow strip along the rear neck, suggesting carving with a few unequal cuts rather than hairs. Give the strip a small turning plane, not a serrated border. Reduce gear-like teeth. Hide it to verify the neck independently, then restore it as directional support rather than the sole identifying feature.

## Use small recesses for eye and nostril

Suggest eye and nostril with small recess shapes and nearby cut planes, not shiny biological eyes. Test a recess enlarged, then reduce it to normal size and restrain its darkness. Carved identity comes from removed planes; small cues should support the silhouette without turning the piece into a realistic animal toy.

## Align the base’s elliptical rings

Use broad foot, narrower waist and upper support with aligned elliptical axes and centres. Interrupt rear edges hidden by the body. Establish stepped planes before narrow shadows. Correct crooked stacked plates through axis and width, or a plain tube through meaningful tier differences rather than extra circular lines.

## Let grain follow the carved body

Limit grain to selected neck and base areas, following their different directions and turning surfaces. Keep it weaker than carved edges and facial recesses. Hide and simplify grain that fragments planes. Different wood colours are illustration choices, not automatic species recognition; the goal is material cues supporting the carving.

## Ground the piece and vary its edges

Use a narrow base contact and a softening lower-right shadow, allowing selected lit edges to approach the background. Review identity small, planes and tiers normally, accidental gaps close up. Reduce heavy darks or hard reflections for an iron-like result, and repair contact rather than darkening everything when it floats.

## Test the carving without grain

Compare grain-free and finished previews at equal size. Repair silhouette if identity depends on texture, or reduce grain if it erases planes. Cover the eye to test orientation through ears, muzzle and neck. Record the most useful outer edge, distinguishing structural information from decoration rather than restoring every omitted detail.

## Keep the design and change the viewing side

Keep silhouette, carved-plane and finished versions with an editable base. Next, turn the head more toward the viewer and rebuild muzzle width, ears, visible cheek and consistent base perspective. Flipping the flat image is not a new observation. Hold palette and light fixed and identify which planes narrow or gaps disappear.
