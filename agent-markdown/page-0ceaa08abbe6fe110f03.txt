## Set proportions with a bounding box

Draw a box encompassing the knob through the bottom and compare total height to greatest width. ## Connect head and body

Draw the head on either side of the center axis and connect it down to the body, observing the neck and shoulder transition. Avoid sticking a perfect round head onto the body: its lower edge may be compressed by a seam or perspective. The sides need not be mechanically mirrored, but should expand coherently around the same axis. Temporarily hide the background and compare empty space on both sides; if a contour bulges suddenly, check the reference before changing it.

## Draw the knob and visible seam

Place the small knob on top of the head with its base touching the mill rather than floating. Use a curved short line for a visible head-to-body seam, consistent with the same rotational perspective; do not complete a full dark ring through a hidden shadow. Avoid copying unreadable logos or numbers.

## Place the base on the table

Draw the lower edge from the photograph; a slightly elevated view may not reveal the entire underside. Let the lowest point touch the table line, then add a short darker contact shadow. Do not hide a floating base with a large shadow: hide the shadow first and check contact. If the product reference is floating on a plain background, explicitly treat the table and shadow as your own scene design rather than source facts.

## Model the wood with value

Block in a warm middle brown, choose upper-left light for the exercise, reserve a lighter vertical band on the lit side and darken the opposite side gradually. The outline is a varying rotational form, so the band can curve with head and body rather than crossing the seam unchanged. A grayish knob can distinguish metal from wood. At thumbnail size verify head, neck and base; if only a bright line remains, broaden the middle-value region.

## Add restrained wood grain

Observe visible grain in the reference and use a few light irregular lines or color shapes following the surface. If a thick finish or low-resolution photo hides grain, omit it. Avoid evenly spaced lines crossing the knob and background, and do not make each grain mark darker than the outer silhouette. Hide the grain layer to inspect the form; if it looks flat without grain, revise values before adding more marks.

## Compare a shared axis

Duplicate the sketch into two thumbnails: align knob, head and base around one center axis in the first, and deliberately shift the knob sideways in the second. Keep other contours unchanged and compare with the photo. If viewpoint or assembly makes the real knob visibly off-center, follow the evidence rather than forcing symmetry. Keep the guide axis in the sketch layer instead of leaving it as a solid line on the finished object.

## Check shadow direction and boundaries

With the chosen light, paint a table shadow toward the unlit side, clearer near contact and softer farther away. It need not reproduce every grain line but should connect to the base. If its direction conflicts with the lit side, reconcile the light source before adjusting opacity. Inspect knob, side contours and canvas corners to avoid cropping the button. At thumbnail size verify that the object separates from the background.

If the knob reveals an ellipse, check whether its long axis fits the body perspective; a circular top can suggest a higher viewpoint. When changing the view, consider any visible lower edge too instead of rotating the knob alone. If the photograph does not show the underside, record only the top observation and do not invent an unseen ellipse.
