A violin needs more than a waisted outline: strings, fingerboard and bridge must share an understandable direction. Draw a simplified resting violin without bow or player, tracing four strings from neck through bridge toward the tail. Proportions serve observation, not making, tuning or repair. Colour and layers separate silhouette from fine lines, while connections are checked manually.

## Give neck and body a shared axis

Use an approximately 1400×1900 px portrait canvas with a pale grey-green background. Establish an axis tilting slightly from lower left toward upper right. Form upper and lower body bouts narrowing at the waist, then attach neck and a simplified scroll. Avoid independently drawn symmetrical vase halves forced together. Separate sketch, wood body, fingerboard, bridge and strings. In one colour, check the neck emerges from the body top. If the head looks like another object beside it, reconnect axis and neck width rather than using strings to tie disconnected forms together. A slight turn may create visible asymmetry while retaining one shared instrument direction.

## Give the fingerboard a clear end above the body

Use dark grey-brown for the fingerboard, extending along the neck toward the body centre with a definite end rather than continuing to the tail. Establish a long narrow top and slight thickness, somewhat wider nearer the viewer and aligned with the axis. Keep wood visible on both sides beneath it. If it resembles a crack, weaken surrounding darkness and expose a lit top. If a floating ruler, clarify the neck junction rather than exaggerating shadow height. Hide strings to inspect the fingerboard, then restore them. Four strings pass over it without requiring equally bright lines in every dark region.

## Mark three sections of four string routes

Mark neck-top, bridge and tail attachment regions in the sketch and route each string through them without switching into a neighbouring line. Keep four routes closer over the fingerboard, spreading slightly near the bridge and returning toward their tail region. Count four low-contrast fine lines, excluding fingerboard edges from the count. Four temporary sketch colours can help, then become one material colour in the final. Correct unexplained crossings at their attachment points instead of hiding them with glints. Trace visible exterior relationships without internal fastening details or string-tension settings.

## Place the bridge beneath the strings

Draw a small thin warm-pale bridge between the fingerboard end and tail. Its feet contact the body and its gently arched top supports four passing strings. Establish foot contact first rather than suspending a crossbar above the strings. A little negative space suggests light construction without carving every opening. Hide strings to check the bridge rests on the body, then restore them and verify that all four pass over its upper edge rather than through its middle. Reduce an oversized bridge; if too invisible, improve local contrast rather than thickening it into a heavy block.

## Place sound holes on the body plane

Add two simplified f-shaped sound holes beside the bridge in low-contrast dark brown, echoing the body’s length. A turned far hole may look narrower instead of both being equally broad viewer-facing stickers. They are openings in the top, not raised letters with exterior cast shadows. Omit inner detail initially and check they stay within the waist and separate from bridge feet. If decorative letters, adjust their relationship to axis, waist and bridge. Reference establishes placement only; drawn hole sizes are not construction or tone specifications.

## Use wood colour and a thin rim for volume

Use warm ochre-brown with upper-left light and lower-right shade, exposing a narrow side rim rather than turning the whole violin into a thick block. Add only two or three low-contrast grain sections lengthwise on the nearer areas, keeping them out of sound holes. Simplify the scroll to a connected curve, not a floating spiral. If flat cut paper, add restrained side thickness and neck shadow. If metallic like a large kettle, reduce sharp white spots. Hide grain to check form and then assess material; clear construction contributes more than elaborate texture everywhere.

## Trace strings with separate layer checks

Show body and fingerboard, add bridge and finally the four strings, tracing each from top to tail. Each layer should explain attachment without damaging the underlying surface. Compare a copy with one string deliberately missing the bridge and observe its unsupported route, then verify all four in the original. Do not thicken them into parallel pipes for clarity; locally lighten them or reduce background competition. Judge the instrument at normal size and zoom only for intersections. This is neither automatic string counting nor tuning validation, and a still-life image cannot establish sound quality.

## Preserve bridge and four fine routes after export

Save the layered original and inspect string continuity over the dark fingerboard, bridge contact and separated hole edges in the PNG. If four lines cannot be read at small size, choose an appropriate display size and adjust locally rather than adding a fifth for density. Keep a grain-free structural preview. A new orientation requires rechecking bridge and hole foreshortening, not merely mirroring and ignoring connections. Record a specific route fault, such as a string bypassing the bridge’s upper edge on the left, instead of blaming brush fineness or insufficient texture.
