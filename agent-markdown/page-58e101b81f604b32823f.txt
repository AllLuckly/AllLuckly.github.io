A matrix’s inverse exponent does not mean taking each entry’s reciprocal. Use an original two-by-two card, compute determinant/candidate inverse, verify identity entry by entry and retain nonexistence for a singular control. Book Isle supports reading, returning and notes; linear algebra is recomputed externally without an assumed matrix calculator or proof checker.

## Copy rows, columns and the full identity

Card M1 has rows(2, 1),(1, 1), written `A=[[2,1],[1,1]]`. Seek the ordinary two-sided multiplicative inverse. Identity rows are(1, 0),(0, 1): off-diagonal zeros matter as much as diagonal ones. Import a clear PDF/EPUB and verify M1 plus row/column labels. For image equations inspect layout rather than assuming searchable entries. Restrict this to real two-by-two square matrices, without mixing generalized inverses into the acceptance condition.

## Compute the determinant before division

The determinant is upper-left times lower-right minus upper-right times lower-left:2×1−1×1=1. Nonzero permits division in the inverse formula. Pair the minus with the correct entries rather than adding to three. The determinant is a scalar; A remains a four-entry matrix. det A=1 does not make A the identity. Draw the products externally and link the result to its source paragraph instead of clipping an unexplained one.

## Exchange diagonal entries and negate the other two

Exchange diagonal 2/1, negate the two off-diagonal ones and divide the whole matrix by determinant one, giving candidate `R=[[1,-1],[-1,2]]`. The scalar applies to every entry, not only row one. Retain candidate status until verification. A is symmetric but not its own inverse, so substituting its transpose based on appearance fails. Keep original, determinant and candidate tied to the same unchanged A.

## Check all four entries by row times column

For AR compute 2×1+1×(−1)=1, 2×(−1)+1×2=0, 1×1+1×(−1)=0 and 1×(−1)+1×2=1 in their respective positions. Compute RA too: rows(1, 0),(0, 1). Verify both without assuming matrix multiplication commutes. Each entry is a row–column dot product, not entrywise multiplication. Checking only diagonal ones can miss off-diagonal errors; enlarge those zeros when reading thumbnails.

For Q=2R, both AQ/QA have diagonal twos and off-diagonal zeros, not I. Correct zeros alone are insufficient: diagonal entries must be one. Keep this scaling control separate rather than accepting proportional-to-identity as an ordinary inverse.

## Apply the inverse and substitute into the system

For Ax=b use column b=(5, 3). Rb=(5−3,−5+6)=(2, 1). Substitution gives 2×2+1=5 and 2+1=3. Keep column orientation instead of silently transposing b. The inverse recovers an input here; it is not required for every system. Retain the answer paragraph separately from your substitution check: finding an answer in Book Isle does not itself verify it.

## Retain no inverse for the singular control

For M2 let B have rows(2, 4),(1, 2). Its determinant 4−4=0 forbids ordinary division by zero. B maps nonzero vector(−2, 1) to(0, 0), so different inputs can share an output and no ordinary inverse uniquely recovers them. Do not invent a zero-matrix inverse or change four to three merely to fill a box. Nonexistence completes the question; keep it separate from M1’s R.

## Test entrywise reciprocals and transpose as counterexamples

Entrywise reciprocal candidate C has rows(0.5, 1),(1, 1); AC has rows(2, 3),(1.5, 2), not I. A’s transpose equals A, but A² has rows(5, 3),(3, 2), also not I. Keep these separate without changing both matrix and multiplication rule. Enlarge ambiguous inverse/T notation rather than guessing. Highlight the inference and failed product where supported, or keep full external notes with a page reference.

For diagonal D with rows(2, 0),(0, 4), the inverse has rows(0.5, 0),(0, 0.25) and multiplies to I. Its zero entries remain zero; entrywise reciprocals would require division by zero. Matrix invertibility does not require every entry nonzero, nor does one zero make a matrix singular. Check the whole matrix and determinant.

## Reopen and recompute from conditions to products

Reopen M1, hide the candidate, recompute determinant/R, verify AR/RA and solution(2, 1), then explain M2’s zero determinant and nonzero kernel vector. Distinguish matrix, determinant, transpose and inverse rather than memorizing four entries. Check version-linked bookmarks/notes and recalculate changed entries/vectors. A later three-by-three problem needs an appropriate method, not this four-entry swap/negate mnemonic applied to nine entries. Mark two-by-two and ordinary two-sided inverse in notes so the method’s scope remains visible in later chapters.
