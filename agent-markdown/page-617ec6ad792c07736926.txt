Check two-forward/two-back four-toed grips, both leg connections and tail occlusion without assuming touching tail feathers bear weight.

English translation of the [Chinese original](<https://allluckly.cn/guides/aquarell-parrot-zygodactyl-branch-tail/>), translated on 2026-11-08. Original publication dates, attribution, code and version notes are retained.

Rather than revisiting feather groups, show how parrot feet surround a branch and where the tail lies in depth. Each foot has four digits, two forward and two backward, forming a zygodactyl grip. Tail feathers approaching or touching wood do not automatically become a weight-bearing fifth limb. Draw a simplified parrot resting on a sloping branch and manually inspect leg roots, claw tips and branch occlusion in Aquarell. Palette and pose are illustration choices, not species identification or proof of locomotor mechanics.

## Establish a sloping branch and body silhouette

Use an approximately 1600×1900 px portrait canvas with a pale cream-grey background. Run a branch lower left to upper right through the lower half, head upper left and body above it. Sketch head, chest, belly and folded-wing silhouette, leaving room for a lower-right tail. Separate sketch, branch, far foot, near foot, body and tail; choose muted green with a warmer chest. Do not place the body entirely beside the branch and rescue it with horizontal stretched legs. Adjust body and feet if sack-like rather than making the tail explain every support; feet provide the main support reading here.

## Connect both legs to the lower body

Locate near and far leg roots below the belly. Feathers may hide upper portions, but each exposed lower leg must reach its own grip. Neither foot may grow from a wing tip, and a side view does not justify omitting the second leg from structural records. Label near/far temporarily and hide the body to check their connections. Restore concealed paths if two detached claws appear. Investigate tail lines, twigs and toes if four legs appear. Only a fragment of the far foot may show in the finish, but the sketch must identify it as this bird’s second leg.

## Give each foot two forward and two backward digits

Define forward and backward relative to the bird. Sketch two digits forward and two backward around opposite sides of the branch. Do not substitute a three-forward/one-back arrangement or line up all four claw tips on the branch front. Bend and overlap digits as needed, first tracing four paths from the foot and then giving each width. Investigate a joint highlight if five claw tips appear. If only three are visible, record the fourth concealed path rather than adding a black hook at an implausible position. Correct grouping matters more than exaggerated sharpness; small directional tips are enough.

## Alternate branch occlusion around the grip

Near digits cover the visible wood surface, while portions wrapping behind are concealed; tiny claw ends may reappear appropriately. Establish a complete branch, then interrupt it according to depth order instead of drawing one continuous line through every digit. Add restrained contact darkness in short useful segments. Check shared foot and leg if plastic-clip-like; add modest volume and wrapping direction if sticker-like. Keep the far foot quieter but subject to the same four-digit structure rather than arbitrary distant black dashes.

## Let the tail approach wood without becoming another foot

Extend the tail continuously from the rear body as a tapered long shape, using two or three quiet feather divisions rather than rigid fingers. It may pass behind and near the branch without a claw-like clasp. If showing touch, describe visible contact alone, not assumed weight bearing. Distinguish tail from feet and remove black terminal hooks if it reads as a third leg. Separate values and depth boundaries if tail and wood merge into one stick. Zygodactyl grip has its own anatomical basis; tail placement does not replace or change the two-foot count.

## Use colour to clarify rather than conceal structure

Use soft yellow-green upper-left feathers, grey-green shade, warm-grey feet and grey-brown wood, with enough separation to locate grips. Keep the wing broad with a few long lines rather than repeating feather-group studies. Adjust neighbouring wood if pale feet disappear instead of blackening every toe. Repair chest/belly turning if the green body flattens rather than adding dense feather marks. Use a simplified curved closed beak without grasping the branch and introducing another support relation. These colours support composition, not reliable species, age or mood identification.

## Review four-digit records and occlusion

Keep four centreline paths for each foot and verify two forward/two backward. Hide feather marks and background and check both leg-to-branch connections. If a foot is wholly occluded, mark its hidden region in the sketch without making the finish see-through or claiming all four digits are visibly exposed. Separately inspect continuous tail origin and branch depth order without toe joins. Adjust one foot’s endpoints or one branch contour at a time, holding pose and light fixed. Distinguish count, connection and occlusion faults instead of burying them in ornament.

## Retain useful grip information in the export

Save the layered original and inspect near-foot groups, far-foot cue and tail root in a PNG at use size. If toes become a black ring, reduce dark marks and restore palm width rather than whitening the whole branch. Repair tail outline and colour if it resembles a hooked claw. Record a fault such as the second rearward near toe merging with bark and use the four-path sketch to repair it. Sources support the basic digit arrangement, not a behavioural experiment for this pose. New climbing photographs require fresh checks of beak, feet and tail; touching surfaces are not automatically weight-bearing supports.
