Unreadable subtitles do not always need a larger font. White text on snow, long sentences wrapping onto three or four lines, and text pressed against the bottom edge involve contrast, line wrapping, and positioning respectively. iCouldPlay's subtitle settings support comparing one change at a time. This guide uses a video with correct subtitles already loaded to find a readable style for your own screen.

## First identify which type of subtitles you are adjusting

Choose a video with clear dialogue whose subtitle text and timing already match. Start with ordinary SRT subtitles to reduce the influence of styling built into effects-heavy formats. If subtitles are not loaded yet, first complete [Importing external subtitles](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/icouldplay-external-subtitles/>), rather than troubleshooting “no subtitles” and “subtitles too small” at the same time.

Open “Subtitle Settings” in app settings and start with the preview. Note the current size, color, and position, or take a screenshot containing only the settings so you can restore them. The preview helps compare styles quickly, but the final check must be in actual playback. Its simple background does not represent every movie scene.

One easily overlooked limit is that subtitles burned into the video image are already part of the picture. Changing player font and size cannot reflow them. Turn off the current subtitle track to check: if the text remains, identify its source before deciding whether to load an additional subtitle file.

## Choose the font size at your normal viewing distance

Keep the system default font initially and choose a moderately long line of dialogue. Put the phone or tablet where you normally watch and gradually adjust the size until you can read naturally. Do not set it while leaning close to the screen only to discover that it is still too small at your actual viewing distance. Different devices need not use the same value.

Next, find a longer sentence and see whether it often wraps onto three or more lines. Larger text fits fewer characters per line and is more likely to cover people or visual information. If short lines are clear but long lines are crowded, reduce the size slightly and improve contrast to keep them readable.

Pay extra attention to total height with bilingual subtitles. Two languages already occupy two lines; if either wraps again, the text block grows taller. First decide whether you really need both languages displayed together. Choosing a suitable subtitle file is often better than shrinking everything until it is hard to read.

## Improve contrast before adding decorative effects

White text with a dark outline is usually a useful baseline. After changing the text color and outline in settings, return to a bright scene, then a dark one, and check whether the same line remains clear. If text disappears only against snow or sky, insufficient contrast may be the main issue rather than font size.

An outline that is too thin can disappear into the background, while one that is too thick can fill the gaps between strokes, particularly in complex Chinese characters. Start with a clear but restrained edge, then compare the shadow. An excessive shadow offset can look like two sets of text; do not use a large offset as a substitute for a proper outline.

You can also compare a style with a background color. A background block helps text remain readable against complex images, but covers more of the picture. It can be useful for courses or interviews where understanding the content matters most. For scenic footage, choose the balance between readability and obstruction that feels comfortable.

## Keep subtitles away from edges and important content

Start with the usual bottom position and see whether enough margin remains after playback controls disappear. Move the vertical position a little at a time rather than jumping from the bottom edge to the center. Check the second and third lines of long sentences too; a suitable first line does not mean that the entire subtitle block is within a safe area.

Some videos already have demonstration controls, name captions, or on-screen text along the bottom. Try moving external subtitles to the top. After changing the position, review three different scenes to ensure that avoiding a lower-third name has not obscured a title above. Subtitle placement should suit the current content; no single layout fits every video.

Switch to landscape and check again. Landscape adds width but reduces available height, so the same subtitle settings may appear taller. If you use split view on a tablet or another window size, check in that actual viewing arrangement rather than relying only on the fixed-size settings preview.

## Why the preview changes but the text in the video does not

First confirm that the text on screen is the text subtitle you just selected, rather than subtitles burned into the image. Then compare a simple SRT file with an ASS file that carries its own fonts, positioning, or animation. The latter may also depend on file styling and player handling, so it is not a reliable basis for concluding that every subtitle setting is broken.

If only some characters are missing, try the system default font to check font coverage. If the entire passage is garbled, check the file encoding. Increasing font size or changing the text to yellow generally cannot fix incorrect encoding; handle that in the original subtitle file.

If subtitles look different during playback and in the settings preview, note the video and subtitle formats, then follow [Playback compatibility troubleshooting](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/icouldplay-player-engine/>)for comparison. If the interface does not show the playback engine's name, there is no need to guess which one is active. Keep the video, subtitles, and timestamp unchanged to identify the source of any difference.

## Finish by watching a short continuous section

Choose one or two minutes containing short and long dialogue, light and dark scenes, and close-ups. Watch the entire section in your usual posture. Notice whether you squint, whether subtitles keep obscuring the image, and whether you can naturally finish reading long lines before they disappear. This is closer to actual viewing than repeatedly enlarging a single paused frame.

Once satisfied, note your usual settings and open another video with ordinary subtitles. If the new video's text is too large or poorly positioned, adjust it for that content rather than insisting on one set of values. Save a record before resetting: the current settings page also resets subtitle timing offset, so recheck synchronization if you previously adjusted it for a particular source.
