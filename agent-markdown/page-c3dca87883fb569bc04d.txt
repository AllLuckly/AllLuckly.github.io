## Define a parent as the product of its two children

Place the root labelled 72 at the top. In an external record define every internal split as two positive integers greater than 1 whose product equals their parent. Connections signify factor decomposition, not addition, size comparisons or organisational rank. First test 6 split into 2 and 3, then return to 72; placing its digits 7 and 2 below it is not factorisation. Title this original integer practice rather than real data. ## Begin the first tree with eight and nine

Below 72 place 8 and 9, since 8 × 9 = 72. Split 8 into 2 and 4, 4 into 2 and 2, and 9 into 3 and 3. Connect every edge to its own parent. Equal 2s need separate text objects rather than one leaf shared by two parents. Leaves may end at different depths: the first 2 need not extend downward, and adding a child 1 merely to align bottoms is invalid. List all four internal splits externally before drawing, then verify that 72, 8, 4 and 9 each have exactly two children with the correct product.

## Count five leaves rather than two distinct values

Read terminal leaves from left to right: 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, giving five leaves. Do not deduplicate repeated primes as if cleaning duplicate files: retaining only 2 and 3 leaves product 6. The full product is 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 72, also written 2³ × 3². Exponents 3 and 2 count occurrences; this does not mean 2 × 3 + 3 × 2. Internal nodes 8, 9 and 4 must not be multiplied again. ## Check prime termination at every leaf

Both 2 and 3 have exactly two positive divisors, 1 and themselves, so those branches terminate. The number 1 is not prime and is excluded as a child under this exercise’s rule. Leaves 2, 2, 2, 9 still multiply to 72, but prime factorisation is unfinished. Check product preservation and prime leaves separately rather than merging them into one “looks right” judgment. These small values permit manual checking; a text label saying prime is no software primality test. Larger integers require another reliable method, and this drawing guarantees neither arbitrary-integer correctness nor computational performance.

## A six-and-twelve split must retain multiplicities

Keep the first tree and create a second: 72 → 6 × 12; 6 → 2 × 3; 12 → 3 × 4; 4 → 2 × 2. Its leaves may read 2, 3, 3, 2, 2, sorting to the same 2, 2, 2, 3, 3 and exponents.
