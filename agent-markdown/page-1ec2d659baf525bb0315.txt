One toast offers soft bread, solid butter and sticky jam. Complex texture is unnecessary: bread has scattered variation, butter clear planes and jam concentrated darks and highlights. Use a slightly angled overhead view to practise assigning different marks to different objects.

## Establish the composition with tilted toast

Create a 1400×1200 pixel canvas with pale warm gray. Make the toast about 800×750, slightly rotated with generous margins. Let the top bulge gently, sides narrow downwards and base stay unpointed. Sketch the face, then about 50 pixels of lower-right thickness so it is not paper-thin.

Prepare cream yellow, light brown, toasted brown, pale butter yellow, dark jam red and a little warm white. Separate toast, butter and jam into layers for overlap adjustments. Fix upper-left light. Omit plates and cutlery until the three materials read clearly, then decide on supporting objects.

## Begin the crumb with loose colour variation

Use an approximately 100-pixel brush for pale-yellow crumb and a light-brown crust about 40 to 70 pixels wide. Vary crust darkness, perhaps deeper at the top and lower right. Use darker brown-yellow for the lower-right thickness plane, making a clear but gentle turn from the face.

Paint two or three broad, irregular low-opacity light-brown variations in the crumb, with loose edges, before adding holes. Zoom out: centre bright, rim darker, side darkest. Wooden-looking bread often needs less uniform interior colour or less straight crust edges, not more tiny texture.

## Place pores only in selected areas

With an 8–18 pixel brush, add short ellipses and irregular crumb dots. Use paler central pores and a few darker near edges. Group three to five dots with clean gaps, not an even scatter. Vary sizes clearly, broadly following bread form without making every dot parallel.

Add two or three short crust cracks near its darkest colour, not one continuous black line. A few light edges on only the largest two or three pores are enough. For dot density, see [Pear stippling exercise](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/aquarell-stippling-pear/>), also using varied density and size to avoid mechanical repetition.

## Show butter thickness with clear planes

Place an approximately 220×160 pixel butter block upper-left on the toast, as a slightly irregular quadrilateral. Pale yellow top, darker yellow side and a short base shadow distinguish it from bread. Keep edges clearer than crumb but corners slightly rounded, avoiding a plastic-block look.

Add one or two faint parallel scrape marks on top. Keep the brightest warm-white patch below one-tenth of that face. A little darker yellow beneath suggests melting, but avoid a luminous ring around the entire block. Melt spreads flat and irregularly across the bread.

## Show jam thickness through value and highlights

Paint dark-red jam lower-right with an irregular outline and two or three rounded bulges. Use medium-dark red centrally, lighter thin edges and deeper thick areas, without black outlining. Cover some bread pores so jam sits on top rather than interpenetrating texture.

Use a small brush for two short warm-white highlights curving with the jam’s bulge. Make them unequal, not parallel, and do not dot every bump. If jam resembles red cloth, check scattered highlights or sharp edges. Concentrated bright and dark areas often help more than extra red shades.

## Add the table shadow last

On a layer below the toast, paint a light gray-brown shadow offset about 40 to 70 pixels down-right. Darken near the bread and soften outward. Butter and jam shadows stay on the toast, separate from the table shadow. A faint napkin edge is enough background; keep the food dominant.

Compare materials at completion: bread should feel loose, butter planar and jam glossy in concentrated areas. Check distinction at reduced size, then inspect excessive pore density. If too many colours appear, return to [Limited-palette exercise](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/aquarell-limited-palette/>)and remove intermediate colours with no clear role.

## Common questions and the next exercise

**Why does everything look like the same plastic?** A common cause is identical white highlights on bread, butter and jam. Bread relies on scattered values and pores, butter on broad restrained light and jam on small concentrated highlights. Remove material-inappropriate white spots before adding colour.

**Must every air hole be individually readable?** No. A few pores convey bread at final size. Dense dots can overpower butter and jam. Next time retain the toast structure and replace jam with honey, comparing highlight shapes and transparent edges rather than copying every texture.

Compare three edges: slightly rough bread crust, neat butter cuts and rounded irregular jam. Even without colour, contours should differ. If all are smooth, add a few crust gaps while retaining distinct butter and jam rhythms; do not texture everything evenly.
