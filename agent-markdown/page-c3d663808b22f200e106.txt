## Check unit length as well as column orthogonality

Columns q1=(3/5,4/5) and q2=(−4/5,3/5) each have squared norm 9/25+16/25=1 and mutual dot −12/25+12/25=0. They are unit and perpendicular, not just perpendicular. These conditions make this real square matrix orthogonal. Multiplying every entry by two preserves mutual zero dot but makes each column length two, losing normalization. Record two self-dots and one cross-dot, not just a right-angle icon.

Deliberately losing Q’s minus sign gives R with rows (3/5,4/5),(4/5,3/5). Both columns still have unit length but cross-dot 24/25, not orthogonal. R also sends (5,0) to (3,4), preserving this sample’s length. For (1,1), however, output (7/5,7/5) has squared norm 98/25 rather than two. The contrasting samples and column test explain why one direction is insufficient.

## Check all four entries after transposing

Qᵀ has rows (3/5,4/5) and (−4/5,3/5). Computing each entry of the matrix product gives QᵀQ=I, with diagonal ones and off-diagonal zeros; QQᵀ also gives I, proving Q⁻¹=Qᵀ. One upper-left one is insufficient: verify all four entries. This shortcut requires the current square orthonormal-column matrix; it is not a universal inverse rule for transposes. Even orthonormal columns in a rectangular matrix do not supply this same two-sided square inverse.

```text
Q = [[3/5,-4/5],[4/5,3/5]]
transpose(Q) = [[3/5,4/5],[-4/5,3/5]]
transpose(Q)*Q = Q*transpose(Q) = [[1,0],[0,1]]
Q*[5,0]^T = [3,4]^T; transpose(Q)*[3,4]^T = [5,0]^T
reflection H=diag(1,-1): transpose(H)*H=I; det(H)=-1
scaling D=diag(2,1/2): det(D)=1; transpose(D)*D=diag(4,1/4)
```

## Transform a length-five input forward and back

For p=(5,0), Qp=(3,4); both lengths are five. Multiplying (3,4) by Qᵀ gives first coordinate 9/5+16/5=5 and second −12/5+12/5=0, recovering p. Inverse rotation is not the reciprocal of each entry. Another p=(1,2) gives (−1,2), both squared lengths five. A negative coordinate is not negative length. Zero maps to zero and legitimately preserves length, without exclusion from the transformation.

Applying Q again does not undo the first rotation. Q² has rows (−7/25,−24/25),(24/25,−7/25), sending original (5,0) to (−7/5,24/5), still length five but not the original position. Undo with Qᵀ, not Q itself unless a special matrix is separately proved self-inverse. Compare two forward rotations with forward-then-inverse, checking input and operation order rather than final length alone.

## Prove length preservation for every input

For any real column p, squared output length is (Qp)ᵀ(Qp)=pᵀQᵀQp=pᵀp, a general proof rather than extrapolation from two points. Determinant 9/25+16/25=1 makes this two-dimensional orthogonal transformation orientation-preserving. It has cosθ=3/5 and sinθ=4/5, counterclockwise about 53.130° in the stated coordinates. A screen with downward y may look opposite; do not change the matrix sign to fit a display. Keep mathematical and interface axes separate.

Similarly, transformed dot product (Qp)ᵀ(Qr)=pᵀr. With both inputs nonzero, preserved norms also preserve their angle. If either is zero, the dot identity remains valid without defining a measurable angle. Separate the general algebra from the nonzero-length requirement for an angle; invertibility does not give a direction to every zero vector.

## Orthogonal reflection and nonorthogonal determinant-one scaling

H=diag(1,−1) has HᵀH=I and preserves lengths, but determinant −1 reflects across the horizontal axis rather than the current pure rotation. D=diag(2,1/2) has determinant one yet DᵀD=diag(4,1/4)≠I, doubling the length of (1,0). It even sends (1,2) to (2,1), accidentally preserving this sample’s length without preserving every length. Keep orthonormality, orientation and one accidental equal-length sample as distinct evidence; one determinant value does not replace the product test.
