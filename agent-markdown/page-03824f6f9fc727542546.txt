Build a single grass–rabbit–fox chain with explicitly labeled energy-transfer arrows and limits.

## Choose one illustrative relationship

National Geographic Education gives grass eaten by a rabbit and a rabbit eaten by a fox as an example. The National Park Service describes food chains as energy transfer between organisms. Draw only this possible chain; do not claim every rabbit eats only grass or every fox eats only rabbits, or call one chain a complete food web. Label the arrows “example: energy-transfer direction,” not predator pursuit.

## Plan a horizontal canvas

Create an approximately 1800 by 900 landscape canvas in VectorPaint, separating background, grass, rabbit, fox, arrows and text with controls visible in the installed version. Save an editable original first. Reserve three similar picture areas at left, center and right, with gaps for two arrows and labels below. The three organisms need not be drawn to their real relative sizes. Use three light temporary placeholder rectangles to check margins and arrow gaps, then hide them once the icons are drawn and ensure labels remain on canvas.

## Draw the grass silhouette

In the left region use several pointed closed paths or shapes for a clump of grass with leaves emerging near the same ground. Choose greens of different values if useful, keeping tips clear of the frame. Start with three leaves of varied size before adding a few more. Vary their heights while keeping their bases near the same patch of ground so they do not appear to float. If the image becomes a green block when shrunk, reduce the leaf count and preserve gaps.

## Draw a recognizable rabbit

Build a simplified side-view rabbit in the center from a body oval, smaller head and two long ears. Use shape or path controls visible in the installed version, without assuming an unverified automatic shape feature. Add a short tail and visible feet, checking that ears join the head and feet sit below the body. If an ear merges with the background, adjust the background value first. If both ears overlap indistinguishably, change one angle slightly while keeping it connected, rather than adding unrelated outlines. The icon is a generalization, not a claim about a particular breed.

## Draw a recognizable fox

In the right region suggest a fox with a long body, pointed ears and bushy tail emerging from the rear rather than floating beside it. Warm brown and a lighter tail tip can add contrast, but this fox is only an example and does not identify a location or population. Ensure its silhouette differs from the rabbit and keep its tail tip clear of the right canvas edge.

## Point from grass to rabbit

Draw the first arrow between grass and rabbit, with its tail near the grass and its tip toward the rabbit, leaving both icons unobscured. Label it “Rabbit eats grass; energy passes to rabbit,” placing the food at the tail and the eater at the tip. Shrink the image to check that the point is recognizable; do not communicate direction by gradient or color alone.

## Point from rabbit to fox

Draw the second arrow consistently from rabbit to fox, labeled “Fox eats rabbit; energy passes to fox.” Confirm that it does not point back at the rabbit, then align its stroke weight and visual height with the first arrow. The arrows show a food-energy pathway, not specific predation frequency, percentages of transferred energy or the animals’ movement paths.

## Add names and roles

Label the icons “Grass: producer,” “Rabbit: consumer,” and “Fox: consumer.” Plants use sunlight in photosynthesis to make their own food; rabbits and foxes obtain energy by eating. Foxes are omnivores and the card shows only the possible rabbit relationship. Keep text large enough and separated from icons so the role explanations remain legible.

## State the simplification

Add a note below: many interconnected food chains form a more complex food web, and this card illustrates only one pathway. Do not add an energy arrow directly from fox back to grass to represent decomposition, which would confuse energy flow with nutrient cycling. If decomposers are needed, create a separate extension with sources and an explicit arrow key.

## Check arrows and reading order

Read “rabbit eats grass; fox eats rabbit” from left to right while tracing both arrows. If a tip points to the organism eaten, rotate or redraw the arrow on its own layer instead of merely changing text. Write “food → eater” on paper and match each tail to its icon before following the tip to the next one; use the same rule after changing labels to English. At phone width inspect grass gaps, rabbit ears, fox tail, arrowheads and explanation. Adjust placement and size before adding decoration.

## Export and inspect the copy

Save the editable original and export a separate PNG using a control visible in the installed version. Reopen it to inspect icons, arrows, labels and margins for cropping. If arrows blur, increase stroke width or output size and export again, then recheck directions. For an English duplicate, edit text separately and allow longer lines. After export check that labels do not cover arrows and that translation has not changed the grass–rabbit–fox order. Keep sources and revision date. Before adding species, verify relationships for the particular place and organisms rather than presenting the example as field research.
