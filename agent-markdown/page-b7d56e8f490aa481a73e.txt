Paint a plains prickly pear in bloom: first establish how its flat pads connect, then add spines and a yellow flower. The U.S. National Park Service describes round or oval flattened pads with spines and small bristles, and yellow flowers. Sources support the structure; pad count, lighting and composition are original choices, not proof of species identification or growth stage.

## Organize references and draft

Read the source and observe photographs, noting pad edges, connections and the flower's position. Do not touch plants to draw them: spines and small bristles may injure skin. Create an approximately 1600 × 1500 canvas and save an editable draft. Separate sketch, pads, flower, spines and background into layers. Canvas dimensions organize brushwork rather than specify actual plant size. Keep references and your draft separately to avoid overwriting either.

## Plan three pads first

Sketch a slightly tilted oval low in the composition for the base. Add one upper pad leaning left and another leaning right. Each upper pad needs a clear overlap at the base's upper edge rather than appearing as an isolated oval. Mark visible edges lightly and stop the rear edge at the pad in front. Shrink the sketch: can you understand the three connections without spines or flower? If they resemble floating balloons, adjust the width of the joins and the overlap first.

## Distinguish thickness and direction

Block in the base in a darker green, the upper-left pad in middle green and the upper-right pad a little lighter. Paint large areas before detail. A darker edge can suggest thickness, but avoid a uniformly heavy black outline. Let the leaning rear pad disappear where the front pad covers it, while keeping the right pad's attachment clear. The upper-left light source is only an exercise choice; color does not establish water stress. If two pads merge visually, separate adjacent values before adding spines.

## Place areoles and spines separately

Sparsely mark small points on each pad to represent spine-bearing sites. Check that points belong to their own surface instead of continuing across occlusion boundaries. Draw fine short spines from some points: those facing the viewer can appear shorter, while edge spines may extend against the background. Visible direction and length can vary; no fixed count is required. The source also mentions fine bristles. There is no need to draw each one, and omission does not mean they are absent. If the spines resemble a thick fence, reduce their number and contrast and preserve the green form.

## Attach a yellow flower to an upper edge

Choose a position near the top of the right pad. Draw a small center and several outward petals around it. Vary value slightly and let front petals overlap those behind; the flower base must meet the pad edge rather than float. Warm dots can suggest the center, but the illustration cannot establish pollination. Yellow flowers are described by the source; petal count, pose and light are drawing choices. If the flower hides the whole join, shrink or reposition it.

## Arrange background and ground

Use the background layer for pale sandy ground and two or three low-contrast stones. Avoid lining every stone up with a pad contour, which makes each pad look separately supported. A short contact shadow can join the base to the ground. Simplify distant plants with lighter color instead of copying an entire reference photograph. Hide the background to inspect the silhouette, then restore it and ensure flower and spines remain visible. The invented dry ground is not a report of a specific location.

## Check depth at thumbnail size

Shrink the image and hide spines, flower and sketch in turn. Are the three pads and joins still readable? Restore the flower and check that it meets the upper edge; then restore spines and check for marks crossing occlusion boundaries. If a spine was painted on the background, back up before redrawing or correcting it using the available editing controls. An exported picture alone does not prove the source remains editable. Magnify one areole and see whether its spines radiate from a small area rather than passing randomly through petals.

## Compare spine locations and occlusion

Beside the sketch draw a simplified pad with five marks on its front surface, adding short spines from only three. Draw a second pad whose rear spines stop at the edge of a pad in front. Compare: front spines share a small origin, while rear spines cannot inexplicably pass through the front pad. Keep this control on the sketch layer and hide it before final export. In the finished picture inspect occlusion at flower petals and pads. If a spine coincides with a petal edge, move its areole or shorten the mark instead of breaking the flower's connection. This checks the illustration, not an actual plant's spine count.

## Export and record

Save the original, export a separate image using the share or export control visible in your installed version, then open it elsewhere to inspect petals, fine spines and frame edges. Reopen the draft and try hiding spines and background separately; if the flower disappears too, check the active layer and file. Record completed operations, observed problems and pending steps. Deliver the original illustration and reference link. No physical-device drawing, field identification or collection is claimed.
