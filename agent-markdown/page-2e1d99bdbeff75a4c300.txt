When reading probability, memorising two definitions can leave mutual exclusion and independence confused. Use Book Isle to locate the text and retain bookmarks and notes, then list four outcomes on paper or in your own notes and check them individually. The example assumes two fair, mutually independent coin tosses; it neither requires real tossing nor claims that four trials must produce one of each outcome. Explain why one event pair is independent and another mutually exclusive, rather than expecting the app to solve probabilities.

## List four ordered basic outcomes

Write HH, HT, TH and TT in your own record, using H for heads and T for tails. The first letter represents toss one and the second toss two. A two-by-two layout can use rows for the first toss and columns for the second. Do not merge HT with TH: their order differs. Each pair is one complete two-toss outcome, not two separate list entries. Under the fair-and-independent assumptions, each cell has probability 1/4 and the four sum to 1. Check cell count and total first. A list of two heads, one of each and two tails must expand its middle category into two ordered outcomes rather than assigning 1/3 to each category.

## Express events as sets of cells first

Let A mean H on the first toss, containing HH and HT, and B mean H on the second, containing HH and TH. Record each event’s full meaning in Chinese or English before its symbol rather than leaving A and B to be guessed later. Mark their cells in two ways. Only HH is shared, so P(A)=1/2, P(B)=1/2 and P(A∩B)=1/4. The intersection means one complete two-toss outcome satisfies both conditions, not that another experiment is performed. If marked cells and formulas disagree, inspect letter positions against the event definitions rather than directly changing probability values to fit an answer. Explain why every listed outcome belongs to its event.

## Check independence by product and conditioning

Compute P(A)P(B)=(1/2)×(1/2)=1/4 and compare it with the intersection, confirming independence here. Then restrict attention to B’s two cells, HH and TH. A occurs only in HH, so P(A|B)=1/2, the same as P(A). The vertical bar means given that B occurred, not division of the letters A and B. The two checks should agree, but conditional probability requires a nonzero denominator. Do not carry the result over if the book changes events or assumptions. Independent tosses do not make every pair of events built from them independent; examine a different pair next.

## Repeat the checks for mutually exclusive events

Keep A as H on the first toss and define C as T on the first, containing TH and TT. A and C share no cell, so their intersection probability is 0 and they are mutually exclusive. But P(A)P(C)=1/4 rather than 0, so they are not independent. Given C, A is impossible: P(A|C)=0 instead of its original 1/2. Record two separate questions: can both occur, and does knowing one occurred change the other’s probability? Do not write the false slogan mutually exclusive therefore independent. Both events here have positive probability; retain that condition when generalising instead of mixing zero-probability cases into the same statement.
