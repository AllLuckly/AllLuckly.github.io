Use one trumpet daffodil as a reference for tepal arrangement, cup perspective and the flower-to-stem connection.

## Choose an identifiable reference

The Royal Horticultural Society describes daffodils with six petal-like tepals surrounding a trumpet-like corona; cultivated varieties differ in shape. Choose a permitted photo of one single trumpet daffodil where the six tepals and central cup can mostly be seen, and record cultivar and source. Do not force double-flowered varieties into the same construction. Draw visible exterior features only and do not infer hidden floral parts.

## Plan the canvas and layers

Create an approximately 1600 by 1800 portrait canvas in the current Aquarell interface and save an editable original; dimensions are a suggestion. Place the flower in the upper half, leaving room for stem and one or two leaves. Separate sketch, flower, stem and leaves, and background using available layers; actual labels may differ on your device. Lightly mark the flower axis and use a slightly oblique reference showing both cup opening and part of the corona wall.

## Establish the flower direction

Use a tilted ellipse to suggest the outer extent of the six tepals instead of immediately making each an identical teardrop. Its short axis reflects the chosen view, not a required physical angle calculation. Mark the flower center and outward direction of the corona; they should form one flower head rather than two disconnected shapes. At thumbnail size check the head against the stem, and move the composition before adding details if it crowds the canvas edge.

## Arrange the six tepals

Mark visible tips and bases of six tepals against the reference. Block in the near-side shapes first, then arrange far-side shapes by overlap; where the corona hides a tepal, draw only its visible part. Far tepals may appear shorter in an oblique view, so do not lengthen every one equally merely to show six. Temporarily number them to check the count, then hide the labels and ensure adjacent edges remain distinct and the central corona is not counted as a seventh tepal.

## Draw the corona opening

Place a tilted ellipse in front of the flower center for the visible opening, oriented consistently with the flower axis. Connect both sides back toward the center to make the trumpet wall seen in the reference; the rim may ripple, but establish one continuous large shape first. Observe how the near rim hides part of the interior, then distinguish the far rim and interior depth with a darker value. If the cup looks like a disc stuck to the flower, check whether opening and base coincide and whether the wall has enough length.

## Connect flower and stem

Locate the connection behind the flower in the chosen photograph and extend a slender directional stem downward. The stem may curve: first compare its angle with the flower axis, then give it width with two contours. Do not run it through the central opening. If tepals hide the junction, stop at the visible edges rather than inventing interior structure. Use a small value contrast to separate stem from flower rather than outlining every junction in black.

## Paint slender leaves

The RHS notes daffodils often have slender leaves. Select one or two visible leaves from the photograph without inventing a complete root system. Use converging curves to suggest each long leaf and observe tip direction and overlap with the stem. Decide which leaf is in front and stop the rear contour at the occlusion. If leaves look like uniformly thick green lines, vary a side curve or width while staying faithful to the reference.

## Separate light and shadow

Block tepals in pale yellow or warm white, choose one light direction and use a slightly darker value to separate neighboring planes. The corona interior can use a deeper yellow or muted orange to suggest space; it need not be uniformly black at its deepest point. Let cast shadows clarify overlap instead of giving all six tepals identical values. At thumbnail size, if the flower merges with a pale background, adjust background or local edge contrast rather than adding one heavy black outline.

## Compare opening and flower axis

Duplicate the sketch into two thumbnails: align the cup ellipse with the flower axis in one, and deliberately turn it far away in the other. Keep tepals, stem and background unchanged. Compare both with the reference and record the near rim, far rim and axis relationship. If the actual flower bends or its rim is irregular, follow the photo rather than treating the construction ellipse as a perfect rigid circle. Keep the chosen sketch and label the other as an exercise copy.

## Check overlaps and canvas edges

Zoom in on the corona-to-tepal junctions, stem connection behind the head, leaf overlaps and all four canvas corners. If shapes appear to pass through one another, return to the sketch and determine front-to-back order; extra fine lines do not replace that decision. Hide the background to check the silhouette, then restore it to inspect yellow against surrounding colors. Mark any visible structure you do not yet understand for later observation rather than inventing hidden parts from one photo.

## Save and review

Save the editable original and use a share or export option actually shown in the current version to save an image separately. Reopen it to inspect the cup, corona rim, stem and crop; return to the original and identify the sketch and flower layers. If a tepal is missing, check layer visibility and crop first. If you opened a flattened image, do not mistake missing layers for a damaged original. Keep the reference and permission note, and mark device or export steps not actually performed as pending.
