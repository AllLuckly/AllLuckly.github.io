Understanding an example’s answer does not necessarily mean being able to explain every substitution. Use Book Isle to separate one page into symbol meanings, givens, conversions and calculation steps, working on your own paper or calculator. The rectangle data are invented rather than taken from a real textbook problem. No formula recognition, automatic solving or unit checking is assumed. Notes retain sources and understanding; calculations still need independent verification.

## Choose an example with complete conditions

Choose a short example from an authorised textbook or handout, keeping statement, diagram and solution rather than only the final line. Record title, edition, chapter and example number, adding file page for PDF, and bookmark its start. Read fully and identify the requested quantity. Retrieve any statement on a preceding page before proceeding instead of guessing missing givens backward from the answer. Start with a small example rather than a multi-chapter proof so substitutions can be explained individually, then expand. Complete context matters more than problem count.

## Separate original symbol definitions from your explanation

Log each symbol’s original meaning, unit and defining location before calculation. Our fictional rectangle uses L for length, W for width and A for area, with case preserved. Follow a book’s different notation and label your mapping instead of silently replacing it with familiar letters. A letter may mean something else in another chapter, so scope definitions to this example. Keep short excerpts separate from your plain-language interpretation. Added assumptions must not be copied as if the textbook supplied them.

## Record units with the given values

For the fictional problem, take length 3 m and width 40 cm and seek area. Retain values paired with units and note whether each comes from statement or caption; do not list bare 3 and 40. Check statement and legend for a missing unit, marking unresolved if absent rather than assuming centimetres. Verify blurred figures in the original instead of substituting plausible values. A file opening successfully does not establish correct formula-image recognition, especially on scans where symbols and superscripts must be read manually.

## Unify length units before substitution

Convert 40 cm to 0.4 m, then calculate 3×0.4=1.2 with result unit m². Add a short explanation beside each line, such as length conversion or substitution into the area relation. Do not write 3×40=120 m²: that treats unlike length units as identical. Explicitly label this expression wrong rather than mix it among correct steps. A calculator’s 1.2 still needs unit and requested quantity; a bare number does not distinguish length, area or something else. Establish meaning before dealing with decimals.

## Cross-check through another unit route

Alternatively, convert 3 m to 300 cm, giving 300×40=12000 cm², then convert to 1.2 m². One metre corresponds to one hundred centimetres in length, but the area conversion squares that factor: 1 m² corresponds to 10000 cm². Dividing area by only 100 is wrong. Both routes use the same fictional givens and should agree in one final unit. Inspect a line that changed numbers without units before recopying the textbook answer. Cross-checking arithmetic does not prove the original conditions true; verify the object and premises in the statement.

## Keep the same quantity across each equality

Inspect every equality: both sides should describe the same quantity, with explanations beside it rather than conversion factors, question numbers or labels accidentally chained into an equation. Label any reconstructed omitted step as your own process, not wording the author supplied. Check definitions before substituting a renamed or similar-looking variable. Verify minus signs, fraction bars, subscripts, superscripts and decimal points against the original, as screenshots and extracted text may distort them. Pause an unreadable step rather than carry uncertainty into further calculation.

## Log the formula’s conditions too

Our example uses the area relation for a rectangle, not the same length-times-width rule for every curved shape. Log any real problem’s sign, angle or ignored-factor conditions before its formula. These are not decorative small print: without them, identical arithmetic may have a different meaning. Explain why the relation applies before calculating instead of trusting an attractive result. Mark uncertain conditions as questions with source anchors. A Book Isle highlight is not an automatic proof; formula and premises must be read together.

## Return to example number and page when search fails

Full-text search may not match a formula’s superscripts, Greek letters or scanned images. Try example number, heading or nearby words, open the context and use the bookmark for the original page. No A result does not prove there is no area formula, and a matching letter does not prove the correct example. Combine chapter, number and distinctive text in notes so changed reflow pages remain traceable. Copy a little through a visible selection control only if available, checking every symbol; otherwise record manually without assuming a formula editor.

## Separate source solution, attempt and error notes

Use plain note sections for source-solution location, your calculation, error cause and next action; no complex new database fields are required. Retain explicitly labelled mistakes, such as using a length factor for an area conversion, instead of deleting everything except the answer. Date later corrections and preserve enough process to explain them. Check symbols and units after exporting important notes. Back up book files and reading data separately and verify example numbers after edition changes; old anchors are not guaranteed to map automatically to newly arranged examples.

## Solve once with the source solution covered

Cover the source solution and independently work both fictional unit routes from the statement and symbol log, then compare. Explaining conversions and requested units matters more than remembering 1.2. Change one given for a small clearly invented variant while retaining other conditions and note affected steps; do not merge it into the textbook’s original problem. NIST supports area-unit usage, while actual derivations require the specific textbook. The workflow organises reading rather than automatically grades: each step needs a source, premises and a reproducible result.
