People, boxes or dots in a comic can encode data or be decoration. Even when quantitative, they require a legend. Read a small infographic in ManYue and manually match icons to stated values. All numerical examples are invented; no chart recognition, automatic counting or area tool is assumed. The result audits this material’s representation.

## Establish that the image encodes quantity

Choose an authorised previously read page and record work, edition, chapter, title, panel and referencing wording. Find a legend, units or explicit values before treating decorative figures as population data. Inspect outside notes, not an icon-only crop. Begin with two or three short rows rather than a complex collage. Verify unreadable wording against the identical source or a clear authorised copy instead of guessing a familiar value from icon count. Attractive presentation does not establish complete quantitative conventions.

## Write the unit represented by each icon

In an invented legend, one box icon represents two items, so three whole icons mean six, not three. Record object, unit, amount per icon and applicable rows rather than an isolated two. Preserve actual conventions such as one thousand people or a percentage instead of importing this example. Register two different shapes separately, without assuming equal values. Locate title or footnote units before counting. An undefined unit means conversion is unavailable, not permission to assume one item per icon.

## Count whole and partial forms row by row

For each row, record label, whole count, partial count and accompanying printed value before totals. Mark overlapped or balloon-hidden icons uncertain, not absent. A half icon means one item under this example only if the legend authorises fractional interpretation; cropping or decoration are alternatives. Not every visible semicircle is half a unit. After enlarging boundaries, recount the whole figure within the same row limits to avoid cross-panel or cross-row duplication.

## Separate icon number from icon size

Check whether rows use equal-sized symbols and whether number, area or another property is the encoding. An invented square with doubled side has four times the area, but does not establish four times the quantity without area encoding. Enlargement may be emphasis only. A person icon’s height cannot directly determine population, and changed proportions add ambiguity. Separate explicit convention, observed size difference and unknown encoding, distinguishing fit-to-screen changes from relative size within the original.

## Compare converted counts with printed numbers

Convert each row from the stated legend and compare printed numbers, checking missed icons, partials, units and rounding before alleging error. Do not change the legend to fit the number. Preserve approximate status where the picture shows only rough proportion; hand-drawn edges do not yield precise decimals. Before totals, check non-overlapping categories and a shared time window. Overlapping groups cannot simply become a population total. Verify supported conversions without pretending to have recalculated unavailable underlying research.

## Check comparison language in the prose

Read the associated more, twice or growth claim and verify object, baseline and legend. Three versus six icons at two items each under identical scope means six versus twelve and supports a twofold quantity. Different units per icon invalidate count-only comparison. Separate total from increase, and static groups from temporal growth. Preserve conditions in notes instead of converting visual density into a ratio. Any external-data check needs separate source/access records; this pass remains within supplied information.

## Leave, recount and preserve the checking chain

Save supported references or an external log for image, legend and claim, with row labels, counts, units and unknowns. Leave the reader, return and hide your result before recounting to avoid counting from memory. Add landmarks on tall images and disclose resized screenshots rather than treating them as original dimensions. Recheck legend and symbols after edition changes without automatic count/coordinate assumptions. Share necessary lawful quotations or a clearly original diagram, not an invented image mistaken for the source’s data.

## Finish with a legend-first numerical conclusion

Finish by explaining units per icon, whole/partial counting, size encoding and supported prose comparisons. A large image may emphasise, a half image may be clipped or unauthorised as fractional, and three icons may represent another unit. Missing definitions prevent conversion rather than justify convenient numbers. Retain one recoverable numerical relation and limitation. The goal is accurate interpretation, not an app-issued correctness stamp for every chart.
