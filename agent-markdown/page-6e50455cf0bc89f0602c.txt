Use an original two-position Earth diagram and a fourteen-hour daylight exercise without attributing seasons simply to solar distance.

English translation of the [Chinese original](<https://allluckly.cn/guides/manyue-seasons-axis-tilt-daylight-hemispheres/>), translated on 2026-11-16. Original publication dates, attribution, code and version notes are retained.

An Earth drawn left or right of the Sun does not by itself identify which hemisphere has summer. Read the North Pole, axis and incoming light before interpreting illumination and day length. Turn two original Earth diagrams into a PDF for ManYue, separating geometry, paper time calculations and image evidence. This is neither weather forecasting, an observation record nor automatic seasonal simulation by the app.

## Mark north and incoming light at both positions

Draw the Sun with Earth at opposite positions A/B externally. Mark N, S, equator and sunlight direction on each. Label it not to scale: circle sizes and gaps serve layout and cannot yield astronomical units. Preserve position markers and export two readable PDF pages. Delay season labels and first describe which end is north and where light comes from. Without N, screen top is not automatically north; rotating the drawing does not change the season.

## Keep the two axes approximately parallel

Use an approximate 23.5-degree tilt relative to the normal to the orbital plane. At A/B keep the axis oriented nearly the same way in space rather than redrawing north toward the Sun each time. The reference is the plane’s normal, not 23.5 degrees from the plane itself. Draw that normal separately before marking the angle. This annual schematic ignores longer-term axial changes. Label rotation/orbit arrows separately: one arrow cannot simultaneously mean axis orientation and a travel path.

## Compare illuminated areas for the same beam

Add equal-width beams meeting more direct and more oblique local surfaces. Show the same beam spreading over greater area when oblique. Hold beam energy and duration fixed; longer drawn arrows do not mean more energy. Actual incidence varies with latitude, time and season. This is geometry, not a temperature estimate from colored patches. The hemisphere tilted toward the Sun receives more direct illumination overall, not perpendicular light at every point; do not collapse equatorial, polar and other latitudes into one temperature claim.

For a geometry-only ledger let the same beam carry 120 J over one interval. Spread over 2 area units gives 60 J per unit; spread over 4 gives 30. Declare area/time units rather than calling 60/30 temperatures or total energies. Real temperature has additional influences. The exercise explains more direct illumination without measuring the solar constant.

## Convert a 210-degree daylight arc to fourteen hours

Use a separate exercise latitude circle with declared daylight arc 210 degrees and night arc 150 degrees, totalling 360. Assuming uniform rotation over 24 hours gives 15 degrees per hour, hence 14 hours of day and 10 of night. These are invented conditions, not a measured location without latitude/date. Use the ratio rather than 210/24 for hours. A displayed semicircle does not automatically give 12 hours without checking the latitude circle’s illuminated fraction. A 180-degree daylight premise gives 12 hours, not 14 with changed labels.

## Do not copy both hemispheres’ seasons in the same direction

When A’s northern end tilts toward the Sun, label northern summer and southern winter; at opposite B the relationship reverses. Both hemispheres share the same solar distance while having opposite seasons, showing why distance alone is insufficient. Left placement is not a universal northern-summer rule. Day-length comparisons also require a named latitude circle and hemisphere: do not assign the exercise’s 14 hours to every location or infer cloud/rain conditions from illuminated area.

## Use perihelion as a check on the distance explanation

Earth’s orbit is not perfectly circular and distance does vary, but perihelion occurs near northern winter and aphelion near northern summer. Retain the approximate seasonal/month relationship without inventing this year’s exact instant or kilometres. If an ellipse is exaggerated for visibility, say so rather than suggesting distance is the main seasonal cause. Nor does axial tilt imply identical half-year illumination everywhere near a pole; latitude/date matter. This mechanism diagram does not describe every regional climate.

## Trace one chain of evidence while reading

Open the PDF in ManYue and cover conclusions, using N, light and axis orientation to identify A/B before checking 210+150 and 14/10 hours. Use an existing bookmark or external page number for the confusing tilt marker, then return by the same identity. Enlarge obscured arrows or revise the external drawing rather than guessing hemispheres from color. The app supports reading/returning; your correct interpretation is not automatic axis detection, hemisphere recognition or annual astronomical validation.

Use a wrong control with north pointing toward the Sun at both A/B. Identify the violated approximately parallel annual-axis condition before correcting an axis; changing one summer label to winter alone does not repair geometry. Retrace light on the corrected pair and retain before/after pages so the error’s location remains evident.

## Reopen and state conditions and unknowns together

Reopen either page and identify north, orbital normal, tilt and illuminated side, then recalculate daylight under the uniform 24-hour premise. State that latitude, weather and exact current-year seasonal instants are absent, so local sunrise cannot be computed. Re-derive after changing axis, latitude circle or arc rather than editing season labels alone. Keep source drawings and corrections, separating diagram conditions, paper results and unperformed observation. Static reading tests neither device astronomy features nor a year of field illumination.
