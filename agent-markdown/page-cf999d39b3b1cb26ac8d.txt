Design an imaginary coastal arch with a continuous distant sea visible through its opening, then layer lit rock, near water and foam.

English translation of the [Chinese original](<https://allluckly.cn/guides/aquarell-sea-arch-opening-coast-surf/>), translated on 2026-11-20. Original publication dates, attribution, code and version notes are retained.

Paint an imaginary sea arch viewed obliquely from offshore, with sky and distant sea visible through the opening. Give the arch thickness and connect both sides to the ground before using overlaps between rock and near surf to locate the water. This is not a study of an existing landmark and cannot establish geological age or current terrain. U.S. National Park Service material on sea-arch formation provides background for understanding the rock and opening.

## Plan sea, sky and the opening as negative space

Create an approximately 2000 × 1400 landscape canvas, put the horizon slightly below the upper middle, and leave water at right. Block the opening in a light color and check that it remains open from the top down to the water before drawing rock texture. Use separate sky, distant sea, rock, near water and foam layers, and save the sketch; dimensions are a composition choice. If the opening becomes an unreadable slit when reduced, widen the negative space or change the view instead of painting a blue fake opening over solid rock.

## Connect both rock masses through a substantial arch

Draw the arch outline around the negative space, connecting the left to a broad cliff and the right to a narrower pillar in the sea. Do not leave an unexplained break between the top and pillar. An inner dark edge can show thickness, but the arch should not become a weightless thin line. Keep the opening irregular rather than a perfect semicircular doorway. In a monochrome draft trace the outline from left cliff across the top, down the right pillar and to the sea; establish those connections before choosing colors.

## Continue the distant sea and horizon behind the opening

First draw the distant sea as one complete color plane, then let the rock layer cover it; the water visible inside and outside the opening is part of the same surface. The horizon may be interrupted by the pillar, but its visible portions need consistent height and direction. Where sky appears inside the hole, the sky–sea boundary follows that same horizontal line. Do not lift the inner horizon merely to fill the hole. Temporarily hide the rock and check that sea and sky continue; restore the rock and confirm blue does not spill onto its near surface.

## Use three planes for light and the underside

Assume light enters from upper left. Paint the top's lit face lighter warm gray, the outer wall middle gray and the downward-facing interior cooler dark gray. Use broad planes to explain turns before selecting hard or softly transitioned edges; do not put the same black line around everything. A darker interior need not be pure black: retain separation from the sea. If the arch resembles a paper cutout, make its underside readable and check its joins to both rock masses instead of only increasing contrast.

## Place cracks on rock without closing the opening

Choose two or three groups of short cracks on large faces, changing their direction with surface turns and stopping visible lines at the opening's edge. Alternate broad light marks and a few fine dark lines instead of an even grid. A crack need not span the entire top, and an illustration cannot establish stability. If dark cracks merge into the opening's boundary, soften interior texture and restore a clear negative shape. Edit texture on its appropriate layer without erasing the sea behind it.

## Put near surf where water meets rock

Put near water in front of the rock, use deeper blue-gray to suggest wave direction, and add broken light foam bands near the pillar. Let foam overlap a small portion of the base while retaining a readable rock–water contact. Avoid an even white ring that makes the rock a floating sticker. Near bands may be wider, with only a few fine marks on distant water. Foam is a composition choice, not a safe water level or live sea condition. If consulting a photograph, avoid copying its entire composition and adjust foam lighting consistently to the chosen light source.

Without moving the horizon, run a near wave diagonally from lower right toward the pillar and break it into smaller foam patches at contact, leaving dark water between them. If white patches merge and obscure the foot, remove some foam rather than move the pillar; if the scene resembles snow, reduce white coverage in the distance. Preserve the previous foam layer to compare versions while the other planes remain fixed.

## Hide layers to inspect depth order

Hide foam and confirm the pillar meets the sea; hide rock and check that horizon and sea extend behind the opening; restore both and verify that foam only partly overlaps the near side. If the opening reads as blue paint on solid stone, inspect layer order and whether the hole is genuinely left open. If inner and outer horizons disagree, correct the sea layer before moving rock. Retain the previous draft and change one relationship at a time so the correction remains traceable.

## Save, export and describe the scene's scope

Save the editable draft, export an image through a control actually offered by the installed version, and open it elsewhere to inspect the hole, horizon and pillar foot for unintended white gaps. Reopen the original and hide only foam; if the rock also disappears, check layer and file selection before overwriting anything. Identify the result as an imaginary coast. Mark unperformed export or reopening pending. Sea arches change with erosion; a static picture cannot establish present conditions, rock stability or safe access.
