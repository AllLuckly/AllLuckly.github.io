One set of three numbers can produce either 8/3 or 4 in a textbook without either being a printing error. Read the definition, intended object and denominator in Book Isle, then keep a traceable variance ledger. Unlike comparing means or naming error bars, this task retains each squared deviation. Calculate manually; the app does not automatically analyse statistics or assess real research.

## Copy the definition and data scope first

Record title, author, edition, chapter and a phrase beside the formula. Ask whether these values are the complete group of interest or a sample from a larger group. Retain the variance symbol, mean definition, summation scope and denominator rather than replacing this chapter’s convention with a formula found elsewhere. Some sources describe a finite group with population variance; others use sample variance for estimation. Read names with their definitions. Mark unspecified sampling unknown: three values alone do not establish random sampling, and a shelf label does not determine statistical meaning.

## Find the mean before listing signed deviations

The original values are 2, 4, 6 in an invented unit called points, not a real survey. Their sum is 12 and mean 4, giving signed deviations minus 2, 0, plus 2. Pair every deviation with its original observation instead of deleting signs and calling the result the original deviations. Their sum zero is a useful check here, not proof of no spread: opposite deviations cancel. If you transcribed 2, 4, 8, the mean changed and the entire chain must be recalculated. Both denominator conventions below use the same mean 4.

## Square deviations before summing the numerator

Square minus 2, 0, plus 2 separately to get 4, 0, 4; the squared-deviation sum is 8. Summing deviations first and then squaring gives zero, a different operation. Units are points squared. Do not call 8 either eight points or the final variance before applying the denominator. Check the square notation explicitly in notes. If a scan’s exponent is unclear, return to the image rather than inserting a remembered formula. Keep the common numerator separate from both denominator conventions to locate whether an error arose in arithmetic or definition selection.

## Keep population description and sample convention separate

For population description of exactly these three values, divide by 3 to obtain variance 8/3. Under the author’s n-minus 1 sample-variance convention, n is 3, denominator 2, result 4. The difference comes from definitions, not an omitted observation. Label both columns and purposes instead of calling 4 universally more correct. Estimators’ statistical properties require sampling assumptions; this task checks definitions and arithmetic, not representativeness of a real population. With one observation, n minus 1 is zero, so this calculation cannot simply continue; record that the stated formula cannot be applied directly.

## Standard deviation restores units but is not the range

Taking square roots gives population-description standard deviation sqrt(8/3), approximately 1.633 points, and sample-convention standard deviation 2 points. Here points is the invented measurement unit, not a decimal-point instruction; write “approximately 1.633 units” if clearer. The range is 6 minus 2, or 4 points. Its numerical coincidence with sample variance 4 does not make them the same quantity; their units differ. Keep name, formula, number and unit together rather than exchanging captions because both show 4. A standard deviation is not a guarantee that every observation lies within that distance of the mean.

## Include repeated observations in the count and denominator

For a control use 2, 4, 4, 6. Both occurrences of 4 are observations, so n is 4 rather than 3 after deduplication. The mean remains 4 and squared-deviation sum 8, but population-description variance becomes 2 and n-minus 1 sample variance 8/3. Do not copy earlier results: the count changed. Label the original three-value and new four-value records separately instead of assuming that adding the mean leaves variance unchanged. This particular calculation does not imply that adding any observation reduces spread. A value other than 4 would require recalculating the mean and numerator too.

## Troubleshoot disagreement through definitions and transcription

When an answer differs, check observation count, mean, each square, denominator convention and final-only rounding before blaming the textbook or app. Retain 8/3 as a fraction until taking its square root rather than treating rounded 2.7 as exact. For unselectable text, transcribe a small formula with chapter and nearby phrase; do not claim search parsed its statistics. If the question asks for standard deviation, variance 4 must not be compared directly with answer 2. Leave unclear symbols, scope or sampling assumptions unresolved instead of inserting guesses into a quotation.

## Exit and return to rebuild both calculation chains

Exit the reader and return to the definition and values 2, 4, 6. Without looking at results, rebuild mean 4, squared sum 8 and both denominators. Check the four-value control’s 2 and 8/3, then identify range and standard deviation separately. Save six fields: edition, data scope, raw observations, definition, per-item calculation and units, retaining unknown sampling conditions. Completion means another reader can reproduce the definition-based difference, not assess a real group from three invented values or have the app validate research or population inference. Establish a new mapping when another chapter changes definitions.
