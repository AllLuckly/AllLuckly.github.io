Drawing branches usually begins with a trunk and adds finer lines on both sides. Negative-space practice reverses the order: observe and paint the sky between branches so pale branches emerge from the background. This forces attention to the distances between forks, preventing every branch from extending at the same angle and length.

## Prepare two colors and a three-layer structure

Create a 1200×1600 pixel canvas with a paper-like pale beige base and muted-blue background. Prepare a slightly darker blue for a few distant gaps. Avoid pure black and white initially; modest lightness contrast suits repeated shape comparisons. Create base-color, sketch, and background-painting layers, ensuring the final blue goes on the background-painting layer rather than altering the base directly.

Choose a brush that covers easily with controllable edges, around 70 to 100 pixels at 100% opacity. Prepare a smaller size around 12 to 20 pixels for forks. Masks, automatic selections, and special texture brushes are unnecessary. If layer and brush opacity are still confusing, read [Three-layer painting workflow](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/aquarell-three-layer-workflow/>).

## Build the structure with varying branch widths

Sketch a trunk extending from lower left to upper right, about 160 pixels wide at the bottom, narrowing to 80 in the middle and tapering toward the end. Include only two main forks: one toward the upper left and one continuing right. Add one or two twigs to each without filling the page. Leave broad sky above and let the lower trunk extend beyond the canvas for a more natural composition.

A fork is not two pipes rigidly joined together. Connect it with a small curved transition, distinguishing the outer turn from the inner notch. Fade the sketch and inspect the gaps: include at least one large triangular gap, one narrow gap, and one area opening beyond the image. If all gaps look alike, adjust the structure before coloring.

## Paint the largest sky shapes first

On the background layer, start filling the largest gap on the right with blue. Fill the center quickly with a large brush, then switch to a smaller one about 20 pixels from the branches. You are defining sky boundaries, not outlining branches. Pause after each gap to compare sizes and confirm that the remaining pale trunk still tapers upward.

Next paint the large area on the left, leaving small gaps between forks until last. Working large to small prevents getting trapped in detail: refining tiny gaps first can consume time before you discover an overly wide trunk. Brushwork in the blue need not be smoothed immediately if the overall gaps read clearly. Slight irregularities along pale edges can suggest natural bark.

## Refine edges with the eraser instead of redrawing the entire tree

If blue intrudes into a branch, erase locally on the background layer to reveal the pale base. Confirm the active layer; erasing the sketch or base will not produce the intended result. Use an eraser smaller than the branch and work in sections along the edge. One long stroke can remove a correct turn. Return to the brush afterward and add blue where the branch is too wide.

Do not polish every branch as smoothly as a vector shape. The trunk's outside can remain continuous while twig tips have slight breaks. The sharp inner corners of forks matter most because they separate branches. If a corner is too blunt, enlarge the adjacent small sky triangle rather than thinning both branches.

## Deepen a few gaps to establish depth

Use the darker blue to lightly deepen two small distant gaps, or add another layer and lower its opacity. Keep dark areas to about one-tenth of the background. Their role is to emphasize selected forks, not shade every edge. Quieter blue near the trunk and slightly darker blue among dense twigs can create variation in spacing and emphasis.

To add a distant second tree, use a separate layer to paint a small silhouette close to the background color behind the main trunk. Its contrast should be weaker than the pale main branches. This is a later variation and can be omitted in the first drawing. Strong background, main branches, and distant tree all competing would disrupt the clear negative-space relationships.

## Check whether the negative spaces hold together

Hide the sketch and zoom out. Pale branches should connect and taper, sky gaps should vary in size, and forks should not contain accidentally broken white lines. Rotate the canvas briefly to notice crowded gaps on one side, then return to the normal orientation to confirm the composition. Keep the base layer visible when exporting an image with the pale background.

**Why does it look like white lightning?** Usually the branch width changes too abruptly, all turns are sharp, or forks lack rounded transitions. Lengthen the trunk's taper and remove one or two crowded small forks. Growth direction matters more than detail count.

**Could I simply paint pale branches for the same effect?** The final colors may look similar, but the observation task differs. Here you compare sky shapes to correct the habit of drawing a tree from memory. Afterward, make a separate positive-shape version and compare the rhythm of the gaps. For background colors, see [Sampling from the canvas](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/aquarell-canvas-color-picking/>), but avoid continually copying details between the two drawings.

For the second exercise, retain only one main trunk and move it to the right. Check whether the broad empty area on the left remains balanced. Do not add many branches merely to fill it; compare the largest sky area with the trunk first. This composition judgment is part of negative-space practice.
