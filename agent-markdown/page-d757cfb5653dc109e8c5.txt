## Check reference and light

Choose a permitted photograph of a perched male Ruby-throated Hummingbird and record source, creator, access date and light direction. Cornell describes a green back and crown, gray-white underside and an iridescent red male throat that can appear dark in poor light. Follow the chosen photograph instead of forcing an evenly bright red throat merely because of the name. Confirm permission before sharing.

## Set up canvas and composition

Create a landscape Aquarell canvas, for example 1700 by 1300 pixels, and save an editable original. Use visible layer controls to separate background, branch, bird base color, throat highlights and sketch. Leave space in front of the bill and behind the tail, and avoid placing the branch exactly on the canvas edge. This size is an exercise choice; use actual controls if labels differ.

## Compare body and head

Block the body with a small oval and mark head, neck, wing root and tail tip. Compare overall length from bill to tail with body height and check the body’s angle to the branch. Connect the head through the neck rather than pasting a circle on top. If the tail touches the frame, move the whole subject instead of changing tail length. At thumbnail size the perched pose and direction should read before feather detail. Hide the branch temporarily to inspect the body silhouette, then restore it and check that the foot contact matches the reference; do not let a background line alter the pose.

## Draw the slender bill and eye

Follow the photo for a slender, slightly downcurved bill, comparing its base width, tip direction and head length before drawing. Do not turn it into a thick triangle. Place the eye inside the head by comparing distances to the bill base and crown; any highlight should be tiny. If the tip is blurred or blocked by a flower branch, draw only the visible part rather than borrowing an exact length from another photo.

## Place folded wing and tail

Observe where the folded wing covers the body and block it as one shape before adding a few feather edges from the reference. Cornell describes relatively short wings when perched, but the exact visible relationship must be checked in the chosen photograph. Compare wing tip and tail tip. Connect the tail base to the body before drawing its outline so it does not float like a separate dark leaf.

## Block green back and pale underside

Lay in a green back and lighter gray-white underside while keeping head, back, belly and wing connected as one volume. Use varying green values for light rather than one saturated color everywhere, and separate the belly from its background. Hide detail to check bill, body, wing and tail. If the bird becomes a single round block, repair large shapes before adding feather lines.

## Suggest the throat’s response to light

On a separate throat layer mark the dark area visible in the photo, then add limited red or warm highlights where light reaches it. Follow the boundary with dark head and pale breast rather than coloring the entire head red. Hide and show highlights: the dark base should remain, and the bright area should explain lighting rather than act as a fixed species label. If the photo shows no bright red, retain the dark throat and note the light.

## Connect feet and branch

Draw the branch’s direction and thickness, including which part is hidden by the body. Follow the visible reference for legs and toes gripping it. Front toes cover the branch and rear portions may be hidden; leave unclear joints unknown rather than inventing them. Check contact points so visible support touches the branch rather than floating above it.

## Check background and small size

Use a muted background that separates fine bill, green crown and branch from neighboring areas. If flowers appear, follow visible parts of the photograph without inventing a pollination action. Reduce the image to phone size and check that the bill remains visible, red throat has not spread over the whole head and the branch still supports the bird. Adjust nearby background value before thickening the subject contour.

## Troubleshoot and export

If the bill looks stuck outside the face, fix the connection at its base. If the throat resembles a red scarf, compare its boundaries with jaw and breast. If feet float, inspect visible toe contact. Save the editable original and use an available share or export control to create a separate PNG or JPEG. Reopen it to inspect bill, tail, branch and highlights for clipping. Return to verify editable layers and record permission and unperformed device steps.

## Compare bright and dark throat versions

Duplicate the editable work. Keep a dark throat in one version and add only a small amount of reference-supported red reflection in the other; leave head, bill, body and background in the same positions. Compare them side by side at small size and ensure the throat remains between jaw and pale breast instead of spreading over the eye or crown. If the bright version resembles a red hood, reduce the highlight and restore the dark boundary rather than changing the whole bird’s proportions. Record the lighting assumption: the reflection expresses the chosen light and does not claim field observation or identical red from every angle. Revisit the sketch to check visible toes against the branch; leave blurred parts unresolved rather than inventing a toe count.
