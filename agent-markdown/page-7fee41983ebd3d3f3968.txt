## Separate imitation from event description

Invented example one is “咚，门响了一声” (“Dong—the door sounded once”); example two only “门响了” (“The door sounded”). 咚 imitates sound, while the door clause narrates a related source. The second states an event without that imitation; sound reference alone does not make functions identical. “Once” is explicit only in the first. Add no count or duration to the second. Attribute neither to a real author, and infer no kicking, wind or knocking without other evidence. Record source certainty separately from sound imitation.

## Leave uncertain sources unresolved

In “咚，屋里的人抬起头” (“Dong; someone inside looked up”), sound and reaction do not explicitly identify a door. Do not carry that source over. Door or dropped-object possibilities remain guesses with missing evidence stated. Adjacent events alone may not establish that the sound caused the look; inspect connectives/context. Familiar spelling is no recording identification and establishes no knocker’s identity. If later text explains the source, append that location/time rather than pretending it was explicit at first reading.

## Do not convert repetition into acoustic measurements

咚咚 differs from 咚 and may suggest rhythmic recurrence, but context determines event counts; each character is not unconditionally one separate knock. Retain explicit continuous-duration wording where present and invent no seconds otherwise. Large type/exclamation marks may emphasize reading without measuring decibels or frequency. Separate textual features from reading impressions: louder reading is not proof of louder original sound. No recording or measurement apparatus is used, and Book Isle is not assumed to turn sound words into accurate audio samples.

## Check whose speech contains the sound word

If a character says “I only heard one dong,” that is a reported perception, not automatically confirmed narration. Direct narrative 咚 remains textual arrangement, not raw real-world audio. Record quotation scope, reporting verb and reporter rather than merging all sound words into one reliable witness. A character may be corrected later, but retain possibilities without declaring unreliability in advance. Beyond counting words, ask who supports source/count claims. Without context retain spelling alone.

## Preserve imitation/narration across languages

Dong may gloss this example without claiming identical pronunciation, convention or acoustics to 咚. A translation using bang or only “the door sounded” needs separate checks of imitation, source and event before discussing effect. Different spelling alone proves no mistranslation, and the second example still lacks an imitative word. Label your gloss separately from published translations; dictionary explanations do not overwrite original wording. Monolingual checking is sufficient without inventing translator choices, and reading aloud is no measured original-object sound.

## Test a controlled wording change

In your own control change “咚，门响了一声” to “咚，杯子落了下来” (“Dong; a cup fell”). Imitative spelling remains while source/action change; the door record cannot remain. This rewrite establishes no universal cup-fall sound and requires no dropped-cup test. Alternatively remove only 咚 from the first sentence: event count remains but imitation disappears. Keep these as distinct controls rather than changing both at once. Name originals/variants separately with edit notes, without inserting them into the sole book. No literary-rewrite feature is assumed.
