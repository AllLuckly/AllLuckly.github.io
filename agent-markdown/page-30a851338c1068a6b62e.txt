## Choose a reference and identify the subject

Choose a permitted side-view photograph of a Canada Goose on water and record the source, creator, access date and direction. Cornell describes a black head and neck, white cheeks and chinstrap, brown back, long neck and webbed feet. Water may hide the feet and light changes apparent colors, so do not add unseen feet or combine another goose’s pose. Confirm the photograph’s license covers your intended use.

## Create a landscape canvas

Create a landscape canvas in Aquarell, for example 1800 by 1300 pixels, and save an editable original. This size is an exercise choice. Use visible layer controls to separate background, water, goose body, detail and sketch. Lightly place the waterline and leave space above the head, behind the tail and ahead of the bird so the long neck is not cramped. If labels differ, use the controls actually available.

## Compare body and neck

Block the floating body with a long oval and mark the highest back point, tail tip and front of breast. Draw the neck’s center direction and compare neck length with body length and its connection to the breast. The neck may bend, so do not force it into a vertical rectangle. At thumbnail size check head position against the body; if too far back, fix the neck base before moving the head alone.

## Place head and broad bill

Block the head at the top of the neck and compare head height, bill base and bill tip. Follow the reference for a broad relatively flat bill and its connection to the forehead. Place the small eye within the head, not on its outline. Do not enlarge it to compensate for dark coloring, and omit nostril or fine marks that are not visible.

## Paint black head and neck with a white chinstrap

Block the head and neck in black or dark gray while leaving room for the white cheek patch. Follow the photo to place the pale area from cheek toward the underside of the jaw. Its shape depends on viewpoint; do not force both sides into a symmetrical frontal design in profile. At thumbnail size distinguish bill, eye and white patch. If the face becomes one black silhouette, adjust dark-gray values rather than enlarging the patch.

## Separate back, breast and folded wing

Use brown for the back and a lighter warm gray-brown for the breast, marking the folded wing boundary from the photograph. Preserve the whole body volume first, then suggest feather overlap with a few directional edges instead of outlining every feather as an identical black stripe. With detail hidden, back, breast, neck base and tail should remain connected. These colors describe the painted lighting, not a unique species color chart.

## Handle the waterline and overlap

Observe where the body meets the water and place the waterline against breast, belly and tail. Do not show a complete belly contour through water that hides it; if the feet are not visible, do not invent their underwater shape. Put water at the appropriate layer and use a few horizontal ripples near the body without crossing the neck. If the goose seems to float like a sticker, first check contact at the breast rather than enlarging a shadow.

## Arrange reflection and background

Simplify the background into muted shore or distant water without letting a dark same-value patch swallow the neck silhouette. If the reference has a reflection, use the waterline as the divider and paint simplified color patches interrupted by ripples. Do not assume a perfectly sharp mirror image. Keep space around the neck so head direction and its connection to the body remain readable when reduced.

## Compare shape and identifying details

Hide feather marks and background to inspect the long neck, body and bill silhouette, then restore the white cheek patch and brown back against the reference. If it reads as a generic waterbird, first check neck length and patch position. If the patch looks like a white ring around the entire neck, revisit its visible boundary. Change one issue at a time so simultaneous changes in head proportion and color do not obscure the cause.

## Troubleshoot, save and review

If the bill looks pasted on, check its base against the head; if the neck base is disconnected, inspect how breast and back lines meet it. Save the editable original, then use an available share or export control to create a separate PNG or JPEG. Reopen it to check head, neck, tail tip and waterline for clipping, and return to confirm body and water remain editable. Retain source, permission and one improvement; mark unperformed device steps as pending.

## Check water contact and neck silhouette

Follow the waterline from breast to tail and identify which body edges are visible and which are hidden by water. If a water line crosses the body, adjust layer order or erase the obscured section. A ripple touching the belly can look like part of the body at small size, so move one ripple if needed. Compare both sides of the neck: dark grays can suggest volume, but its width should follow the photo rather than becoming broader to emphasize the white cheek patch. Use a neutral-background copy with detail hidden to check that dark neck and white cheek remain distinct. Restore the background and inspect the waterline and neck together. Simplify feather edges that are blurred in the reference instead of borrowing unseen details from another goose.

## Recheck the waterline

Trace the waterline past breast, belly and tail and identify the section hidden by the body. Remove ripples crossing the goose, then zoom out to inspect its contact with the water.
