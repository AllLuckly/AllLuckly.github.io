Stippling does not require identical dots; it requires a readable form from a distance. A pear narrows at the top and fills out below, making it useful for observing how density changes follow a contour. This exercise builds a pear with dark-brown dots on a warm-white background. Test a small area for reliable marks first, then build gradually rather than treating more dots as the definition of completion.

## First verify how to make a clear dot

Create a 1200×1400-pixel portrait canvas with a pale warm base, adding one sketch layer and one stippling layer. Choose a round brush with relatively clear edges, about 8 to 12 pixels wide, initially at 100% opacity. Make ten very short test strokes in a corner and observe whether they leave small round dots or short ellipses. Tap behavior depends on the current gesture settings. If a tap only toggles the interface, make a short stroke and lift instead of repeatedly tapping and waiting.

You do not need to increase brush spacing dramatically and drag regular dotted lines for this exercise. That approach suits decorative borders; see [Brush-spacing exercise](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/aquarell-brush-spacing/>); the pear's values need density controlled independently in different areas. After confirming the mark, clear the test layer or create a new painting layer, retaining the parameter notes.

## Build the pear from three widths

Set the pear's height to about 850 pixels, its widest lower section to about 520 pixels, its shoulders to about 300 pixels, and the narrowing below the stem to about 150 pixels. Connect these positions with faint lines, allowing slight asymmetry. Avoid a pointed bottom; the section resting on the table should be flatter. Tilt the stem slightly toward the upper right to reduce the stiffness of a perfectly centered composition.

Set the light at the upper left and reserve an irregular light area on the left side. It need not form a neat white border along the silhouette; it should follow the pear's surface. Mark the lower-right shadow area with three to five faint indications, then stop relying on outlines. Reduce the sketch layer until it is light enough for the final dots to define the form's boundary naturally.

## Use only sparse midtones on the first pass

Place sparse small dots in the pear's middle region, with neighboring dots about three to five dot diameters apart. After completing a small patch, move elsewhere rather than filling rows from the upper-left like a printer. Varying the order helps prevent horizontal or vertical stripes. Use fewer, smaller dots near the light area, and leave room to build density near the right-side shadow.

It is normal for the first pass to look pale. When reduced, the image should already read as a pear, though its volume is still weak. Check whether overly even dots have turned the outline into a string of beads. If so, add a few staggered dots just inside the contour to make the boundary a density transition. Do not enclose the stippling with a continuous solid line.

## Connect the shadows into a direction on the second pass

Increase density mainly in the lower-right half, reducing spacing to about one or two dot diameters. Near the deepest shadow, allow a few dots to touch without immediately joining them into a solid mass. The shadow should form a coherent region rather than isolated black patches scattered everywhere. Follow the pear's rounded belly, gradually increasing or decreasing density as the surface turns.

Deepen the small hollow beneath the stem with finer dots and build the stem from a few short strokes. Concentrate dots most tightly where the pear meets the table, then spread the cast shadow horizontally outward. A separate shadow layer makes its position easier to adjust. Its dots can be slightly larger and more widely spaced to distinguish them from the skin texture. Do not make the cast shadow a rounded mass as full as the pear itself.

## Suggest the skin with a few variations

Consider skin spots only after the volume works. In a few midtone areas, add slightly larger brown dots between the existing small ones, keeping the total to about a dozen. These represent surface markings, not a new value structure. Keep the highlight clean. Equal numbers of spots everywhere make the pear dirty and flat.

Try two colors in a new practice copy: lay down a very thin pale yellow-green base, then retain the dark-brown dot layer. Do not cover your only original. In the two-color version, the base merely supports the image; density must still establish the shadows. If uncertain about color choices, first refer to [Limited-palette exercise](<https://allluckly.cn/en/guides/aquarell-limited-palette/>), choosing just one background color and one dot color.

## Check the result and troubleshoot repeating patterns

Zoom out to roughly a quarter-size view and confirm that the light, middle, and dark regions separate at a glance. Then zoom in to see whether many dots have dragged into thin lines. A few ellipses do not hurt the exercise, but obvious long tails change the texture. The pear should remain clearly readable after hiding the sketch. Export a normal PNG to check its display appearance, and retain the editable file so you can adjust only the dot layer next time.

**Why does it resemble a printed halftone screen?** Common causes are identical dot sizes, regular row spacing, and matching starting points in each row. Vary the order in which you place dots and adjust spacing through the value transitions; this usually helps more than randomly changing colors. Stippling needs organized density, not a mechanical grid.

**Can I quickly fill the shadows with a very large brush?** It can produce a dark block, but it skips this density exercise. If the shadow is already solid, redo a smaller area on a new layer and compare sparse, medium-density, and close-packed states. Do not try to rescue an entirely over-dark dot layer by erasing individual holes.

You can also inspect the work flipped horizontally. If the darkest region looks like a patch pasted onto the surface, check whether the light area is too large or the shadow too narrow. For the next exercise, keep the same brush but move the light to the upper right and change the density distribution accordingly. This comparison tests whether you understand volume or have simply memorized the first drawing's dot positions.
