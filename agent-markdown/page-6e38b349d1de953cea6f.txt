Use public species information and a permitted upper-view reference to layer adult wing shapes, veins, dark borders and white spots.

## Choose an upper-view reference

The US Fish and Wildlife Service describes adult monarchs as having orange wings, black veins and borders, with a double row of white spots on the upper-side border. Choose a permitted photograph from above with open wings and record its source and specific usage rights. If a wing is obscured or damaged, draw what is visible rather than completing it from another picture. This adult study is a different life stage from the site’s chrysalis exercise; do not touch or disturb wild butterflies or move plants where they are resting to arrange a composition.

## Compose and save an editable original

Use the current Aquarell interface to create an approximately 1800 by 1400 pixel canvas and save an editable original. The size is only a suggestion. Lightly mark the body center, horizontal wing span and upper and lower margins so all visible wing edges have room. Separate sketch, background, body, wings and details using available layers. If button labels differ by device, use identifiable functions and do not assume automatic wing-pattern generation.

## Extend two pairs of wings from the body

Block in a slender body and determine head direction from the photograph. Mark the approximate forewing and hindwing outlines on both sides. Connect wings near the thorax rather than the end of the abdomen; forewings usually appear ahead or above depending on orientation. Compare each visible wing’s length, width, attachment and outer curve before adding small spots. Perspective may make the sides unequal, so do not force a mechanical mirror; compare each wing tip’s distance from the body before adjusting the composition.

## Check how forewings and hindwings meet

Draw visible wing roots near the body and determine the boundary and possible overlap between forewing and hindwing. At small size, ensure the body remains between the four wings rather than a dark outline turning them into one centerless orange disk. Where one wing is obscured by another, indicate the overlap without completing unseen patterns. Confirm the large outlines before adding color and veins.

## Block orange and reserve the border

Fill each wing interior with orange, leaving enough space around the edge for a dark border. Do not first black out the whole silhouette and then attempt to erase the pattern from it. Light, shadow and photographic conditions affect the observed orange; begin with a middle tone and add lighter or darker areas where visible. Keep body and wing surfaces distinguishable in the layer structure so borders can be adjusted without overwriting the original.

## Run veins outward from wing roots

Using the reference, mark the most prominent dark veins from each wing root outward, then add visible branches. Observe how they divide orange areas rather than drawing arbitrary parallel lines from the edge. Fine lines can meet the dark border near the margin. At reduced size, if veins merge into a black mass, reduce line width or count; if they look like a floating grid, check their direction from the correct root. Do not infer sex from a single drawing pattern.

## Place dark borders and white spots

Draw a dark border along each visible wing edge, adjusting width to the photograph and retaining wing contours at corners rather than creating a uniform ring. Add light marks for white spots. The public source describes two rows on the upper side, but draw only the observable range if the photograph is blurry or occluded. Leave dark spaces between spots so they do not form one white line; exact spot counts need not be copied, and spots should not be scattered across every orange region.

## Keep wing edges legible against the background

Use a muted light background or simplified plant shapes to support the subject. Avoid putting dark leaves directly against dark wing borders where they could be mistaken for wing area. Hide spots and fine veins temporarily, leaving body, four wing outlines and main colors to check roots and forewing-hindwing proportions. Restore details and reduce the view; if spots vanish, first increase contrast around the border rather than making every spot the same large circle.

## Record the adult beside the chrysalis study

Place the adult thumbnail beside the site’s chrysalis exercise and compare only visible structures: the adult has extended wings, while the chrysalis has its suspension and surface markings. Do not invent intermediate transformation stages or draw adult veins on the chrysalis. Record source, viewpoint and unseen portions for each image so features from different photographs and life stages are not assembled into one object.

## Save and recheck

Save the editable original, use a share or export entry actually visible in the current version to save a separate image, then reopen it to inspect wing edges, any visible antennae and canvas corners. Return to the original to identify body, wing and background layers. If spots or wing tips are missing, check visibility and crop before overwriting the only original. Keep public species and photographic sources; mark device, export and reopening steps pending if unperformed.

## Design a readable rhythm of white spots

On a separate sketch, draw two short stretches of dark wing border: crowd the white spots into a white streak in the first and leave dark gaps between them in the second. Reduce both to the intended display size and compare which still reads as spots on an edge, then use the photograph to choose spacing. This exercise does not require inventing hidden spots or identifying an individual butterfly by the example count.
