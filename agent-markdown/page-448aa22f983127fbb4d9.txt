Use an invented footprint trail to check left/right alternation, toe direction, receding spacing and recessed sand shading rather than repeated stamps.

Shrinking repeated copies of one foot shape does not necessarily produce a readable trail. Feet must alternate, toes must support the travel direction, and recesses must not look like raised insoles. Draw simplified barefoot prints receding toward the upper right, using invented shapes rather than real footprint identification or gait measurements. Drawing layers in Aquarell support staged checks, but route, left/right identity and depth are arranged manually.

## Establish route and ground direction

Use an approximately 1800×1400 px landscape canvas with a pale warm-sand background. Sketch a faint route curving from lower left toward upper right, marking near, middle and far zones before detailed prints. Let the ground lighten and lose contrast gradually into the distance without rising into a sudden sand wall. Separate sketch, near prints, middle/far prints, shadows and grains. The route is a centre guide, not a line every print must occupy. Reduce the view to check its recession. If a bend becomes an abrupt reversal, smooth the route rather than forcing later footprints to rotate individually around a discontinuity.

## Distinguish left and right with one simple pair

Draw one near pair with smaller heels, broader forefeet and simplified toe contours. Identify a left/right pair: big toes sit on the inward-facing sides rather than translating one foot shape twice. Small-toe count detail may be omitted, but inner and outer sides must read. Label left and right in the sketch and hide those labels in the finished image. If they resemble sole stickers, weaken the complete outlines and later establish recessed shading. If both splay excessively outward, correct their overall directions rather than adding toe dots to conceal contour problems.

## Alternate positions around the route

Place prints alternately left and right around the route, with related lateral spacing rather than three consecutive same-side prints presented as ordinary forward walking. Point toes roughly along the local route tangent, turning gradually instead of suddenly facing backward. Use L/R or left/right placeholders first, read their sequence, then substitute foot shapes. This is illustration rhythm, not a prescribed real stride length. Move an unexplained centreline print toward its assigned side or establish a deliberate unusual action. For the first study, use simple walking around a bend rather than mixing jumps, running and reverse motion without explanation.

## Reduce both print size and spacing with distance

Make near prints larger and more widely separated along the route, reducing both apparent size and spacing with distance. Do not shrink shapes while increasing their spacing, or abruptly return the last two to middle-ground size. Three or four pairs suffice initially; excessive prints become noise. Their ground-plane shapes also foreshorten rather than all facing the viewer with identical proportions. Mark their bounds lightly and compare near/middle/far gradients before detail. If distant prints resemble scattered pebbles, weaken dark edges and restore the ordered trail rather than adding tiny toes to rescue recession.

## Keep recessed shading from reversing

Assume upper-left light and test the interior walls of one near print: the wall facing upper left is lighter and the opposite wall facing away is darker, with only slight pale unevenness along the outer sand rim. Compare a simplified hollow sketch to ensure you have not added an exterior cast-shadow ring that turns the print into a raised plaque. Keep the floor somewhat darker than surrounding sand but not solid black. Heel and forefoot may differ slightly in depth without implying actual pressure analysis. If relief is ambiguous, hide grains and toe marks and compare broad values. Rebuild one sample before applying it elsewhere rather than darkening every outline as a substitute for depth.

## Keep grains and broken edges subordinate

Add a few collapsed edges and fine sand dots only to two or three near prints, breaking edges without changing left/right identity. Suggest toes with a few soft small hollows rather than separate black holes. Distant prints need broad shapes and low contrast, with grains no clearer than the prints themselves. Hide texture to check the trail, then restore it. If the ground resembles scattered sesame seeds, remove unrelated dots and retain quiet space. Sand colour may vary warmly or coolly without changing lighting direction beneath every print. Natural irregularity should build on a traceable sequence rather than randomly damage silhouettes.

## Check labels and a texture-free version

Restore left/right labels in a copy and read them in travel order, then trace each print from heel toward toes to check route alignment. Hide texture separately to inspect recessed shading: these tests distinguish identity from material. Deliberately substitute a left print for a middle right one to see the broken sequence, then inspect the original. No real footprint photograph is needed, and the drawing cannot identify a person or diagnose their actions. If one shape is ambiguous, compare its inner boundary with the near sample pair instead of deciding identity solely from its side of the route. Position and shape must agree.

## Preserve the recession hierarchy after export

Save the editable artwork and inspect trail endpoints, nearest pair and final two prints in the PNG at sharing size. If distant prints merge into a black dashed line, reduce contrast or remove one pair rather than enlarging every toe. If near prints look raised, repair their sample interior shading instead of adding a heavier tabletop-like cast shadow. Keep a texture-free preview. A later study can change route curvature and redistribute alternation and spacing, not simply bend the old group and skip checking. Record an exact fault, such as the third right print still pointing along the old route, so the next edit targets a specific print and error type.
