Build a clearly fictional weather legend that distinguishes cold and warm fronts by shape and text.

## Set the legend’s scope

This image explains cold- and warm-front symbols only; it depicts no real region or live forecast. A US National Weather Service example uses a blue line with triangles for a cold front and a red line with semicircles for a warm front. Follow that convention and label the image “Teaching illustration, not a forecast.” For actual weather, consult the latest local meteorological information.

## Create an editable canvas

Create an approximately 1600 by 1000 landscape canvas in VectorPaint, choosing dimensions for your reading context. Separate background, two front lines, symbols and text into layers using controls visible in your installed version. Reserve two equally wide blank regions, cold front above and warm front below, with text space on the right. Save an editable original before adjusting.

## Draw the cold-front baseline

Use a path or line tool to draw a gently curving blue line from left to right in the upper region. It is fictional and does not locate a real front. Start with a consistent substantial stroke so it does not break at small size, and check that both ends remain on the canvas. Keep the curve gentle rather than suggesting a detailed geographic boundary.

## Place triangle symbols

Draw one filled blue triangle with its base near the line and its point on the same side of the line. Duplicate it or draw several and space them legibly along the curve. In this example the points indicate cold-front movement. Shrink the view to spot triangles hidden by the line or touching one another; rotate and move individual marks as needed to keep their tips consistent. Where the curve bends sharply, use fewer marks and arrange them individually so they stay near the baseline without crossing to the other side.

## Draw the warm-front baseline

Draw a red line in the lower region, roughly but not necessarily exactly matching the upper line’s length. Practice with a straight line first, then choose a gentle curve if useful; leave ample space between the two. Do not connect the lines to an invented low-pressure center, because this exercise is a legend rather than a complete weather-system diagram.

## Make warm-front semicircles

Construct a filled red semicircle with a path or combined shapes, placing its straight edge against the warm-front line and its rounded side consistently on one side. Duplicate or draw marks individually with readable spacing. If rotation pulls a flat edge away from the line, correct it rather than relying on color to conceal the gap. Confirm the marks are semicircles, not full circles, and are not mixed with triangles.

## Add names and descriptions

Label the blue line “Cold front: blue line and triangles” and the red line “Warm front: red line and semicircles,” keeping text clear of lines and marks. You may explain that cold-front triangle tips show movement direction; do not infer a real warm front’s location, time or weather intensity from this example. Keep the title and teaching disclaimer more readable than decorative small text. Use dark explanatory text against the background. If a line is too long, break it at a meaningful point rather than shrinking it to squeeze into the space. Ensure translated labels do not cover the marks or the disclaimer.

## Distinguish without color alone

Temporarily preview the image in grayscale, or inspect a separate export using a system viewer. Check that triangles versus semicircles, vertical position and text still distinguish the rows. If not, enlarge marks, increase line-to-background value difference and verify complete labels rather than merely making colors brighter. Restore color while preserving the red-blue convention and preventing thin lines from merging into the background.

## Check direction and overlaps

Follow the cold-front line from left to right, inspecting each triangle’s base and tip. If a tip flips to the opposite side, rotate that mark and adjust its contact point. Check that all warm-front arcs lie on the same side. Zoom in for collisions and out for continuous lines; after every adjustment revisit the labels because enlarged marks can crowd text. Return the canvas to its normal reading orientation, verify the two row labels are not swapped, and inspect the symbol-to-line joins for confusing gaps.

## Export and inspect the result

Save the editable original and export a separate PNG from a control visible in the installed version. Reopen it at its intended display size and check symbols, labels and disclaimer for cropping. If strokes become soft, increase output size or line weight and export again. If a shape changes, correct the original; do not present the result as a weather chart certified by a meteorological agency.

## Keep a reusable inspection record

Keep a legend copy without real place names and record the sources, chosen red and blue, mark orientation and export dimensions. When changing the background or translating labels, first check triangle and semicircle shapes, then label-to-color correspondence, then repeat grayscale and small-size checks. For an actual forecast, direct readers to their local weather service instead of inventing an unverified location or time.
