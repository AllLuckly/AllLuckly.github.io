Use rectangles, a fold line and dimension labels to compare the geometric half of A4 with nominal A5.

## Define the question

Folding a landscape A4 sheet across its long dimension makes two equal halves, commonly described as close to A5. Published nominal sizes in whole millimeters differ by half a millimeter from direct division. Adobe lists A4 as 210 by 297 mm and A5 as 148 by 210 mm. This drawing separates the two figures. It is not a calibrated cutting template, and it does not assume the app automatically recognizes or outputs a physical paper size.

## Calculate both sizes

Place A4 in landscape orientation: 297 mm wide and 210 mm high. Folding at the horizontal midpoint gives 297÷2=148.5, so the ideal geometric half is 148.5 mm wide by 210 mm high. Nominal A5 in portrait orientation, matching the half-sheet orientation, is 148 mm wide by 210 mm high. The short-dimension values differ by 0.5 mm. Do not label the half’s 148.5 as an exact 148, or infer that every physical sheet, fold or cut achieves the ideal.

## Arrange canvas and legend

Create an approximately 1600 by 1200 pixel landscape canvas in the current VectorPaint interface and save an editable original. Reserve the left side for A4 and its center line, the right for half-sheet and A5 labels, and the bottom for a comparison. Canvas pixels serve layout only. Separate paper rectangles, fold, dimension lines, text and background using available objects or layers. If labels differ, use identifiable functions in the current interface.

## Construct the A4 outline manually

Draw a landscape rectangle with width-to-height ratio 297:210. If the interface offers identifiable width and height fields, use the same scale, for example 594 by 420 drawing units; otherwise draw proportionally and label it schematic. Use a light fill and darker outline and label it “A4 landscape: 297×210 mm.” The 594×420 values are enlarged drawing units for layout, not physical millimeters, and should not be confused with nominal A3 dimensions.

## Place the fold center line

Draw a thin dashed line from the top to bottom edge at the midpoint of the long side. If the example width is 594 drawing units, the line lies 297 units from the left edge. Both regions have equal width and correspond to an ideal half-sheet of 148.5 mm. Label it “fold line” and explain the dashed line in the legend. Do not draw it as a missing cut-out or imply the sheet has already been folded.

## Label half-sheet and A5 separately

At right draw a small rectangle for the half-sheet labeled “geometric half: 148.5×210 mm.” Use text or a neighboring rectangle for “nominal A5: 148×210 mm.” Both may be drawn at a common scale, but the 0.5 mm difference can be too small to see on screen. Use explicit numbers rather than silently exaggerating it. If the difference is enlarged for visibility, label it “difference exaggerated” so readers do not infer a true-scale comparison.

## Check dimension lines and orientation

Place 297 along A4’s horizontal long edge and 210 along its vertical short edge. For the half-sheet, label horizontal 148.5 and vertical 210. Use mm consistently and separate text from arrows so 148.5 is not read as the distance from fold line to the far edge of the full sheet. A5 is often listed as portrait 148×210; rotating paper changes which edge is horizontal, not the edge lengths. Read the directions from the drawing and compare with the paper calculation.

## Reverse-check with a numeric table

Make three working rows: landscape A4 297×210; its equal half 148.5×210; portrait nominal A5 148×210. Check numbers, units, orientation and legend in each row. If the line is one-quarter of the full width from the left, the halves are unequal and the fold must move. If right-side shapes appear greatly different, confirm whether exaggeration is labeled. Do not remove the numerical distinction merely because A5 is commonly described as half A4.

## Compare two A5 sheets and two half-sheets arithmetically

Two ideal halves placed along the 297 mm dimension give 148.5×2=297. Using the integer nominal values, two 148 mm A5 edges give 296, one millimeter less than A4’s 297. This shows why the nominal and geometric numbers should not be conflated. It does not predict a particular physical gap: paper tolerances, folds, cutting and measurement affect observation.

Write “148.5+148.5=297” and “148+148=296” by the legend so the difference can be checked directly. If the diagram uses a twofold drawing scale, the 1 mm numerical difference corresponds to 2 drawing units. If shapes are drawn by eye, say they are not to scale and do not measure actual millimeters from the screen. Let the labels explain the calculation instead of portraying a tiny difference as a large gap.

## Handle readability and print boundaries

Use solid paper boundaries and a dashed fold line with a text legend so they remain distinct in grayscale. For printing, check paper type, scaling and margins in the actual print app; nonprintable areas and fit settings may change output. Print an ordinary test page and measure it before deciding its use. This guide is not calibrated on a specific device and does not claim a direct print is an exact cutting template. Retain the unprinted original for adjustment.

Reopen and check the A4 outline, center line, half-sheet 148.5, nominal A5 148, units and text. Return to identify rectangles, lines and labels. ## Verify the length difference through area

Calculate the areas separately in the draft: the geometric half is 148.5×210 = 31,185 square millimeters and nominal A5 is 148×210 = 31,080 square millimeters, a difference of 105 square millimeters. Write square millimeters explicitly rather than mixing area with the 0.5-millimeter edge difference. This compares the stated nominal numbers only; it does not establish the accuracy of physical folding or printing.
