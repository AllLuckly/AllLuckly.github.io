Use public references and a permitted photograph to practice an upright animal, the edge of its mound and its visible black tail tip.

## Identify the species and reference

The US National Park Service describes the black-tailed prairie dog as having a black-tipped tail, small ears, a short tail and strong legs; its burrow entrance can have a surrounding mound. Choose a photograph permitted for use, prioritizing a clear relationship between body and entrance. If the tail is hidden, do not attach one from another photograph merely to show the black tip. Record source, usage rights and species. Do not approach or feed wildlife, disturb it, or enter a burrow.

## Arrange the composition and original

Create an approximately 1800 by 1400 pixel landscape canvas in the current Aquarell interface and save an editable original. These proportions are only exercise choices. Use a low horizon for the distant prairie and leave room for a foreground mound and entrance. Separate sketch, mound, animal and details using available functions; if labels differ, use identifiable entries on the device. The subject may be off center, but leave space above the head and around the tail and hole.

## Draw the entrance and mound first

Block the mound as a low irregular earth-colored rise, using a curved boundary for the slope near the opening. Determine entrance direction from the reference rather than always drawing a front-facing circle. Paint the light soil first and use a dark shape for the visible opening, preserving its connection to the surrounding rim. If the animal obscures the entrance, draw only what is exposed rather than overlaying a complete black circle on the body. Mound height and hole size are not measurements.

## Build the upright body and support

Use a short upright oval for the torso and connect the head above it, adjusting the head-neck curve to the photograph. Compare the approximate widths of crown, shoulders, belly and rump before marking visible hind feet or body contact with soil. If the animal emerges from the hole, draw only visible parts rather than inventing complete legs below the entrance. At small size, the mound edge should explain whether the animal stands on it, in front of the hole or partly behind it.

## Place eye, ear and forelimbs

Determine the head direction first. In a side-view reference the muzzle should point consistently and the eye sit on the visible side of the head. Ears are small and close to the outline, not rabbit-length. Place visible forelimbs from the photograph; where paws are hidden against the chest, suggest only the visible contours rather than inventing complete fingers. Hide fur temporarily to check head direction. If the face is unclear, separate its value from the background instead of darkening every edge.

## Separate the tail root and black tip

If the tail is visible, locate its root at the rear of the body and draw a relatively short form. Put black only at the tip visible in the reference rather than coloring the whole tail black. A tail on the ground, raised or hidden depends on the chosen pose; do not combine every possible position. Separate the tail from the mound with a light value first, then darken the tip. If the tip disappears at small size, adjust nearby background value while retaining the tail proportions.

## Separate the subject from soil in tan values

Block the animal in tan or sandy brown while retaining structure in head, belly and legs. Give the mound a slightly different temperature or value so their silhouettes do not merge. Reserve a few light areas according to the reference; fur blending with earth does not mean both must use identical color values. Add restrained short fur strokes only near silhouette and value transitions. Check the body shape first rather than hiding contact errors under dense texture.

## Compare entrance occlusion

Make two thumbnails: in one the animal stands in front of the entrance; in the other the mound edge hides its lower body. Keep head, body and environment direction consistent. Compare the exposed opening, depth order and contact shadow, then continue with the version matching the photograph. These variants are compositional studies, not evidence that the animal is entering, leaving or calling. If entrance depth is unclear, reduce it to a dark opening with a continuous soil rim rather than painting the entire foreground black.

## Add prairie and check the focal point

Suggest the background with a few low grass clumps and distant horizontal bands; do not make every blade as dark and sharp as the animal. Hide grass and fur temporarily, leaving mound, entrance and animal shapes. Check that ear, muzzle, body and tail have readable contours. Restore details and check whether the dark entrance competes with the eye. If the tail is hidden, write “not visible” in the working notes; there is no need to invent a black tip.

## Save, export and recheck

Save the editable original, then use a share or export entry actually visible in the current version to save a separate image. Reopen it and inspect the four corners, top of the head, entrance edge and visible tail; return to the original to identify sketch, mound and subject. If output is missing content, check layer visibility, crop and location before overwriting the only original. Include the public and photographic sources, and mark device, export or reopening steps as pending if unperformed.

## Compare the mound’s share of the composition

Draw temporary bounding boxes around the mound and animal in the sketch, note their approximate widths and heights, and compare the visible ranges with the reference. If the mound is so large that it consumes the space above the head, resize or move the mound box first without changing the pose. If the reference crops one side, record only the visible range. Hide the boxes and inspect the subject’s placement again.
