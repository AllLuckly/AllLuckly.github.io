A beetle’s shell is conspicuous, but connections among thorax, abdomen and legs often determine whether it reads coherently. Draw an invented beetle along one axis before paired legs and antennae. Colours are illustrative rather than species identification, and manually organised values create the hard surface without a special material preset.

## Set head, thorax and abdomen scales

Use an approximately 1500×1500 px square with a tilted body, small head, broader thorax and largest wing-case mass. Separate far legs, shell, near legs and lights. Preserve one axis without requiring mirroring. Correct a string-of-beads appearance through differing widths and connected masses before the central seam.

## Let both wing cases share one volume

Model one oval abdomen before a restrained seam dividing the cases. Taper it toward the tail rather than creating a large crack. Test shared volume with the seam hidden. Soften a bean-pair split or clarify a local seam when merged, avoiding complete black frames around independent halves.

## Use overlap between thorax and head

Let the thorax partly overlap the head’s rear and connect over the wing-case roots, interrupting hidden edges. Establish this in flat colour before shadows. A turned head needs related antenna direction. Repair a sticker-like head through attachment, or reduce an oversized shield-like thorax’s wall and shadow weight.

## Place legs from paired origins

Use three simplified paired legs with organised body-side origins rather than random shell-edge attachments. Establish a few bent segments and near/far occlusion before tiny ends. Vary joint angles. Inspect roots with shell hidden if necessary; simplification may omit anatomical detail, but visible connections should not contradict one another.

## Turn shell reflections with the surface

Under upper-left light, curve a broad lighter reflection over the shell and retain deeper middle values lower right. Verify in grey before restrained green or brown. Reduce large pure-white/pure-black areas on a metal-badge result, or restore a selected clear jump when cloth-like. No special shine brush or universal metallic insect colour is assumed.

## Use antennae and eyes as directional cues

Use modest side-eye darks and antennae rooted in the head, establishing curves before sparse segmentation. Vary their directions without moving the origins. Keep far details quieter and judge at normal size; enlarged microscopic segmentation should not overwhelm the shell or turn the eyes into oversized highlighted beads.

## Limit pits and engraved-looking marks

Choose either a small patch of pits or fine lengthwise marks, compressed with the surface and weaker than seam and reflection. Smooth areas may remain untextured. Reduce spot contrast and count when they resemble stains. These patterns are deliberate illustration choices, not verified identifying features of a species.

## Separate leg contacts from the body shadow

Use a soft body shadow and small deeper foot contacts rather than equal black strips beneath every leg. Keep the ground clear of distracting grass. Repair floating through explicit contacts or restore legs swallowed by shadow. Review body masses small and leg origins normally; shadow should not conceal faulty attachments.

## Check the shell without bright reflections

Hide brightest accents to test head, thorax and cases, restoring them only as surface support. Repair middle values when volume depends entirely on white spots. A grey-leg copy can isolate pose. Record one useful attachment dark and one dispensable texture mark instead of assuming brighter and finer automatically means more complete.

## Keep the palette and turn the body

Keep silhouette, unaccented and finished versions with separate legs. Next, rebuild a more oblique view, changing case widths, thorax overlap and visible legs under the same light. Compressing the image alone will not update attachments. Record a newly occluded leg and retain the distinction between fictional drawing and scientific species identification.
