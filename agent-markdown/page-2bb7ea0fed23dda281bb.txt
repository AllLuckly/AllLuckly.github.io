**To lighten the contents of an already painted layer, consider layer opacity before repainting it.** Aquarell supports layer opacity and blending modes. Here, opacity is the main variable, so you can understand what causes each visible change.

The exercise aims for three comparable effects: a sketch that recedes, shadows of moderate strength, and highlights that do not look intrusive. Use an existing layered small drawing, or create a 1200 × 1200 pixel canvas and draw a simple round object.

## 1. Confirm that you are adjusting the entire layer

Open the Layers panel, select the layer to adjust, and check its thumbnail and selected state. Temporarily hide it to confirm that the intended content disappears, then show it again. For example, if a sketch and leaf veins share a layer, both will fade together. That happens because the content shares a layer, not because the app has failed to distinguish the lines.

Note the original opacity before changing it. For practice, keep the normal blending mode instead of changing the mode at the same time. Different compositing methods affect color relationships; changing two settings at once makes it harder to tell which one solved the problem.

## 2. Keep the sketch's structure without making it dominate

Lower the sketch layer from 100% to 60% and check whether the outer contour is still clear, then try 35%. Each time, zoom out enough to see the whole canvas rather than staring at a single enlarged corner.

If the sketch serves as final line art, too little opacity can make the subject lose its boundaries. If it is only a construction guide, a lighter setting may be more appropriate. No single value suits every artwork. You could note “original 100%, comparison 60%, provisional final 35%.” This records your decision process rather than treating the result as an app preset.

Lowering opacity does not automatically make a rough outline accurate. Structural problems still require changes to the sketch itself. Opacity controls visual weight; it cannot replace corrections to the form.

## 3. Handle the shadow layer separately

Create a shadow layer above the base color and paint the shadows with a darker, slightly cooler color. Establish a reasonably clear area first, then use layer opacity to control its overall strength. Start at 50% and compare it with 25% and 75%, watching whether the object retains its basic sense of volume.

If the shadow layer contains overlapping strokes, reducing the whole layer's strength will usually retain the differences created by those overlaps. For an even, broad shadow, pay attention to how you apply strokes and how many times you cover the area. Lowering opacity at the end will not smooth away every seam.

Also check whether the shadows spill onto the background. Lowering the whole layer fades those spills too, but does not change their boundaries. If the outline needs cleaning, confirm the active layer and fix the edges rather than lowering opacity until they disappear.

## 4. Control small bright accents with a highlight layer

Paint a few highlights on a new detail layer, such as a short bright edge along a cup's rim or a reflection on a leaf. Reduce their number before adjusting their strength. Too many bright marks can make the object look covered in white spots, and reducing opacity may still fail to restore a clear direction of light.

Try lowering the highlight layer to 70%, then decide whether it needs 40%. If the base color is already very light, the highlights may be hard to see. Adjust the relationship between the base and shadows instead of continually adding white. When zoomed out, highlights should help describe the shape rather than become a cluster of noise that attracts attention first.

## 5. Compare brush opacity with layer opacity

Paint two color patches in a blank test area. On one, make three passes with a low-opacity brush. On the other, paint with a more opaque brush, then lower the opacity of the whole layer. They may look similar, but their intersections, textures, and edges may differ.

Remember this distinction when choosing a workflow: use brush opacity when you want every overlapping stroke to contribute to color buildup; use layer opacity when you want existing content to recede as a whole. You can combine both in finished work, but learning to control them separately first makes troubleshooting easier.

## Final checks and returning to the original state

Check the sketch, shadows, and highlights in turn at the final display size to see whether each serves a purpose. Temporarily hide a layer: if the image becomes clearer, that layer may be too strong or unnecessary. If hiding it removes volume, show it again and make a small adjustment.

Record the values for the three layers and keep the native artwork. After exporting an image, check the overall strength of the colors again, remembering that screen brightness and viewing conditions affect your judgment. This tutorial does not provide color calibration or promise identical appearance across devices, and no device display errors have been measured.

## Use side-by-side comparisons to avoid drifting away from the goal

After adjusting for a while, your eyes gradually adapt to the new strength. Save a preview, change the opacity once more, and compare the two at the same size against the same background. Address one question at a time, such as whether the shadows are too heavy. Avoid changing composition and color simultaneously, so you can tell which version solves the problem.

## Questions and limitations

### Why does adjusting one layer make the whole picture lighter?

Check whether most of the artwork is on this layer or whether it contains a color block covering the entire canvas. Layer adjustments affect the actual contents; they do not recognize what you intended to be “just the shadow.”

### Does setting opacity to zero delete the content?

Opacity is a compositing display setting, not pixel deletion. Still, confirm which operation you selected so that you do not accidentally clear or delete the layer. Restore the original value, check that the content is visible, and keep the native file.

### Do I need to change the blending mode as well?

No. Find a suitable strength in the current mode first, then try blending modes for a specific purpose. No mode is guaranteed to produce the same effect with every color combination, and blindly applying a fixed combination is not recommended.

### How can I avoid forgetting the original value as I make adjustments?

Record the value or save a separate original before your first adjustment. When using Undo, watch for painting operations made between adjustments: repeated undo operations may also revert other changes, so do not infer the state from the number of taps alone.
