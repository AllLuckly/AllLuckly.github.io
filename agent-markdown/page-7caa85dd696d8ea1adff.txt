Giraffe patches should not be random puzzle pieces on flat paper: their visible shapes change across the front, sides and turns of the long neck. Draw an invented giraffe bust, establishing a tilted neck and head before using a few irregular brown patches to describe the surface. This is not species identification by markings, nor an attempt to copy every patch in a photograph. Aquarell provides drawing and layer controls; patch positions, overlaps and apparent sizes are arranged manually in response to the volume.

## Establish the pose without patches

Use an approximately 1500×1800 px portrait canvas with a pale blue-grey background. Sketch the neck rising from the lower left toward the upper right, broader at its base and narrowing toward the head instead of becoming a parallel-sided tube. Block in a slightly elongated head with the muzzle pointing right, locating ears and short horns with simple shapes. Separate sketch, base colour, patches and details, beginning with pale yellow-brown. Reduce the silhouette and distinguish neck, jaw and muzzle. If the head seems plugged onto a pipe, reshape the rear-head-to-neck turn instead of darkening a seam with patches. The pose is the image’s framework; markings cannot replace it.

## Give the neck one continuous value form

Light the left/front of the neck from the upper left, gradually turning toward grey yellow-brown on the right to establish one long volume with a slight planar turn. Do not give every neck segment a spherical highlight or fill it with black patches before deciding its shadow side. Hide the sketch and check that the base colour still has direction. If it resembles a flat strip, strengthen the gradual darkening on one side instead of blackening both outlines. Broad lighting must continue through areas that will later contain patches. Those are surface colours, not separate light sources. Shadow may concentrate near the neck base without abruptly becoming a black collar.

## Place a few large patches with pale channels

Begin with five to seven relatively large irregular patches in mid-brown. Make the front-facing ones broader, with slightly angular boundaries but without repeating the same hexagon. Leave pale-yellow channels between them, varying their width without merging the patches into one brown wall. Work on the visible neck front first, before filling the head and ears. Reduce the view and check whether the large shapes support the neck direction. If they resemble decorative tiles, break overly regular horizontal rows while retaining one shared surface. The pale channels are part of the pattern too; every empty space need not receive another small patch.

## Compress patches as they turn to the side

Narrow the visible width of patches near the right outline, aligning them with the tilted neck instead of tiling identical sizes all the way to the edge. For one patch crossing the turn, sketch its broad continuation over the surface and retain only the currently visible part; no hidden patch is needed beyond the silhouette. A patch may be cut off at the contour rather than squeezed inward to display its entire shape. Hide shading and check that the distribution still compresses from front to side. If the edge looks like a separate row of little dots, reconnect those shapes to the larger patches they continue instead of shrinking every patch mechanically.

## Light the patches as surface colour

Restore broad shading, warming lit brown patches slightly and cooling and darkening shadowed ones. Avoid making the pale base on the shadow side brighter than all the lit-side patches. Compare patch-to-base relationships within regions, then assess the whole neck volume. Keep many patch edges clear and a few turning edges softer without thick outlines or individual cast shadows: markings are not tiles attached to the skin. If they look like raised collage pieces, remove the repeated shadow beneath every patch and retain only the broad form shading. No measured biological colour is required, but one stable light direction and material should keep the neck from fragmenting into separate objects.

## Use head details to support orientation

Place the eye on the visible side using a small dark mark and restrained surrounding shadow, rather than two large symmetrical eyes that force the turned head into a frontal view. Set one ear ahead of the other, describing the near ear more clearly and the far ear with less detail. Connect the short horns to the head instead of floating them as decorations. Separate upper and lower muzzle with one small shadow plane rather than abundant hair. Hide patches and confirm the head direction remains clear. If muzzle and eye imply different orientations, correct their positions in the block sketch rather than repeatedly enlarging features for cuteness and upsetting the head/neck proportion. Use only a few smaller head markings, not copies of large neck patches.

## Keep the mane subordinate to the neck contour

Suggest a short directional mane along the back of the neck in groups following its curve, not as equally long outward-pointing needles. A few tips may interrupt the silhouette, but the neck form must continue underneath. Hide the hair once: if the neck breaks apart, the fuzzy edge has concealed a structural gap. Use fewer pale hairs close to the background colour, allowing brown patches and pale channels to carry the main rhythm. If the animal resembles a plush toy, reduce contour fuzz while preserving clear head/neck turns. If it looks like a wooden carving, gently soften a few selected edges instead of blurring every area.

## Test surface attachment with a counterexample

Save a small comparison copy and deliberately make side patches as wide as front ones. Observe whether the neck flattens, then check the original’s compression again. Change only the patches, retaining contour and lighting so the source of the difference is clear. After PNG export, inspect whether edge patches have merged into a black outline; reduce contrast or leave a little pale channel where necessary. Keep the unmarked base and finished image. In a later study, change only the head/neck tilt and redistribute the patches instead of applying the old pattern wholesale. Record a precise issue, such as an overly wide third patch on the right, to keep surface pattern serving volume.
