Two pH units from 3 to 5 do not mean a twofold hydronium difference. Read specified-concentration cards and a volume control in ManYue, checking logarithms, units and neutrality. These are ideal reading inputs, without testing/mixing liquids or making health/hazard judgments.

## Declare dilute aqueous concentration approximation

Use two ideal dilute aqueous solutions at 25°C: A has \[H3O+\]=1×10⁻³mol/L, B has 1×10⁻⁵mol/L. Put concentrations on P1, volumes on P2 and temperature/errors on P3; export PDF and retain source work. Check exponent signs and bookmark P1. Approximate pH as −log10(\[H3O+\]/1mol/L). The rigorous definition uses activity; concentration is not exact activity for all real solutions, particularly high ionic strength. State the approximation explicitly.

## A lower pH corresponds to higher hydronium concentration

A gives −log10(10⁻³)=3, B gives 5. The concentration ratio 10⁻³/10⁻⁵=100 makes lower-pH A higher in hydronium. Two units mean 10², not 2mol/L or “five portions of acid.” Correspondence arrows indicate a mathematical conversion. Uncalibrated liquid colour does not determine exact concentration or a chemical-strip reading from a comic panel.

## Invert the formula and test midpoint assumptions

Use \[H3O+\]=10^(−pH)mol/L to restore 10⁻³ and 10⁻⁵. A pH 4 control gives 10⁻⁴, the geometric midpoint, not the arithmetic concentration mean. The latter is 0.000505mol/L with pH≈3.2967, not 4. This is a mathematical concentration average, not a claim about mixing arbitrary acids; volumes, equilibria and reactions need further conditions. Preserve original, inverse and control rather than alter exponents to fit a linear-looking scale.

```text
ideal dilute concentration approximation; 25 degC
A: hydronium=1e-3 mol/L -> pH=3
B: hydronium=1e-5 mol/L -> pH=5
A/B concentration=100; pH difference=2
A volume=0.001 L: hydronium amount=1e-6 mol
B volume=1 L: hydronium amount=1e-5 mol
neutral criterion: [H3O+]=[OH-], not universal pH=7
```

## Use volume before comparing total amount

P2 gives A volume 1mL=0.001L and B volume 1L. Concentration×volume gives hydronium amounts 1×10⁻⁶mol and 1×10⁻⁵mol. B has 100-times lower concentration but 1000-times larger volume, hence 10-times greater amount. This counts hydronium in the specified state, not every potentially donated acidic proton or titratable total acidity. pH contains no volume information and cannot alone rank sample totals.

Misreading A’s 1mL as 1L gives 10⁻³mol,1000 times its correct 10⁻⁶, and reverses the amount ranking against B. Restore 0.001L only; A’s pH remains 3, not 6. Correcting volume/amount does not change the given per-litre concentration. Retain both concentration and volume units and explain which input was repaired.

## Compare both ions and temperature before calling neutrality

At 25°C let Kw=1×10⁻¹⁴. Neutrality gives \[H3O+\]=\[OH−\]=1×10⁻⁷mol/L and pH 7. A (pH 3) and B (pH 5) are acidic here because hydronium exceeds hydroxide, not because below 7 is universal at every temperature. Kw gives hydroxide 10⁻¹¹ for A and 10⁻⁹mol/L for B; each product is 10⁻¹⁴. Keep units and temperature rather than turn an isolated seven into an unconditional neutrality definition.

At 25°C, hydroxide 10⁻¹¹ gives pOH 11 for A, and 10⁻⁹ gives 9 for B. Adding pH 3/5 gives 14; neither multiplying pH×pOH nor adding concentrations expresses Kw. A has 100-times greater hydronium but 100-times smaller hydroxide under common temperature/Kw, not an unconditional comparison between different temperatures.

## A 6.31 temperature control can still be neutral

P3 uses the textbook’s 80°C pure-water condition: both hydronium and hydroxide≈4.9×10⁻⁷mol/L, giving pH≈pOH≈6.31 while remaining neutral. Their sum≈12.62, not the 25°C value 14. This illustrates temperature-dependent Kw, not heating safety or an author measurement. Check temperature and approximation before comparison; 6.31 below 7 does not make this pure water acidic or require replacing its value by 7.

## Diagnose logarithm base, volume and neutrality scope

A twofold claim for two pH units misses base 10; a smaller B amount misses volume; acidic labelling at 80°C misses equal ions. Enlarge exponents, H3O+, OH− and distinguish a formula subscript 3 from concentration 3. Repair one column and invert the logarithm or multiply via Kw. Colour photographs or unspecified “acidity” supply no exact pH/hazard label. Unknown approximation, temperature and volume remain unknown instead of expanding one calculation into unsupported conclusions.

pH alone does not identify an acid’s intrinsic strength. Dissociation equilibrium and analytical concentration affect solution pH; lower pH alone does not prove stronger acid species without composition/concentration. This task compares specified hydronium states, without identifying solutes, Ka or formulations. Acidity, high concentration and hazard are not interchangeable; record unsupported claims separately.
