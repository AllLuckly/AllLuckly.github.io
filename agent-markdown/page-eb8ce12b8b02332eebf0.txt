Use public animal information and a side-view reference to practice an otter silhouette, tapering tail and ground contact.

## Choose a reference and define the view

The US National Park Service describes the river otter as having a long streamlined body, a small head and a tail that tapers toward its end. Choose a permitted side-view North American river otter reference on a bank with body and tail root visible. Treat parts hidden by water, plants or the body as unknown. Record species, image source and usage rights; do not infer real behavior from the illustration or approach or disturb wildlife.

## Compose and save a working original

Create an approximately 1800 by 1400 pixel landscape canvas using the current Aquarell interface and save an editable original first; dimensions are only exercise choices. Place the bank low in the picture, with the head facing either direction and enough space beyond the tail. Separate sketch, bank and water, body and details with available layers; use identifiable functions if labels differ on the device. Lightly draw a ground line and a body-direction line.

## Build one continuous body shape

Block in the torso as a long rounded form and attach a smaller head at the front. Observe the transition from neck to back rather than sticking a circular head onto an ellipse. The belly may touch the bank locally or leave a gap, according to the chosen photograph. Temporarily hide other details and shrink the view: if head and torso read as two separate circles, revise the connection; if the whole animal looks like a thick stick, adjust the back and belly curves.

## Connect the tail base to the torso

Start the tail with a broad root at the rear of the body and narrow it gradually toward the tip as shown in the reference. Draw a directional center line before its two boundaries rather than beginning with a thin stroke. The tail may curve, be partly hidden in grass or reach water, but the visible portion should connect to the body. When comparing with the preceding beaver study, compare only contours supported by each source and reference; do not interchange the tails.

## Place short legs and visible contact

Mark the underside first, then locate where near and far legs emerge according to the reference. Use short simplified shapes for direction and place visible feet on the bank; do not invent four fully visible legs when the body hides them. Add small dark contact shadows to connect feet and ground. Hide those shadows temporarily to inspect the foot positions; if a sole floats above the bank, correct its contour instead of concealing the error with shadow.

## Keep the head direction clear

Use a few contours for muzzle and eye, keeping the eye on the head rather than the neck. Let detail size follow the reference and canvas; first separate the face from its background with a small value shape, then decide whether a few short marks should suggest whiskers. At small scale check that eye and muzzle remain visible against the dark background. If the face looks frontal while the body is in profile, revise the muzzle direction in the sketch instead of adding more facial marks.

## Arrange values and suggest fur

Block the body in a middle brown, using a darker brown to distinguish selected belly, back-shadow or tail areas, and reserve light areas based on the reference. Suggest watercolor with restrained strokes and varied edges; do not assume an automatic fur or wet-paint feature. Check that the large shapes retain the body structure before adding a few short marks following volume near the contour. Dense outlines can obscure the streamlined silhouette.

## Define bank and water

Suggest the bank in muted brown-green and water in blue-gray. Decide whether the animal is on land or partly in water before arranging occlusion. Show contact where a foot rests on the bank, and do not carry water highlights across exposed land portions if the tail enters water. Simplify the background to a few stones or grass shapes, avoiding the same values and contours as the body. Hide the background and check that body and tail read without depending on the grass.

## Compare tail shapes with two thumbnails

Duplicate the sketch or make two small studies: one with a tail that is too thin and one tapering gradually from its root. Keep head, body and ground line the same. Compare with the reference and choose the closer study, noting tail-root width and tail-tip direction; if the photograph hides the tip, compare only what is visible. Save a record before deleting or hiding the unused study, so you can review whether a mistake came from shape or occlusion. This is a drawing exercise, not an animal measurement.

## Record observations rather than guessing

Beside the sketch, note the body direction, the tail-root connection and which legs are obscured. Distinguish “not visible” from “unclear in the drawing”: return to the reference for the first, and adjust value or contour for the second. If consulting another photograph, label its different viewpoint instead of mechanically joining two views into one pose.

## Save and recheck the export

Save the editable original, then use a share or export entry actually visible in the current version to save an image separately. Reopen it to inspect the head, tail tip, corner crop and foot contact, and return to identify sketch, body and background layers in the original. If the tail is missing, check visibility and crop first; if layers appear merged, check whether you opened only the image copy. Include the reference source and mark unperformed device, export and reopening steps as pending.

## Compare body and tail with proportion bars

Draw a simple proportion bar beside the reference and the sketch, marking the front of the head, the body-to-tail-root junction and the tail tip. Align the bars by total length and compare the tail-root position. If the tail is hidden in the reference, mark its end unknown rather than completing it from another photograph. The bars apply only to this view; save a draft for comparison after adjusting.
